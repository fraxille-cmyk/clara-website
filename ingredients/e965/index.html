Maltitols (E965)

E965SweetenerNeutral · benign

Diets

✕ Not low-FODMAP
  • Not low-FODMAP — maltitols is a polyol.

Maltitols are sugar-free sweeteners made from starch that taste very close to sugar but can cause mild digestive discomfort if eaten in large amounts.

What it is

Maltitols belong to a family of ingredients known as sugar alcohols or polyols. Despite the name, they contain no alcohol in the everyday sense. They are carbohydrates that occur naturally in small amounts in certain fruits and vegetables, though the version on your food label is produced at scale. They look and taste remarkably like regular table sugar, providing nearly the same level of sweetness but with fewer calories, and they do not behave the same way as sugar once inside the body.

How it's made

The journey of this ingredient begins with natural starches, typically obtained from corn, wheat, or tapioca. Enzymes are first used to break this starch down into maltose, which is a type of sugar. This maltose syrup then undergoes a chemical process called hydrogenation, where hydrogen is added under high pressure in the presence of a metal catalyst. This reaction transforms the sugar into a sugar alcohol, which is then purified, filtered, and either crystallised into a white powder or kept as a thick, clear syrup.

Why manufacturers use it

Food makers love maltitols because they behave more like real sugar than almost any other sweetener. They provide bulk, texture, and a pleasant mouthfeel to sugar-free foods, and they do not have the bitter aftertaste associated with some artificial sweeteners. They also do not brown when heated, which helps maintain a consistent appearance in baked goods. Commercially, they are highly stable, extend the shelf life of products by keeping them moist, and do not feed the mouth bacteria that damage tooth enamel.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this NEUTRAL, a benign additive. It is approved for use in foods, but it is well known for causing mild digestive issues like bloating, gas, and a laxative effect when consumed in large quantities. This happens because our bodies only partially absorb maltitols, leaving the rest to ferment in the lower gut. While it is not considered a high-concern ingredient, people with sensitive digestive systems often choose to limit their intake of foods containing large amounts of sugar alcohols.

What the science says

Major food safety authorities have thoroughly evaluated maltitols and confirmed their safety. Because they are only partially digested, they cause a much lower rise in blood glucose compared to regular sugar, which has been confirmed by numerous clinical evaluations. JECFA and EFSA have both reviewed the safety data and concluded that no daily limit is established for maltitols, meaning they can be used as needed in foods, though European regulations require a warning label on products containing more than ten percent polyols due to the laxative effect.

Where you'll find it

You will find this ingredient in a wide variety of sugar-free and reduced-calorie products, including chocolates, chewing gums, hard candies, baked goods, ice creams, and throat lozenges. On the ingredient list, it might be written as maltitol, maltitol syrup, maltitols, or simply by its European code, E965. It is also frequently blended with other sweeteners to balance out the overall flavour profile of diet foods.
