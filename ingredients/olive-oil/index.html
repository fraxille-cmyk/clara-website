Olive oil

Processed ingredientNeutral

Diets

✓ Suits every diet Clara checks — as an ingredient

Olive oil is a natural, minimally processed plant fat pressed from olives, used as a cooking medium and flavour enhancer in savoury foods.

What it is

Olive oil is a liquid fat obtained by pressing whole olives, the fruit of the olive tree. It is a traditional ingredient of the Mediterranean region, rich in monounsaturated fatty acids, particularly oleic acid. Unlike highly refined seed oils, olive oil retains much of its natural character, including its distinctive green-gold colour, aroma, and natural antioxidants. It is widely appreciated both as a cooking medium and as a finishing oil to add depth and richness to savoury dishes.

How it's made

The journey of olive oil begins with harvesting ripe olives from the trees. These olives are washed and crushed into a thick paste using large rollers or stone mills. This paste is then slowly mixed, a process called malaxation, which allows the tiny oil droplets to gather into larger drops. The mixture is pressed or spun in a high-speed centrifuge to separate the liquid oil and water from the solid fruit pulp. Finally, the water is removed, leaving behind pure olive oil ready for bottling.

Why manufacturers use it

Food manufacturers choose olive oil to bring a premium quality and a rich, authentic flavour to their products. It serves as an excellent natural preservative, keeping air away from ingredients like sun-dried tomatoes or grilled vegetables when they are packed in jars. Olive oil also provides a smooth texture and satisfying mouthfeel in pestos, dressings, and baked goods. Its stability under moderate heat makes it a reliable choice for prepared meals, beating cheaper seed oils that lack its characteristic taste profile.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this NEUTRAL. As a whole food ingredient rather than an additive, it is not classified under standard additive tiers. Olive oil is a source of dietary fats that the body uses for energy and cellular structure. It is well tolerated by the human digestive system and has been consumed as a dietary staple for thousands of years. No daily intake limit is established for olive oil, as it is treated as a standard food ingredient rather than a chemical substance requiring strict regulatory restriction.

What the science says

The scientific consensus on olive oil focuses on its high content of monounsaturated fats and polyphenols. Major food authorities have evaluated its nutritional profile, noting that replacing saturated fats in the diet with unsaturated fats like those in olive oil helps maintain normal blood cholesterol levels. European regulators have also recognised the role of olive oil polyphenols in protecting blood lipids from oxidative stress. These evaluations are based on its consumption as a normal part of a balanced diet, with no safety concerns identified.

Where you'll find it

You will find olive oil in a wide variety of savoury products, including pasta sauces, pestos, salad dressings, marinades, and roasted vegetables packed in jars. It is also used in baked goods like focaccia and crackers. On a food label, it is simply listed by its plain name as olive oil, extra virgin olive oil, or refined olive oil, depending on the grade used. Because it is a natural food ingredient rather than a food additive, it does not have an E-number.
