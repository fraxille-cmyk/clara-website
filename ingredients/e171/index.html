Titanium dioxide (E171)

E171AdditiveAvoid · high concern

Titanium dioxide is a white food colour banned in the European Union because regulators could not rule out damage to genetic material from nanoparticles.

What it is

Titanium dioxide is an inorganic substance that occurs naturally as a mineral. It is a brilliant white powder that is highly effective at scattering light, which makes it one of the most powerful whitening agents known. In the food industry, it has long been used to give a bright, clean look to otherwise dull ingredients, or to provide an opaque background that makes other colours pop. Beyond food, you might recognise it as the active ingredient that makes mineral sunscreens look white on your skin.

How it's made

The production of titanium dioxide begins with natural titanium-bearing ores, such as ilmenite or rutile. Manufacturers extract and purify the metal oxide using either a sulphate process, which treats the ore with sulphuric acid, or a chloride process, which uses chlorine gas at high temperatures. The resulting material is washed, dried, and milled into an extremely fine powder. A significant portion of this finished powder consists of nanoparticles, which are tiny particles measuring less than one hundred nanometres in size.

Why manufacturers use it

Food manufacturers use this additive primarily for its unmatched ability to impart a bright white colour and a smooth, opaque finish. It is incredibly stable under heat, light, and varying acidity levels, meaning it does not fade during processing or shelf life. This stability makes it far more reliable and cheaper than natural alternatives like calcium carbonate or starch. It also helps to prevent light from degrading sensitive ingredients in packaged foods, preserving the visual appeal of the product over many months.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this AVOID, a high-concern additive. European regulators banned its use in food because they could not rule out genotoxicity, which is the ability of a substance to damage the genetic material inside cells. This concern is specifically linked to the nanoparticle fraction of the powder, which can accumulate in the body over time. While the European Union has revoked its approval, other regulators, including the FDA, still permit its use, arguing that the evidence of harm under normal dietary exposure is insufficient.

What the science says

The scientific debate focuses on how the body processes the tiniest nanoparticles of this mineral. Because these particles are so small, they can penetrate biological barriers and build up in organs. EFSA evaluated the evidence and concluded that a limit for daily intake could not be established due to the unresolved concerns about genetic damage. Conversely, the FDA maintains that the substance is acceptable for food use at levels not exceeding one percent of the food by weight, asserting that absorption through the digestive tract is extremely low.

Where you'll find it

You will find this additive in highly processed sweets, chewing gum, cake decorations, white sauces, and powdered coffee creamers. On ingredient labels, it is typically declared by its plain name, titanium dioxide, or by its European code, E171. Since the European ban, many manufacturers have reformulated their products for the European market, but the ingredient remains common in products sold in other regions, particularly in confectionery and decorative baking supplies.
