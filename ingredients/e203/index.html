Calcium sorbate (E203)

E203AdditiveAvoid · high concern

Calcium sorbate is a synthetic preservative withdrawn in the EU because there was not enough data to prove it does not damage genetic material.

What it is

Calcium sorbate is a synthetic preservative designed to keep food fresh. It is the calcium salt of sorbic acid, a compound originally found in the berries of the rowan tree, though the version used in food is entirely man-made. Chemically, it is a fine white powder that dissolves easily in water. It works by releasing sorbic acid into food, which helps to keep the product stable and free from spoilage over time. While it is similar to other sorbates, its unique calcium structure gives it different solubility properties.

How it's made

To make this preservative, manufacturers start with synthetic sorbic acid, which is produced through a chemical reaction involving crotonaldehyde and ketene. This acid is then neutralised using calcium hydroxide, commonly known as slaked lime. The resulting chemical reaction produces calcium sorbate, which is then filtered, dried, and ground into a fine powder. Because the entire process relies on industrial chemical synthesis, the final ingredient is classified as synthetic and does not come from natural plant sources.

Why manufacturers use it

Manufacturers use this ingredient primarily to prevent the growth of moulds, yeasts, and certain bacteria in food. It is highly valued because it does not alter the flavour, colour, or texture of the food. It is also cheaper and more stable than many natural alternatives, allowing products to sit on supermarket shelves for much longer without spoiling. By stopping microscopic organisms from multiplying, it prevents early spoilage and extends the commercial life of packaged goods.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this AVOID, a high-concern additive. This rating is because the European Union withdrew its approval for the ingredient after manufacturers failed to provide enough scientific evidence to prove it does not damage genetic material. While the FDA still permits its use, EFSA withdrew the acceptable daily intake in 2015 due to these unresolved questions. No limit is established in Europe because the additive is no longer allowed there, and Clara advises avoiding it where it remains on shelves elsewhere.

What the science says

European regulators re-evaluated this preservative and found a significant gap in the scientific evidence. EFSA concluded that the available data was insufficient to exclude genotoxicity, which is the ability of a substance to damage the genetic information within cells. Because the food industry did not perform the necessary safety tests to fill this gap, regulators could not confirm its safety. Consequently, the European Commission officially removed it from the approved list of food additives, although the FDA in the United States still maintains its status as generally recognised as safe.

Where you'll find it

You might still find this ingredient in imported baked goods, cheeses, spreads, and processed fruit products. On a label, it is typically listed as calcium sorbate or by its European code, E 203. In countries where it is still permitted, it is often grouped with other preservatives in the ingredients list. Because of the European ban, many global manufacturers have replaced it with potassium sorbate, but it remains important to check labels on imported foods.
