Gold (E175)

E175AdditiveNeutral · limited concern

Diets

✓ Suits every diet Clara checks — as an ingredient

Gold is a natural precious metal used as a decorative surface colour, rated NEUTRAL by Clara because it is rarely consumed and has no established daily limit.

What it is

Gold is the very same precious metal used to make jewellery and coins. In the food world, it is classified as a natural mineral colour. When beaten into incredibly thin sheets or ground into tiny flakes, it becomes edible. It has no taste, no texture that you can feel on your tongue, and no nutritional value. It is used purely for its brilliant, metallic yellow shine, bringing a touch of luxury to celebratory foods rather than acting as a traditional ingredient.

How it's made

To make edible gold, raw metallic gold is melted down and refined to an extremely high level of purity, typically over ninety-two per cent, to ensure it is free from other metals like copper or cadmium. The purified gold is then cast into small bars. These bars are rolled and beaten repeatedly, historically by hand and now by specialised machinery, until they are hammered into sheets that are thousands of times thinner than a human hair. These delicate sheets are either left whole or crumbled into flakes and dust.

Why manufacturers use it

Manufacturers use gold solely for its visual impact. It provides a genuine, reflective metallic lustre that artificial colours cannot replicate. Because it is highly stable, it does not react with moisture, light, or oxygen, meaning it will not tarnish, fade, or change colour over time. It is an expensive ingredient, so it is reserved for premium products where the goal is to create an eye-catching, luxurious presentation that justifies a higher price point.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this NEUTRAL, a limited-concern additive. Because it is chemically inert, gold passes through the human digestive system completely unchanged without being absorbed into the body. However, EFSA has not established an acceptable daily intake because the available toxicological data are insufficient to set a specific limit. While there is no evidence of harm from the tiny amounts used in food, its use is restricted to specific decorative applications rather than general consumption.

What the science says

European and American regulators have evaluated gold and determined that it is acceptable for highly specific, limited uses. In its re-evaluation, EFSA noted that because gold is not absorbed by the gut, it does not enter the bloodstream or accumulate in organs. No toxicological concern has been identified at the levels currently consumed, though scientists agree that more data would be needed to establish a formal daily safety limit. It remains authorised because its actual intake from decorated foods is extremely low.

Where you'll find it

You will find gold on labels listed as gold, gold leaf, or by its E-number, E175. Because of strict regulations and its high cost, its use is restricted to a few specific categories. It is typically found on the surface of high-end chocolates, as a delicate coating on luxury confectionery, or suspended as shimmering flakes in premium liqueurs. On US labels, it is declared as gold or gold colour additive under specific federal regulations.
