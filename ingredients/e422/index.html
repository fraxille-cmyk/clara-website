Glycerol (E422)

E422AdditiveNeutral · benign

Glycerol is a natural, sweet liquid used to keep foods moist and soft, with a long history of safe use in everyday groceries.

What it is

Glycerol, also widely known as glycerin or glycerine, is a simple, clear, and odourless liquid with a mildly sweet taste and a thick, syrupy texture. It belongs to the alcohol family of chemicals, though it does not behave like the alcohol in drinks. In nature, it forms the backbone of fats and oils, meaning it is already present in our bodies and in many of the natural foods we eat every day, such as cheese, butter, and vegetable oils. Because of its ability to attract and hold water, it is highly valued in both food and cosmetics.

How it's made

To make the glycerol used in food, manufacturers typically start with natural vegetable oils, such as coconut, palm, or rapeseed oil. These fats are split into their basic parts using high pressure and steam, a process called hydrolysis, which separates the fatty acids from the glycerol backbone. The resulting liquid is then carefully filtered, distilled, and purified to remove any remaining water and impurities. This produces a highly concentrated, food-grade vegetable glycerin that is identical to the glycerol naturally found in living cells.

Why manufacturers use it

Food manufacturers rely on glycerol primarily as a humectant, which is an ingredient that draws in moisture and keeps food from drying out. It helps baked goods, energy bars, and dried fruits stay soft and chewy on the shelf for much longer than they would otherwise. It also acts as a thickener and a solvent, helping to blend flavours and colours evenly throughout a product. Because it has a natural sweetness but does not raise blood sugar in the same way as table sugar, it is often used in low-sugar and low-carbohydrate foods.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this NEUTRAL, a benign additive. Glycerol has an exceptionally long safety record when consumed at the levels typically found in food. Because our bodies already process glycerol as a normal part of digesting dietary fats, it is metabolised easily and used as a source of energy. There is no evidence of adverse effects from the amounts used in everyday grocery products, and no daily intake limit has been established by food safety authorities because it is considered so low in risk.

What the science says

Major food safety authorities have thoroughly evaluated glycerol and confirmed its safety. The European Food Safety Authority re-evaluated it and concluded that there is no need to set a numerical acceptable daily intake, as it poses no safety concerns at typical dietary levels. The Joint FAO/WHO Expert Committee on Food Additives also classifies it as safe for use in food without a specified limit. Scientists have found that while consuming extremely large quantities might cause a mild laxative effect or a temporary headache, such amounts are far beyond what anyone could realistically eat from normal food products.

Where you'll find it

You will find this ingredient in a wide variety of foods, including soft baked goods, cake decorations, marshmallows, shredded coconut, energy bars, and sugar-free confectionery. On ingredient labels, it may be listed under its E-number, E422, or by its common names such as glycerin, glycerine, glycerol, or vegetable glycerin. It is also common in imported European foods where it might appear under its continental names like glicerina, glicerolo, or glyzerin.
