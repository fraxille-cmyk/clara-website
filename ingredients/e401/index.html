Sodium alginate (E401)

E401AdditiveNeutral · benign

A natural thickener and stabiliser extracted from brown seaweed, sodium alginate is a carrageenan-free alternative with a long history of safe use.

What it is

Sodium alginate is a natural substance derived from the cell walls of brown seaweed, which grows abundantly in cold water oceans. Chemically, it is the sodium salt of alginic acid, a complex carbohydrate that helps seaweeds remain flexible yet strong in turbulent waters. When dissolved in water, it behaves much like other plant-based soluble fibres, absorbing moisture to create a smooth, viscous gel. It is a popular alternative to other seaweed extracts like carrageenan, offering a clean way to alter the texture of foods without using animal-derived gelatines.

How it's made

The production of this ingredient begins with harvesting wild or cultivated brown seaweed. The seaweed is washed, chopped, and treated with an alkaline solution, typically sodium carbonate, to dissolve the alginic acid within the plant tissue. This mixture is filtered to remove the solid plant debris, leaving a clear liquid. Next, the liquid is treated with an acid or a calcium salt to precipitate the alginate out of the solution. Finally, this precipitate is converted back into a sodium salt, dried, and milled into a fine, cream-coloured powder that dissolves easily in water.

Why manufacturers use it

Food manufacturers value this ingredient for its exceptional ability to thicken liquids and stabilise emulsions. It prevents ingredients from separating, ensuring that salad dressings stay mixed and ice cream remains creamy without forming large ice crystals. One of its most unique properties is its ability to form a firm gel in the presence of calcium, even at room temperature. This makes it incredibly useful for creating structured foods, such as reconstituted fruit pieces, vegetarian casings, and the liquid-filled pearls used in molecular gastronomy and bubble tea.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this NEUTRAL, a benign additive. It has a long history of dietary use and is processed by the body much like any other soluble dietary fibre, passing through the digestive system largely intact. Because it is not absorbed into the bloodstream and shows no adverse effects even at high levels of consumption, regulators have determined that it poses no safety concerns. No limit is established for its daily intake, meaning it can be used in foods at the levels necessary to achieve the desired culinary effect.

What the science says

Scientific bodies have thoroughly evaluated this seaweed extract and confirmed its safety. EFSA re-evaluated the additive in 2017 and concluded that there was no need to establish a numerical acceptable daily intake, as it is of no toxicological concern. The FDA also affirms its safety, designating it as generally recognised as safe for direct food use. Because it is a carrageenan-free seaweed extract, it is often favoured by those who wish to avoid other types of seaweed-derived thickeners that have faced closer scrutiny in recent years.

Where you'll find it

You will find this ingredient in a wide variety of processed foods, including ice cream, puddings, pie fillings, salad dressings, and processed cheese. It is also used to create the gel coatings on some meats and desserts. On ingredient lists, it is typically declared by its plain name as sodium alginate, or by its European code, E401. On US labels it is listed as sodium alginate, where it is permitted in a variety of categories to stabilise, thicken, and firm food formulations.
