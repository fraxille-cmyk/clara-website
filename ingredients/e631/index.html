Disodium inosinate (E631)

Disodium inosinate is a savoury flavour enhancer that works alongside other ingredients to deepen the taste of processed foods.

What it is

Disodium inosinate is a sodium salt of inosinic acid, a compound found naturally in animal tissues like meat, poultry, and fish. It belongs to a family of substances called nucleotides, which are the building blocks of genetic material in all living things. In food, it acts as a powerful flavour enhancer, particularly for savoury and meaty tastes. It does not have much flavour on its own, but it works in harmony with other savoury compounds to make a food taste much richer and more satisfying.

How it's made

To produce this ingredient on a commercial scale, manufacturers typically start with natural starches from crops like tapioca or corn. These starches undergo a fermentation process using specific bacterial strains that naturally produce nucleotides. Once the fermentation is complete, the resulting inosinic acid is purified, neutralised with sodium to form a stable salt, and crystallised into a fine white powder that dissolves easily in liquid during food manufacturing. Alternatively, it can be extracted directly from dried fish or meat extracts.

Why manufacturers use it

Manufacturers use this ingredient because it is an incredibly efficient flavour enhancer, especially when paired with monosodium glutamate. When these two ingredients are combined, they create a synergistic effect that multiplies the savoury sensation far beyond what either could achieve alone. This allows food companies to use much less overall seasoning while still delivering a deep, satisfying taste. It is a highly cost-effective way to maintain a strong savoury profile in packaged foods that undergo high-heat processing, which can sometimes dull natural flavours.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this NEUTRAL, a benign additive. It is processed by the body in the same way as the natural nucleotides found in everyday foods like meat and mushrooms. The only notable consideration is that it metabolises into purines, which are compounds that some individuals need to limit to avoid joint discomfort. For the general population, the ingredient is processed without issue. No upper limit is established for daily intake because the amounts used in packaged foods are extremely small and do not pose a safety concern.

What the science says

Scientific bodies have evaluated this ingredient thoroughly and found no safety concerns at typical dietary levels. JECFA established that no numerical daily limit is necessary because the substance is a normal constituent of living cells and is easily metabolised by the human body. The European Food Safety Authority re-evaluated the additive and confirmed its safety in food applications. Because it breaks down into uric acid, regulators note that individuals who are sensitive to purines may wish to monitor their intake, but for the wider public, the scientific consensus is clear and stable.

Where you'll find it

You will find this ingredient in a wide variety of savoury processed foods, including instant noodles, potato crisps, canned soups, savoury sauces, gravy granules, and pre-packaged rice mixes. On food labels, it is commonly listed by its full name as disodium inosinate or simply as E631. It is frequently grouped next to monosodium glutamate or disodium guanylate on the ingredient list, as these substances are almost always used together to maximise their flavour-enhancing properties.
