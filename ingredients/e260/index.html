Acetic acid (E260)

E260AdditiveNeutral · benign

Acetic acid is the natural organic acid that gives vinegar its sharp taste and sour smell, used to preserve food and control acidity.

What it is

Acetic acid is a simple organic acid that you already know well, as it is the main component of household vinegar after water. It is a clear, colourless liquid with a sharp, pungent aroma and a distinctly sour taste. In nature, it is produced by certain bacteria during fermentation, which is how wine turns into vinegar. In the food industry, it serves as a reliable preservative and acidity regulator, helping to keep packaged foods fresh and giving them a pleasant, tangy bite.

How it's made

While the vinegar in your kitchen is made by fermenting alcohol, the pure acetic acid used as a food additive is typically produced through chemical synthesis. Manufacturers usually make it by reacting methanol with carbon monoxide in a highly controlled industrial process. This chemical route yields a highly concentrated, exceptionally pure acid. Once purified and diluted to the correct strength, it is identical in its chemical structure and behaviour to the natural acid found in fermented fruits and grains.

Why manufacturers use it

Manufacturers rely on this acid primarily to control the acidity of packaged foods, which helps prevent the growth of unwanted bacteria and moulds. By lowering the pH of a food product, it acts as an effective preservative that extends shelf life without the need for harsher chemicals. It is also highly valued for its flavouring properties, providing that familiar, sharp tang in pickles, sauces, and dressings. Because it is highly stable and inexpensive to produce, it is a preferred choice for maintaining consistent quality.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this NEUTRAL, a benign additive. This rating reflects its status as a natural acid with an incredibly long history of safe use in human diets. Because our bodies are fully equipped to metabolise it, regulators have determined that it poses no safety concerns when consumed in normal dietary amounts. No limit is established for its daily intake, meaning there is no specific upper boundary on how much can be safely consumed. It is processed by the body just like the acid in everyday vinegar.

What the science says

Major food safety authorities have thoroughly evaluated this substance and confirmed its safety. EFSA re-evaluated it as a food additive and concluded that it does not pose any safety concerns, deciding that establishing a numerical acceptable daily intake is unnecessary. The FDA also affirms its safety, designating it as generally recognised as safe for direct addition to food. Because it is a simple molecule that the body naturally produces and breaks down during normal metabolism, scientific consensus views it as entirely benign.

Where you'll find it

You will find this ingredient in a wide variety of savoury products, including pickles, salad dressings, mayonnaise, ketchups, brown sauces, and marinades. It is also used to adjust the acidity in canned vegetables, soups, and some baked goods. On ingredient lists, it is typically declared by its plain name as acetic acid, but you might also see it listed by its European code, E260, especially on products imported from or sold in the United Kingdom and Europe.
