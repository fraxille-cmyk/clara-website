Cane sugar

Processed ingredientNeutral

Diets

Cane sugar is a traditional sweetener made from sugar cane stalks, used to add sweetness and texture to foods.

What it is

Cane sugar is a familiar sweetener that comes from the sugar cane plant, a tall grass that thrives in warm, tropical climates. Chemically, it consists almost entirely of sucrose, which is a simple carbohydrate made of equal parts glucose and fructose. It is the very same white or golden crystal that has been used in kitchens for centuries, providing a direct source of energy that our bodies recognise and metabolise quickly. Unlike synthetic sweeteners, it is a natural agricultural product that has a long history in the human diet.

How it's made

The journey from field to pantry begins by crushing harvested sugar cane stalks to squeeze out their sweet juice. This juice is clarified with lime to remove impurities and then boiled down to evaporate the water, leaving behind a thick, dark syrup. As the syrup cools, sugar crystals begin to form. These crystals are spun in a centrifuge to separate them from the liquid molasses. Depending on how much molasses is left behind or added back, the result is raw, brown, or fully refined white sugar.

Why manufacturers use it

Food makers choose cane sugar because it does far more than just make things sweet. It acts as a natural preservative by binding water, which slows down the growth of spoilage organisms and keeps baked goods moist. It also provides bulk and structure, helping cakes rise and giving biscuits their characteristic crunch. When heated, it undergoes caramelisation, which develops rich brown colours and complex flavours that artificial alternatives simply cannot replicate. It is a highly reliable, low-cost ingredient that performs multiple roles at once.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this NEUTRAL. While it is not a chemical additive, consuming it in large quantities can lead to an imbalance in our daily energy intake. The body processes these simple carbohydrates very rapidly, which can cause quick rises in blood glucose levels. No regulatory upper limit is established for cane sugar as an ingredient, but health organisations generally advise keeping our intake of all free sugars to a sensible minimum to support overall physical well-being. It is best enjoyed as an occasional part of a balanced diet.

What the science says

Major food authorities have evaluated sugar consumption extensively over the decades. The FDA classifies sucrose as generally recognised as safe, noting its long history of common use in food. EFSA has conducted comprehensive dietary assessments on added sugars, concluding that while they are a useful source of energy, high intake is linked to metabolic changes and dental issues. Because it is a basic food rather than a novel additive, researchers focus on how much we consume overall rather than the safety of the substance itself.

Where you'll find it

You will find this ingredient in an incredibly wide array of supermarket items, from sweet biscuits, cakes, and fizzy drinks to savoury sauces, ready meals, and breakfast cereals. On packaging, it is usually declared plainly as cane sugar, organic cane sugar, cane juice, or simply sugar. Because it is a whole food ingredient rather than a food additive, it does not carry an E-number on European labels, making it easy to spot in the ingredient list.
