Wheat flour

Processed ingredientNeutral

Diets

✕ Not gluten-free✕ Not low-FODMAP
  • Not gluten-free — wheat flour comes from a gluten grain.
  • Not low-FODMAP — wheat flour is high in fructans.

Wheat flour is a staple ingredient made by grinding wheat grain, providing quick energy but fewer nutrients than its whole-grain counterpart.

What it is

Wheat flour is the powdery substance obtained by milling the kernels of the wheat plant. It is the fundamental building block of baking, composed mostly of starch and proteins like gluten. Unlike wholemeal options, refined wheat flour has the outer bran and the inner germ removed, leaving only the starchy endosperm. This gives it a fine texture and a pale appearance, making it highly versatile in the kitchen but less nutritionally dense than the original whole grain.

How it's made

The process begins with harvesting wheat grains, which are thoroughly cleaned to remove chaff and stones. These grains are then tempered with moisture to toughen the outer bran coat, making it easier to separate. Next, the tempered grains pass through a series of large metal rollers that crack them open. Sifting screens separate the fine white endosperm from the coarser bran and germ. The remaining endosperm is ground repeatedly into a smooth, consistent powder, which is sometimes aged or bleached to achieve a bright white colour.

Why manufacturers use it

Food producers rely on wheat flour because of its unique proteins, gliadin and glutenin, which form gluten when mixed with water. This gluten network gives dough its elasticity and strength, allowing it to rise and hold its shape. It creates the soft, chewy texture we expect in bread and the delicate crumb in cakes. Commercially, it is incredibly stable, inexpensive, and easy to store for long periods, making it the most cost-effective base for a vast array of packaged foods.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this NEUTRAL. Refined wheat flour provides carbohydrates but lacks the fibre and nutrients of whole grain flour. Because the milling process strips away the nutrient-rich bran and germ, the body digests this flour very quickly, leading to rapid rises in blood sugar. While it is not a chemical additive and poses no immediate safety concerns for the general population, choosing whole-grain alternatives offers far more sustained energy and digestive support.

What the science says

Regulators view wheat flour as a standard food ingredient rather than an additive, meaning no daily intake limit is established. Scientific assessments focus on the nutritional differences between refined and whole grains. Research shows that refining wheat removes essential B vitamins, iron, and dietary fibre. To address this, some countries mandate the enrichment of white flour with synthetic vitamins, though public health guidelines globally still encourage consumers to choose whole grains for better metabolic and digestive health.

Where you'll find it

You will find this ingredient in almost every bakery aisle, appearing in breads, biscuits, cakes, pastries, pasta, and crackers. It is also used as a thickener in sauces, gravies, and ready meals. On packaging, it might be listed simply as wheat flour, or under its European names such as farine de blé or tarwemeel. Because it is a primary food ingredient rather than a food additive, it does not carry an E-number.
