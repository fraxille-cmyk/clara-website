Sodium metabisulphite (E223)

E223AdditiveNeutral · limited concern

Sodium metabisulphite is a sulphite preservative used to keep food fresh and stop discolouration, though it can trigger reactions in sensitive people.

What it is

Sodium metabisulphite is an inorganic salt that belongs to the sulphite family. In the kitchen, you might recognise its close chemical cousins, like the sulphur dioxide used in winemaking. It is a white, crystalline powder that releases a familiar, sharp sulphur smell when dissolved in water. It acts as a powerful preservative by releasing sulphur dioxide gas, which works to keep food looking fresh and stops unwanted microscopic life from growing in our groceries.

How it's made

To make this additive, manufacturers start with sodium hydroxide, commonly known as lye, or sodium carbonate. They pass sulphur dioxide gas through a hot solution of one of these sodium compounds. This chemical reaction creates sodium bisulphite. As the solution is carefully cooled and concentrated, the sodium bisulphite molecules join together and crystallise out of the liquid as sodium metabisulphite. The crystals are then dried, sifted, and packaged for food manufacturers to use.

Why manufacturers use it

Food makers rely on this ingredient because it is an incredibly efficient multi-tasker. It stops the natural browning that happens when cut fruits, vegetables, and potatoes are exposed to the air, keeping them looking fresh and appealing. It also slows down the growth of yeasts, moulds, and bacteria, which extends the shelf life of packaged goods. For manufacturers, it is a highly reliable and cost-effective way to maintain both the colour and the safety of foods during transport and storage.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this NEUTRAL, a limited-concern additive. While it is fine for the vast majority of shoppers, it can trigger breathing difficulties and skin reactions in individuals who are sensitive to sulphites. Because of this, regulators require its presence to be clearly highlighted on food labels when it exceeds a certain level. EFSA has established a temporary acceptable daily intake of 0.7 milligrams per kilogram of body weight, expressed as sulphur dioxide equivalents, to ensure consumers do not ingest too much across their daily diet.

What the science says

The safety of this compound has been thoroughly evaluated by international food safety bodies. JECFA and EFSA have both reviewed the evidence regarding how our bodies metabolise sulphites. An enzyme in our liver normally turns sulphite into sulphate, which is harmlessly excreted in urine. However, because some individuals have lower levels of this enzyme, they can experience sensitivity reactions. EFSA keeps the daily limit under review to ensure it protects sensitive groups, noting that while it is not a concern for most, clear labelling remains essential.

Where you'll find it

You will find this preservative in a wide variety of supermarket items, including dried fruits, dehydrated potatoes, lemon juice, vinegar, and processed seafood. It also appears in some baked goods and prepared meals. On a food label, it is typically listed by its full name, sodium metabisulphite, or by its European code, E223, often with a bold warning to alert those who need to avoid sulphites.
