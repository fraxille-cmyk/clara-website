Indigotine (indigo carmine) (E132)

E132AdditiveAvoid · limited concern

Indigotine is a synthetic blue food colour that Clara rates as an additive to avoid because of ongoing questions about its purity and synthetic nature.

What it is

Indigotine, also known as indigo carmine, is a synthetic dark blue dye used to colour foods and drinks. While natural indigo has been harvested from plants for thousands of years to dye textiles, the food additive is a highly purified, chemically manufactured version. It dissolves easily in water and produces a deep, rich blue shade that can also be blended with yellow dyes to create various shades of green. It is a highly stable colourant that holds up well under light and heat, making it a favourite in the food industry.

How it's made

The production of food-grade indigotine is a fully synthetic process that starts with coal tar derivatives or petroleum-based chemicals. Manufacturers synthesise the dye by reacting indigo with concentrated sulphuric acid, a process known as sulphonation. This chemical reaction makes the dye soluble in water. The resulting mixture is then neutralised with sodium salts, filtered, washed, and dried to form a fine, dark blue powder or granules. This synthetic route ensures a highly consistent colour strength that is difficult to achieve with plant-based extracts.

Why manufacturers use it

Food manufacturers choose indigotine because it provides a vivid, reliable blue colour at a very low cost. Natural blue pigments are notoriously unstable, fading quickly when exposed to light, heat, or acidic environments. Indigotine resists these processing conditions beautifully, ensuring that products look appealing on the supermarket shelf for months. It is also highly efficient, meaning only a tiny amount is needed to colour a large batch of food, which keeps production costs low compared to using expensive natural alternatives.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this AVOID, a limited-concern additive. While it is approved for use in many countries, Clara advises avoiding synthetic dyes when natural alternatives exist. EFSA classifies the additive as authorised and maintains an acceptable daily intake of 5 mg per kg of body weight. Some researchers raise questions about the synthetic impurities that can remain from the manufacturing process, although regulators deem these levels low enough to be of no toxicological concern under normal consumption patterns. No lower limit is established for children, who are the primary consumers of brightly coloured foods.

What the science says

The safety of indigotine has been evaluated by several international bodies over the decades. JECFA and EFSA have both reviewed the toxicological data, concluding that the dye does not accumulate in the body and is largely excreted unchanged. However, because it is a synthetic coal-tar derivative, scientific discussions continue regarding its potential to affect sensitive individuals or contribute to behavioural changes in children, though a direct link remains unproven. Regulators continue to monitor intake levels to ensure that cumulative exposure from food and cosmetics does not exceed the established daily limit.

Where you'll find it

You will find this colour in confectionery, ice cream, sports drinks, cake decorations, and processed desserts. On ingredient labels, it is typically declared as indigotine, indigo carmine, indotine, or by its European code, E132. It is often paired with yellow colours to create bright green shades in lime-flavoured sweets, tinned peas, and green beverages, so it is always worth checking the label of green-coloured foods if you wish to bypass synthetic additives.
