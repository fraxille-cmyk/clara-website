Cellulose (E460)

E460AdditiveNeutral · benign

Cellulose is a natural plant fibre used to add bulk and texture to foods, and Clara rates it as a neutral, benign additive.

What it is

Cellulose is the structural backbone of the plant world, a tough carbohydrate that gives strength to plant cell walls. It is the most abundant organic compound on Earth, found in everything from grass and trees to the fruits and vegetables we eat every day. In the food industry, it is used in a purified form as a natural fibre that does not dissolve in water. Because our bodies cannot break it down, it passes through our digestive system without being absorbed, acting as dietary fibre rather than providing energy or calories.

How it's made

To make the food-grade ingredient, manufacturers start with natural plant sources, most commonly harvested wood pulp or cotton fibres. This raw material undergoes a thorough purification process where it is treated with water and mild acids to dissolve away non-cellulose components like lignin and hemicellulose. The remaining pure cellulose is then washed, neutralised, and dried. Depending on how finely it is ground, it becomes either a fine white powder or a slightly textured substance made of tiny, microscopic crystals.

Why manufacturers use it

Food makers value cellulose because it is an incredibly versatile tool for improving texture and stability without changing the flavour of a product. It acts as a bulking agent, adding volume to low-calorie foods, and helps to stabilise emulsions so ingredients do not separate. In grated cheese, it prevents the individual pieces from sticking together and clumping. It also helps to retain moisture in baked goods, ensuring they stay soft and fresh on the shelf for longer, which makes it a highly cost-effective alternative to more expensive starches.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this NEUTRAL, a benign additive. Because cellulose is a natural plant fibre that our bodies do not absorb, it passes through the digestive tract unchanged. EFSA has evaluated both powdered and microcrystalline cellulose and confirmed they pose no safety concerns, establishing no upper limit on daily intake. It is a familiar substance that behaves in the body just like the natural fibre found in raw vegetables, meaning it is processed safely and naturally by our digestive system without any unwanted accumulation.

What the science says

Major food safety authorities have thoroughly reviewed cellulose and found no reason for concern. The European Food Safety Authority re-evaluated it in 2017 and confirmed that there is no need to set a numerical daily limit because it is non-toxic and does not get absorbed into the bloodstream. JECFA, the joint expert committee of the FAO and WHO, has also reviewed the ingredient and reached the same conclusion. Because it functions simply as an insoluble dietary fibre, scientific consensus agrees that it is a benign addition to our food supply.

Where you'll find it

You will find this ingredient in a wide variety of packaged foods, including shredded cheeses, grated parmesan, spice mixes, baked goods, ice creams, and low-calorie diet products. On ingredient lists, it is typically declared simply as cellulose, powdered cellulose, or microcrystalline cellulose. In European products, it may also be listed by its official additive code, E460, or split into its specific forms as E460i and E460ii, while on US labels it is often listed as microcrystalline cellulose or powdered cellulose.
