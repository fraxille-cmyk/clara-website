Whole wheat flour

Whole foodGood

Diets

✕ Not gluten-free✕ Not low-FODMAP
  • Not gluten-free — whole wheat flour comes from a gluten grain.
  • Not low-FODMAP — whole wheat flour is high in fructans.

Whole wheat flour is a minimally processed whole food that retains all parts of the grain, making it a highly nutritious choice for everyday baking.

What it is

Whole wheat flour is a powdery substance made by grinding entire kernels of wheat. Unlike refined white flour, which has its outer layers stripped away, this flour retains the whole grain in its original proportions. This means it contains the starchy endosperm, the oil-rich germ, and the fibre-packed bran. It is a familiar kitchen staple that brings a rustic texture, a nutty flavour, and a rich brown colour to baked goods, offering more natural nutrients than its highly processed counterparts.

How it's made

The journey starts in the wheat fields where the grain is harvested and cleaned to remove chaff and stones. The whole kernels, known as wheat berries, are then fed into a mill. In traditional stone milling, large rotating stones crush the entire grain into a single, cohesive flour. In modern roller mills, the bran, germ, and endosperm are sometimes separated temporarily during the grinding process to achieve an even grind, but they are always blended back together at the end in the exact proportions of the original grain.

Why manufacturers use it

Food makers choose this ingredient to improve the nutritional profile of their products and to appeal to shoppers looking for wholesome options. It provides a hearty structure and a robust, nutty flavour that works beautifully in rustic breads, crackers, and biscuits. Commercially, it allows manufacturers to make whole-grain claims on their packaging. While the natural oils in the germ mean it has a slightly shorter shelf life than refined white flour, the rich texture and earthy taste it adds to baked goods make it a highly desirable ingredient.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this GOOD. As a minimally processed whole food rather than an additive, it is a nourishing ingredient that fits into a balanced diet. It naturally contains dietary fibre, B vitamins, and minerals that are often lost during the refining of white flour. Because it is a natural grain product, no daily intake limit is established, and it is widely recognised by food authorities worldwide as a wholesome staple. It is a simple, traditional ingredient that you can feel comfortable eating every day.

What the science says

Food safety authorities and health organisations have evaluated whole grains extensively. The consensus is that retaining the bran and germ provides important dietary fibre and micronutrients that support a well-functioning digestive system and help steady the release of energy. EFSA and the FDA recognise whole grains as a beneficial part of a daily diet. Because it is a standard agricultural commodity rather than a chemical additive, it does not undergo the restrictive additive evaluations, and no safety concerns or limits are associated with its consumption.

Where you'll find it

You will find this ingredient in the bakery aisle, appearing in wholemeal breads, wraps, crackers, pasta, and breakfast cereals. On packaging, it might be listed under its common name, whole wheat flour, or as wholegrain wheat flour and whole grain wheat flour. If you are buying imported European goods, you might spot it under its Dutch name, volkoren tarwemeel. It does not have an E-number because it is a whole food ingredient rather than a food additive.
