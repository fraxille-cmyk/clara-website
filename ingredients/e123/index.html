Amaranth (E123)

E123AdditiveAvoid · high concern

Amaranth is a synthetic dark red food colour banned in the US and highly restricted in Europe due to historical safety concerns.

What it is

Amaranth is a synthetic dark red food dye that has no relation to the nutritious ancient grain of the same name. It belongs to a chemical family known as azo dyes, which are famous for producing intense, stable colours that do not fade easily under heat or light. In the food industry, it is used to give products a deep, rich reddish-brown or purple hue. Because it is entirely artificial, it does not offer any nutritional value, serving purely to alter the visual appearance of what we consume.

How it's made

This colour is manufactured entirely in a laboratory through a chemical synthesis process. It is derived from petroleum-based starting materials, which undergo a chemical reaction called diazotisation. During this process, organic compounds are coupled together to form the characteristic azo bond that gives the dye its intense red pigment. The resulting substance is then purified, crystallised, and prepared as a sodium salt powder or liquid concentrate, ready to be added to commercial formulations.

Why manufacturers use it

Food manufacturers use this dye because synthetic colours are far more stable, predictable, and inexpensive than natural alternatives derived from plants or insects. It maintains its deep red shade even when exposed to high temperatures during processing, acidic environments, or long periods on store shelves. A very small amount of this highly concentrated powder can colour thousands of litres of liquid, making it an incredibly cost-effective way to standardise the appearance of products and make them look more appealing to shoppers.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this AVOID, a high-concern additive. This rating is based on its regulatory history, as the FDA delisted and banned the colour in 1976 due to safety concerns. In Europe, the European Food Safety Authority maintains a very low acceptable daily intake of 0.15 mg per kg of body weight. While it remains authorised in the European Union, its use is highly restricted to a small number of specific products, reflecting a cautious regulatory approach to this synthetic substance.

What the science says

The safety of this dye has been evaluated by several major international bodies over the decades. IARC classifies amaranth as Group 3, not classifiable as to its carcinogenicity to humans. While European regulators at EFSA re-evaluated the colour in 2010 and decided to keep the strict daily limit, they did not find new evidence to warrant a complete ban. However, the US FDA took a much stricter stance decades ago, concluding that safety could not be guaranteed, which led to its removal from the American market.

Where you'll find it

Because of strict European regulations, you will not find this colour in everyday groceries like sweets or soft drinks. Instead, it is restricted to niche categories such as aperitif wines, spirit drinks, and fish roe. On ingredient labels in the UK and Europe, it is typically listed by its name as amaranth or by its European code, E123. On older labels or products from other regions, you might occasionally see it referred to as FD&C Red No. 2.
