Brilliant blue FCF (E133)

E133AdditiveAvoid · moderate concern

Diets

✓ Suits every diet Clara checks — as an ingredient

A synthetic blue food colour made from petroleum that is used purely for cosmetic reasons to make processed foods look bright and appealing.

What it is

Brilliant blue FCF is a synthetic dye that gives food a vibrant, artificial blue shade. It belongs to a class of chemicals derived from coal tar or petroleum, rather than any plant or mineral found in nature. Because it is highly soluble in water and incredibly stable under heat and light, it is a favourite tool for food designers who want to create eye-catching shades that do not fade on the supermarket shelf. It has no nutritional value and is used solely to alter the appearance of what we eat.

How it's made

This colour is manufactured through a complex chemical synthesis starting with aromatic hydrocarbons derived from petroleum. Specifically, it is made by condensing benzaldehyde-o-sulfonic acid with ethyl-benzyl-aniline-sulfonic acid. The resulting compound is then oxidised, purified, and converted into a sodium or ammonium salt. The final product is a dark reddish-blue powder or granule that dissolves easily in water to produce a brilliant, clear blue hue. Because it is entirely synthetic, its production relies on industrial chemical processes rather than agricultural harvesting.

Why manufacturers use it

Food manufacturers use this dye because natural blue pigments are notoriously unstable, expensive, and prone to turning grey or brown when heated or exposed to light. This synthetic alternative is cheap, highly concentrated, and maintains its bright colour indefinitely. It is also frequently blended with yellow dyes to create vivid green shades, or with red dyes to produce deep purples. This versatility allows manufacturers to create highly appealing, consistent, and colourful products that attract shoppers, especially children, at a fraction of the cost of natural extracts.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this AVOID, a moderate-concern additive. This synthetic colouring serves a purely cosmetic purpose, meaning it represents an unnecessary industrial exposure with no benefit to your health. While EFSA permits its use with an acceptable daily intake of 6 mg per kg of body weight, choosing to bypass synthetic dyes is a straightforward way to reduce your intake of highly processed, non-nutritional chemical additives. Since it offers no nutritional or preservative advantages, avoiding it helps keep your diet closer to whole, unadulterated foods.

What the science says

The safety of this dye has been evaluated by major international food authorities. JECFA established an acceptable daily intake of 6 mg per kg of body weight, a limit that EFSA confirmed during its re-evaluation of food colours. Most of the ingested dye passes through the digestive system unabsorbed, though a tiny fraction can enter the bloodstream. While regulators deem the current exposure levels acceptable for the general population, some consumer advocacy groups raise questions about the sensitivity of certain individuals to synthetic dyes, and research continues into how these compounds interact with our biology.

Where you'll find it

You will find this colour in a wide variety of highly processed foods, including tinned peas, blue raspberry-flavoured sweets, ice creams, cake decorations, sports drinks, and liqueurs. On ingredient labels, it is commonly listed by its name, brilliant blue FCF, or under its alternative names such as blue 1, FD&C Blue No. 1, or its European code, E133. It is often tucked away at the very end of the ingredient list, sometimes grouped under a general heading of colours.
