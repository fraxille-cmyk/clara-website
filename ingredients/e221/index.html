Sodium sulphite (E221)

E221AdditiveAvoid · moderate concern

A synthetic preservative used to prevent browning and spoilage, but EFSA maintains a temporary safe limit that many people exceed in their daily diet.

What it is

Sodium sulphite is a soluble sodium salt of sulphurous acid. It belongs to a family of sulphur-based compounds commonly used in food processing to keep products looking fresh. In its pure form, it is a white, odourless powder that dissolves easily in water. It acts as a powerful reducing agent, which means it reacts readily with oxygen in its environment, helping to protect delicate food components from the degrading effects of air exposure.

How it's made

This ingredient is produced synthetically in industrial facilities. Manufacturers prepare it by reacting sulphur dioxide gas with a solution of sodium carbonate or sodium hydroxide. This chemical reaction yields sodium sulphite, which is then purified, crystallised, and dried into a stable powder. Because it is entirely synthetic, it does not rely on any animal or plant-derived raw materials during its manufacture.

Why manufacturers use it

Food producers rely on this additive primarily to prevent browning and preserve the appealing colour of foods. When fruits, vegetables, or shellfish are cut or processed, exposure to oxygen triggers enzymes that turn them brown. Sodium sulphite stops this enzymatic reaction in its tracks. It also acts as an antimicrobial agent, slowing down the growth of unwanted micro-organisms, which extends the shelf life of packaged goods and maintains a clean look on supermarket shelves.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this AVOID, a moderate-concern additive. While it is authorised for use, the European Food Safety Authority maintains a temporary daily limit of 0.7 milligrams per kilogram of body weight due to unresolved uncertainties and gaps in the scientific data. Furthermore, EFSA exposure assessments show that many consumer groups regularly exceed this temporary limit through their normal daily diet. IARC classifies sulphites as Group 3, not classifiable as to their carcinogenicity to humans.

What the science says

Regulators have evaluated this compound several times to determine how it behaves in the human body. When consumed, it is normally metabolised by enzymes into sulphate, which is then excreted. However, some individuals have a lower tolerance to sulphur compounds and may experience sensitivity reactions. Because of data gaps regarding its long-term accumulation and systemic effects, EFSA has kept its safety limit temporary until more comprehensive studies are completed, noting that current dietary intake levels are higher than ideal.

Where you'll find it

You will find this preservative in a variety of processed foods, particularly dried fruits, peeled potatoes, prepared vegetables, and certain wines. On ingredient labels in the United Kingdom and Europe, it is typically declared by its functional name followed by its E-number, appearing as sodium sulphite or E221. On US labels, it is listed as sodium sulfite, and federal regulations prohibit its use on meats, raw fruits and vegetables, or foods that serve as a source of vitamin B1.
