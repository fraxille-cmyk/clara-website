Potato

Whole foodGood

Diets

✓ Suits every diet Clara checks — as an ingredient

A whole food that provides carbohydrates and some fibre, making it a nutritious and natural ingredient in many savoury products.

What it is

A potato is a starchy tuber that grows underground on the plant Solanum tuberosum. It is a staple whole food, familiar to kitchens everywhere, and is prized for its versatility and comforting texture. Biologically, it serves as the energy storage unit for the plant, which translates to a rich source of complex carbohydrates and dietary fibre when we eat it. Unlike highly processed additives, it remains a complete, natural food that retains its inherent nutrients, including potassium and vitamin C, when handled gently during food preparation.

How it's made

The journey of this ingredient is remarkably straightforward. Potatoes are planted in soil, grown to maturity, and harvested from the earth. Once dug up, they are washed thoroughly to remove soil, sorted by size, and inspected for quality. Depending on how they will be used in a final product, they are either kept whole, sliced, diced, or cooked and mashed. For some packaged foods, they are dehydrated into flakes or flour, a process that simply involves cooking, mashing, and drying the vegetable on large heated rollers without chemical modification.

Why manufacturers use it

Food manufacturers use this ingredient because it provides a natural, recognisable base that consumers trust. It acts as an excellent thickener, binder, and texturiser in soups, stews, and ready meals. The natural starches within it absorb water during heating, which helps to create a smooth, satisfying mouthfeel and gives body to sauces. It is also highly cost-effective and structurally stable, meaning it holds its shape well during freezing, thawing, and canning processes, which beats out many synthetic thickening agents that might break down under heat.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this GOOD. As a whole food, it is a nourishing ingredient that provides essential carbohydrates and some fibre to support daily energy needs. There are no regulatory limits or safety concerns associated with eating it as part of a balanced diet, and no limit is established for its consumption. It is digested naturally by the body, breaking down into simple sugars that fuel our muscles and brain, while its fibre content helps support a steady digestive process. It is a wholesome addition to any packaged meal.

What the science says

Because it is a traditional whole food rather than a novel additive, scientific evaluations focus on its nutritional value rather than chemical safety. Major food authorities view it as a core dietary component. The FAO and WHO recognise it as an important source of energy and nutrients globally. Research shows that when prepared without excessive added fats, it provides highly bioavailable minerals and complex carbohydrates. No regulatory body has raised safety concerns regarding its standard use in food products, and it remains a universally accepted dietary staple.

Where you'll find it

You will find this ingredient in a vast array of supermarket products, ranging from ready meals, canned soups, and frozen hotpots to savoury snacks, crisps, and pre-made potato salads. On packaging labels, it is listed simply as potato or potatoes, with no E-number associated with it because it is a whole food rather than a food additive. It may also appear in its dried forms, such as potato flakes or potato flour, in gluten-free baking mixes and instant mashed potato packets, where it serves as a natural structuring ingredient that keeps the recipe simple and clean.
