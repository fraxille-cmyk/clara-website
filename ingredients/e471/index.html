Mono- and diglycerides of fatty acids (E471)

E471AdditiveNeutral · limited concern

A very common plant- or animal-based emulsifier used to blend oil and water, keep bread soft, and give ice cream a smooth texture.

What it is

Mono- and diglycerides of fatty acids are a family of food additives used to blend ingredients that would otherwise separate. They are closely related to natural fats, which are triglycerides. While a triglyceride has three fatty acids attached to a glycerol backbone, these substances have only one or two. This unique structure allows one end of the molecule to attract water while the other end attracts oil, making them highly effective at holding mixtures together. They are found naturally in small amounts in many foods and are digested by the body in the same way as regular dietary fats.

How it's made

These substances are produced by reacting glycerol with natural fats and oils, which can come from either plant or animal sources. Plant sources typically include soybean, rapeseed, sunflower, or palm oil, while animal sources are derived from lard or tallow. The mixture is heated under pressure in the presence of a catalyst to break down the triglycerides into mono- and diglycerides. The resulting material is then refined, deodorised, and sometimes fractionated to achieve the desired consistency, which can range from a pale yellow liquid to a hard, waxy solid.

Why manufacturers use it

Manufacturers rely on this ingredient to stabilise emulsions, which are mixtures of oil and water that naturally want to separate. In baked goods, it interacts with starches to slow down the staling process, keeping bread soft and extending its shelf life. In ice cream and frozen desserts, it helps control the size of ice crystals, ensuring a smooth, creamy texture that does not become gritty. It is highly favoured because it is incredibly efficient, requiring only tiny amounts to achieve a stable texture that holds up well during transport and storage.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this NEUTRAL, a limited-concern additive. Because these compounds are structurally almost identical to the fats we eat every day, the body recognises and metabolises them in the exact same way. They are broken down into glycerol and fatty acids during digestion. No safety concerns have been identified at typical dietary levels, and no daily limit is established by regulators. However, because they are often derived from palm oil or animal fats, some shoppers prefer to minimise them for environmental or dietary reasons.

What the science says

Major food safety authorities have thoroughly evaluated this ingredient and confirmed its safety. The European Food Safety Authority re-evaluated it in 2017 and concluded that there was no need to establish a numerical acceptable daily intake, as it poses no safety concerns at the levels found in food. The Joint FAO/WHO Expert Committee on Food Additives also allocated an acceptable daily intake of not specified, which is their safest classification. Scientists continue to monitor the purity of these additives to ensure they do not contain unwanted manufacturing by-products, but the substance itself remains widely accepted.

Where you'll find it

You will find this ingredient in a wide variety of processed foods, including sliced bread, cakes, margarine, peanut butter, ice cream, and ready meals. On ingredient lists, it is commonly declared as mono- and diglycerides of fatty acids, or simply E471. Because it can be sourced from either plants or animals, vegetarian and vegan products will often specify if the ingredient is of plant origin, though the chemical structure remains identical in both cases.
