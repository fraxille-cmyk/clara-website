A common emulsifier made from fats that helps blend oil and water, rated neutral by Clara with no safety limits or major concerns.

What it is

This ingredient is a mixture of food molecules that help oil and water mix together smoothly. Chemically, they are very similar to the natural fats found in vegetable oils and animal fats, but they have been slightly altered. While a typical dietary fat molecule has three fatty acids attached to a glycerin backbone, these molecules have only one or two. This structure gives them a dual nature, allowing one end to cling to water and the other to cling to fat, which keeps mixtures stable.

How it's made

To make this ingredient, manufacturers start with fats and oils, which can come from either plant sources like palm, soya, or rapeseed, or from animal sources. These fats are reacted with glycerol at high temperatures in the presence of an alkaline catalyst. This chemical process breaks down the triple-fatty-acid structures into single and double forms. The resulting mixture is then refined, deodorised, and sometimes crystallised into a powder or paste that can be easily added to food formulations.

Why manufacturers use it

Manufacturers rely on this ingredient because it is an exceptionally reliable emulsifier. Without it, processed foods containing both fat and water would quickly separate into unappealing layers. It gives ice cream a smooth, creamy texture and prevents it from melting too quickly. In baked goods, it interacts with starches to keep bread soft and prevent it from going stale, while in margarine and spreads, it ensures a consistent texture that is easy to spread straight from the fridge.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this NEUTRAL, a benign additive. Because your body digests these molecules in the exact same way as ordinary dietary fats, they do not pose any unique safety risks. The European Food Safety Authority evaluated this substance and determined that no acceptable daily intake limit needs to be specified. This means it is considered safe to consume at the levels typically found in food, with no known accumulation or unwanted effects in the body.

What the science says

The scientific consensus on this ingredient is highly stable. Major food safety authorities have reviewed the scientific evidence and found no cause for concern. Because these molecules are normal intermediates in the digestion of dietary fat, the body processes them using its standard metabolic pathways. No toxicological concerns have been identified in animal or human studies, and regulators have not found any reason to restrict its use or set a maximum daily intake limit.

Where you'll find it

You will find this ingredient in a vast range of processed foods, especially bakery products, cakes, margarines, ice creams, and whipped toppings. On a food label, it is usually listed as mono- and diglycerides of fatty acids, or simply by its European code, E471. On US labels, it is often listed as mono- and diglycerides. If you prefer to avoid animal-derived ingredients, it is worth checking with the manufacturer, as the source fats can be either vegetable or animal in origin.
