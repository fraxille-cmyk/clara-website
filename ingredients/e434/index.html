Polysorbate 40 (E434)

E434AdditiveAvoid · moderate concern

Clara rates this synthetic emulsifier as AVOID because several population groups can easily exceed the established daily limit in their normal diet.

What it is

Polysorbate 40 is a synthetic emulsifier used to blend ingredients that would naturally separate, such as oil and water. It is a chemically modified compound made by combining a sugar alcohol called sorbitol with a fatty acid called palmitic acid, which is commonly found in palm oil, and then reacting the mixture with ethylene oxide. This process creates a sticky, amber-coloured liquid that helps oil droplets stay suspended in water, giving processed foods a smooth, uniform texture that lasts for months on the shelf.

How it's made

The production of polysorbate 40 is an industrial chemical process. It begins with sorbitol, a sugar alcohol derived from corn syrup, which is dehydrated to form a compound called sorbitan. This sorbitan is then reacted with palmitic acid, a fatty acid usually sourced from vegetable oils. To make the substance water-soluble, the resulting ester is treated with ethylene oxide in a process called ethoxylation. This chemical modification alters the structure to ensure it can bond with both water and fat molecules, creating the final synthetic additive.

Why manufacturers use it

Food manufacturers rely on polysorbate 40 because it is a highly efficient stabiliser and dispersing agent. It prevents oils and fats from separating in liquid foods, which is essential for maintaining an appealing appearance and texture. It is particularly valued because it works well in small quantities and remains stable across a wide range of temperatures and acidity levels. This makes it more versatile and cost-effective than natural alternatives like lecithin, helping to extend the shelf life of processed foods and maintain a consistent mouthfeel from the factory to the kitchen.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this AVOID, a moderate-concern additive. While EFSA has established a group acceptable daily intake of 25 mg per kg of body weight, the regulator noted in its evaluation that this limit was exceeded at high consumption levels by several population groups. Because it is easy to consume more than the recommended limit through a standard diet of processed foods, Clara advises keeping this additive out of your shopping basket. There is no toxicological concern at low levels, but the potential for overconsumption makes caution necessary.

What the science says

Major food authorities have evaluated polysorbate 40 to determine how it behaves in the human body. When consumed, it is partially broken down in the digestive tract, where the fatty acid portion is absorbed and metabolised just like dietary fat, while the remaining portion is excreted in urine and faeces. In 2015, EFSA conducted a comprehensive re-evaluation of polysorbates and raised concerns that high consumers, particularly children, could exceed the group safety limit. The regulator concluded that exposure estimates should be refined, but the current limit remains in place.

Where you'll find it

You will find this ingredient in a variety of processed foods, including bakery products, cake mixes, ice cream, frozen desserts, salad dressings, and cocoa-based confectionery. On ingredient labels in the United Kingdom and Europe, it is typically listed as emulsifier: polysorbate 40 or by its European code, E434. On US labels, it is listed as polysorbate 40, where its use is permitted in specific food categories under federal regulations.
