Stearyl tartrate is a synthetic emulsifier used to improve bread texture, but it is now banned in the EU due to a lack of safety data.

What it is

Stearyl tartrate is a synthetic food additive that belongs to the family of emulsifiers. It is created by combining stearyl alcohol, which is a fatty substance often derived from coconut or palm oil, with tartaric acid, an organic acid found naturally in grapes. The resulting compound is a waxy, cream-coloured solid that helps oil and water mix together smoothly. While it sounds like a natural fat, it is a highly modified substance designed specifically for industrial food processing.

How it's made

The production of stearyl tartrate is a multi-step chemical process. Manufacturers start with stearyl alcohol, which is typically obtained by chemically reducing stearic acid from plant oils or animal fats. This alcohol is then heated and reacted with tartaric acid under controlled conditions in a laboratory or chemical plant. This reaction, known as esterification, alters the molecular structure to create a compound that can bond with both water and fat molecules at the same time.

Why manufacturers use it

Food manufacturers use stearyl tartrate primarily as a dough strengthener and emulsifier in baked goods. It interacts with the gluten proteins and starches in flour, making the dough more elastic and easier to handle in large industrial machines. This results in bread with a larger volume, a more uniform crumb structure, and a softer texture. It also helps retain moisture, which keeps packaged baked goods feeling fresh on supermarket shelves for a longer period.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this AVOID, a high-concern additive. The European Union withdrew its authorisation for this substance because there was not enough scientific evidence to prove its long-term safety. When regulators cannot establish a clear limit for how much a person can consume daily without risk, the ingredient is removed from the market. While it is not proven to cause harm, the lack of basic data means we cannot be sure of its effects on the body.

What the science says

The scientific consensus on stearyl tartrate is defined by a lack of information rather than evidence of harm. EFSA evaluated the additive and withdrew the acceptable daily intake in 2020 because of severe gaps in the available toxicological data. Without comprehensive studies on how the body metabolises and excretes this compound, regulators cannot confirm its safety. In contrast, the FDA permits its use under specific regulations, showing how different authorities handle gaps in scientific literature.

Where you'll find it

You will primarily find this ingredient in industrial bakery products, such as packaged sliced bread, rolls, buns, and cake mixes. On ingredient labels, it may be listed by its name, stearyl tartrate, or by its European code, E483. On US labels, it is sometimes listed as stearyl tartrate under specific food additive regulations. Because of recent regulatory changes, it is disappearing from European products but may still appear in foods imported from other regions.
