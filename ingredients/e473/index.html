Sucrose esters of fatty acids (E473)

E473AdditiveAvoid · moderate concern

A synthetic emulsifier that mixes oil and water, rated AVOID because some people may consume more than the recommended daily limit.

What it is

Sucrose esters of fatty acids are synthetic emulsifiers made by joining ordinary table sugar with fatty acids. In nature, sugar and fat do not easily bond, but this chemical marriage creates a molecule where one end clings to water and the other clings to oil. This unique structure allows the substance to act as a bridge, keeping ingredients together that would otherwise separate into unappealing layers. It is a highly modified compound designed to mimic the natural emulsifying properties found in egg yolks, but with much greater stability and strength.

How it's made

To manufacture this ingredient, producers start with sucrose, which is common sugar refined from sugar beet or sugar cane. This sugar is chemically reacted with fatty acids, which are typically sourced from vegetable oils like palm, coconut, or soy, though animal fats can sometimes be used. The reaction requires a chemical solvent and a catalyst to force the sugar and fat molecules to bond. After the reaction is complete, the mixture is carefully purified, washed, and dried to remove the solvents, leaving behind a fine, odourless, off-white powder.

Why manufacturers use it

Manufacturers rely on this ingredient because it is an exceptionally versatile emulsifier. It keeps oils and water-based liquids perfectly blended, which prevents separation in bottled sauces and creamy drinks. It also modifies the texture of fats, helping to create a smooth mouthfeel in low-fat spreads and preventing fat bloom in chocolate. In baked goods, it interacts with starches to keep bread soft and prevent it from going stale. It is favoured over natural alternatives because it remains stable under high heat and across a wide range of acidity levels.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this AVOID, a moderate-concern additive. While the ingredient itself has no known toxicological concerns, EFSA established an acceptable daily intake of 40 mg per kg of body weight in 2004. In a subsequent 2018 assessment, EFSA raised concerns because exposure estimates showed that some population groups, particularly children, could easily exceed this daily limit through their normal diet. Because it is so easy to consume more than the recommended limit, Clara advises choosing products without this additive to keep your overall intake within a comfortable range.

What the science says

This additive has been thoroughly evaluated by major food safety authorities. EFSA and JECFA have both analysed its safety, concluding that the body breaks it down into normal dietary components, specifically sucrose and free fatty acids, which are then metabolised in the usual way. However, because it is used in a very wide variety of processed foods, scientists monitor how much of it we consume in total. The main open question is not about its safety at low levels, but how to manage its widespread use so that young children do not exceed the established daily limit.

Where you'll find it

You will find this ingredient in a wide range of processed foods, including flavoured milk drinks, non-dairy creamers, chewing gum, ice cream, processed fruit, and baked goods. On ingredient lists in the UK and Europe, it is typically declared as sucrose esters of fatty acids or by its E-number, E473. On US labels, it is permitted as a direct food additive and is usually listed under the name sucrose fatty acid esters.
