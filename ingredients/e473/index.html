Sucrose esters of fatty acids (E473)

E473AdditiveNeutral · limited concern

An emulsifier made by linking sugar with fatty acids. Clara rates it as neutral, with a daily intake limit established by European regulators.

What it is

Sucrose esters of fatty acids are a group of chemically modified compounds that act as emulsifiers. They are created by joining ordinary table sugar, known as sucrose, with fatty acids, which are the building blocks of fats. This unique combination allows them to interact with both water and oil, helping substances that normally separate to stay smoothly blended. While they are synthetic in their final form, they are built from familiar, naturally occurring components that the body recognises and processes.

How it's made

To manufacture this ingredient, producers start with sucrose, usually refined from sugar cane or sugar beet, and combine it with fatty acids derived from edible oils or fats. Because sugar and fat do not naturally bond, the reaction requires a chemical process called transesterification, often using solvents and catalysts to force the molecules to link together. The mixture is then carefully purified, washed, and dried to remove any remaining solvents, leaving behind a fine, off-white powder or a waxy solid ready for food production.

Why manufacturers use it

Manufacturers rely on these esters because they are exceptionally versatile emulsifiers. They help create stable oil-in-water mixtures, preventing separation in bottled drinks, sauces, and dressings. In baked goods, they interact with starches and proteins to improve the texture, increase the volume of loaves, and keep bread soft for longer. They also help distribute fat evenly in chocolates and confectionery, preventing a greyish fat bloom from forming on the surface and ensuring a smooth melt in the mouth.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this NEUTRAL, a limited-concern additive. This rating reflects the fact that while the ingredient is generally well tolerated, regulators have set a specific limit on how much should be consumed daily. EFSA keeps its daily limit of 40 mg per kg of body weight, which was established in 2004. Because it is broken down in the digestive tract into ordinary sugar and fatty acids, it does not accumulate in the body, and there are no major safety concerns when consumed within this recommended limit.

What the science says

Scientific bodies have thoroughly evaluated these esters over several decades. The Scientific Committee on Food and later the European Food Safety Authority analysed how the body processes the compound, confirming that it splits into normal dietary components during digestion. A comprehensive re-evaluation by EFSA in 2018 reaffirmed the safety of the ingredient and maintained the existing acceptable daily intake. No toxicological concerns or adverse effects have been identified in these systematic reviews, and no limit is established by the FDA beyond general good manufacturing practices.

Where you'll find it

You will find this ingredient in a wide variety of processed foods, including bakery products, dairy alternatives, flavoured drinks, chewing gum, and processed meat. On ingredient lists in the United Kingdom and Europe, it is typically declared as sucrose esters of fatty acids or by its E-number, E473. On US labels, it is listed as sucrose fatty acid esters, where it is permitted under federal regulations as a direct food additive.
