Ammonia caramel (E150c)

E150cAdditiveAvoid · moderate concern

Ammonia caramel is a dark brown food colour made with ammonia compounds, which can create a byproduct that IARC classifies as Group 2B, possibly carcinogenic.

What it is

Ammonia caramel is a dark brown food colour used to give processed foods a rich, dark appearance. While the word caramel makes us think of sugar melted in a saucepan at home, this is a highly industrialised substance. It is one of four distinct classes of caramel colouring used in the food industry. This specific version is chemically modified using ammonia compounds during the heating process, which alters its molecular structure so that it behaves differently in food mixtures compared to ordinary melted sugar.

How it's made

To make this colour, manufacturers heat carbohydrates, such as glucose syrup or sucrose, under controlled temperature and pressure. Unlike home cooking, they introduce ammonia compounds into the mixture. This chemical reaction, known as caramelisation, is accelerated and modified by the ammonia. The process results in a complex mixture of dark compounds. During this reaction, a byproduct called 4-methylimidazole, commonly known as 4-MEI, is unintentionally formed. The final liquid or powder is then filtered and standardised for use in food factories.

Why manufacturers use it

Manufacturers use this ingredient because it is an incredibly stable and cheap way to colour food. It dissolves easily in water and remains stable under light, heat, and varying acidity levels. This makes it much more reliable than natural plant extracts, which tend to fade on supermarket shelves. It also has a positive electrical charge, which prevents it from clumping or settling when mixed with certain proteins and starches, ensuring that packaged foods maintain an even, appealing brown colour throughout their shelf life.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this AVOID, a moderate-concern additive. The main concern is that the manufacturing process creates a byproduct called 4-methylimidazole, or 4-MEI. IARC classifies 4-methylimidazole as Group 2B, possibly carcinogenic to humans. Because this colour is added purely for cosmetic reasons and adds nothing nutritionally to your diet, avoiding it is a sensible way to limit your exposure to this byproduct. EFSA considers it safe within established limits, but has raised concerns about how much of it children might be consuming.

What the science says

Regulators have evaluated this colour to ensure it does not pose an immediate risk, but they keep a close eye on dietary intake. EFSA established an acceptable daily intake of 300 milligrams per kilogram of body weight in 2011, but noted that highly exposed toddlers and children might exceed a refined limit of 100 milligrams per kilogram of body weight. While the colour itself is authorised for use, the presence of the manufacturing byproduct remains a point of discussion among food safety experts who monitor cumulative exposure from multiple processed foods.

Where you'll find it

You will find this ingredient in a wide variety of dark-coloured foods and drinks. It is commonly added to beers, stouts, dark sauces, gravies, vinegar, and pre-packaged bakery items. On a food label, it is usually listed as ammonia caramel, plain caramel, or by its European code E150c. On US labels it is listed as caramel color, where it is exempt from certification. Checking the ingredients list on savoury sauces and baked goods is the easiest way to spot and avoid it.
