Inosinic acid is a benign flavour enhancer that boosts savoury taste, naturally found in meat and fish, and rated neutral by Clara.

What it is

Inosinic acid is a naturally occurring substance that plays a fundamental role in how we perceive savoury tastes. It is a type of organic compound called a nucleotide, which is found in abundance in animal tissues, particularly in fish, poultry, and red meat. When you taste a rich, slow-cooked broth or a mature cheese, you are experiencing the natural effects of this compound. In the food industry, it is used to replicate and intensify this deep, satisfying savoury sensation, which is often referred to as umami.

How it's made

Although it occurs naturally in animal tissues, the inosinic acid used as a food additive is typically produced through a modern fermentation process. Manufacturers use specific strains of microbes, such as bacteria or yeasts, which are fed a simple sugar source like starch or molasses. These microbes naturally produce the compound as they grow. The mixture is then carefully filtered, purified, and crystallised to isolate the pure acid, resulting in a white powder that can be easily dissolved into food mixtures.

Why manufacturers use it

Manufacturers add this compound to foods to enhance and deepen savoury flavours. It has a remarkable synergistic relationship with glutamates, which are other flavour-enhancing compounds. When combined, they multiply the savoury taste of a product far more than either could do alone. This allows food companies to create rich, meaty, or brothy flavours in processed foods without having to use expensive meat extracts, and it helps them reduce the overall salt content of a recipe while keeping the taste satisfying.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this NEUTRAL, a benign additive. Because it is a substance that the human body is entirely familiar with and metabolises naturally, it does not carry the safety concerns associated with some synthetic additives. The European Food Safety Authority has evaluated its safety and determined that no limit is established for its daily intake, meaning it is considered safe for the general population at the levels typically used in food. There are no known adverse effects from consuming it in normal dietary amounts.

What the science says

The scientific consensus on this compound is highly stable. Major food safety authorities have thoroughly assessed its safety profile. The European Food Safety Authority re-evaluated the additive in a comprehensive review and confirmed that it poses no safety concerns, choosing not to set a numerical limit on daily consumption. The US Food and Drug Administration also permits its use as a flavour enhancer. Because it is broken down in the body into compounds that are naturally present in our cells, it is processed without any unusual metabolic strain.

Where you'll find it

You will commonly find this ingredient in highly savoury, processed foods such as instant noodles, potato crisps, seasoned snack mixes, canned soups, commercial sauces, and pre-packaged gravy mixes. On ingredient lists, it is typically declared by its name as inosinic acid, or by its European code, E630. It is frequently listed alongside monosodium glutamate or other nucleotides, as they are almost always used in combination to achieve the maximum flavour-enhancing effect.
