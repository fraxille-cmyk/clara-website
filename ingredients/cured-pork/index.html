Cured pork is pork preserved with salt and seasonings, offering rich flavour and a long shelf life, though it is best enjoyed in moderation.

What it is

Cured pork is simply pork meat that has been preserved using salt, seasonings, and sometimes sugar or smoke. It is a traditional food preparation that humans have used for thousands of years to keep meat from spoiling before the invention of refrigeration. You already know it in everyday forms like bacon, pancetta, prosciutto, and traditional hams. The curing process changes the texture of the meat, making it firmer, and deepens its natural pink colour while concentrating its savoury flavours.

How it's made

The journey starts with cuts of pork, such as the belly, leg, or shoulder. Producers apply a curing mixture, which always contains salt and often includes sugar, spices, and sometimes small amounts of sodium nitrite to preserve the pink colour and stop bacterial growth. This mixture is either rubbed directly onto the surface of the meat for dry curing, or dissolved in water to make a brine for wet curing. After curing, the pork may be air-dried for months to develop complex flavours, or smoked over wood chips to add a distinctive aroma.

Why manufacturers use it

Food makers choose cured pork because it delivers a deep, savoury depth of flavour and a satisfyingly firm texture that fresh meat cannot match. From a commercial standpoint, the curing process draws out moisture and raises the salt content, which stops the growth of spoilage organisms. This gives the product a much longer shelf life and makes it easier to transport and store without spoiling. It also allows manufacturers to create convenient, ready-to-eat sliced meats that retain their appealing pink colour on supermarket shelves.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this NEUTRAL. While cured pork is a traditional food rather than a modern chemical additive, it is often high in sodium and may contain added nitrites used during the preservation process. Because it is a whole food ingredient and not an additive, no daily intake limit is established for cured pork itself, though dietary guidelines generally recommend enjoying processed meats in moderation. Choosing varieties made with traditional dry-curing methods can help you minimise your intake of synthetic preserving agents.

What the science says

The scientific consensus on cured pork focuses on the preservation methods used to make it. JECFA and EFSA have evaluated the safety of the nitrites and nitrates frequently used in the curing process, establishing acceptable daily intakes to ensure consumer safety. Researchers note that the high salt content in these products can affect how the body regulates fluid balance and blood pressure. Because of these factors, major food safety authorities recommend balancing cured meats with plenty of fresh, unprocessed foods in your daily diet.

Where you'll find it

You will find cured pork in the chilled aisle of the supermarket, sold as bacon, pancetta, prosciutto, salami, and various types of deli ham. It also appears as a flavouring ingredient in ready meals, tinned soups, and savoury pastries. On a food label, it is typically listed plainly as cured pork, cured ham, or pork cured with salt, often followed by a list of the specific spices, sugars, or preservatives used in the curing recipe.
