Glyceryl triacetate is a synthetic liquid used to dissolve and carry food flavourings, rated neutral by Clara because it has no known health concerns.

What it is

Glyceryl triacetate, often called triacetin, is a clear, odourless, and oily liquid. Chemically, it is a compound made by combining glycerol, which is a natural alcohol found in fats, with acetic acid, the main component of household vinegar. Although these raw materials are found in nature, the ingredient itself is a synthetic creation. It behaves as a solvent, meaning it helps to dissolve other substances that do not easily mix with water, making it a highly useful tool in food chemistry.

How it's made

To manufacture this ingredient, chemical producers react glycerol with acetic acid or acetic anhydride under controlled heat. This chemical process is known as esterification, where three acetic acid molecules attach to a single glycerol molecule. After the reaction is complete, the mixture undergoes a series of purification steps. It is neutralised, washed with water, and distilled to remove any leftover raw materials, resulting in a highly pure, stable liquid ready for food production.

Why manufacturers use it

Food manufacturers use this ingredient primarily as a carrier for concentrated flavourings and colours. Many intense flavours, such as those used in sweets, baked goods, and chewing gum, are highly concentrated oils that do not disperse evenly on their own. By dissolving these flavours in glyceryl triacetate, manufacturers can distribute them evenly throughout a large batch of dough or confectionery. It also acts as a humectant, helping to keep foods moist and preventing them from drying out on the shelf.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this NEUTRAL, a benign additive. Once consumed, your body breaks it down into glycerol and acetate, two completely normal substances that your cells metabolise for energy just like the fats and acids in your regular diet. Because of this natural and efficient breakdown, regulators have found no reason to set a limit on how much can be consumed. EFSA re-evaluated the substance in 2023 and assigned an unspecified acceptable daily intake, meaning it is of no toxicological concern.

What the science says

The scientific consensus on this ingredient is exceptionally clear. Major food safety authorities have reviewed the biological data and concluded that it behaves exactly like dietary fat during digestion. The European Food Safety Authority completed a thorough re-evaluation in 2023 and confirmed there are no safety concerns, establishing that no numerical daily limit is required. In the United States, the Food and Drug Administration has long designated it as generally recognised as safe, with no open questions regarding its safety in food.

Where you'll find it

You will most commonly find this ingredient in highly flavoured processed foods, including chewing gum, hard sweets, baked goods, and some beverages. It is also used in food packaging and as a plasticiser in container linings. On a food label, it may be listed by its full chemical name, glyceryl triacetate, its common name, triacetin, or by its European code, E1518. On US labels, it is typically declared as triacetin or glyceryl triacetate.
