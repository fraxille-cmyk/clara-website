Taurine

IngredientNeutral

Diets

✓ Suits every diet Clara checks — as an ingredient

Taurine is a natural amino acid found in your body and in foods like meat and fish, most famous for its role as an ingredient in energy drinks.

What it is

Taurine is an organic compound known as an amino acid, which is a building block of proteins, though it is not used to build proteins in the same way other amino acids are. Instead, it floats freely in your tissues, especially in your brain, eyes, heart, and muscles. It is a natural part of the human body and is also found in animal foods like meat, seafood, and dairy. It plays a vital role in keeping your cells hydrated, balancing minerals, and supporting the nervous system.

How it's made

While taurine can be extracted from animal tissues, the vast majority of the taurine used in food and drinks today is made synthetically in a laboratory. This chemical synthesis typically involves reacting ethylene oxide with sodium bisulphite, or reacting monoethanolamine with sulphuric acid. This process creates a highly purified, crystalline white powder that is chemically identical to the taurine found naturally in your body, making the final ingredient suitable for vegetarians and vegans.

Why manufacturers use it

Manufacturers add taurine to products primarily for its reputation as a physical and mental performance enhancer. It is a signature ingredient in energy drinks and sports supplements, where it is used to support muscle function and hydration during exercise. Because it dissolves easily in water and has a relatively neutral taste that is easy to mask with flavours and sweeteners, it is highly convenient for liquid formulas. It is also added to infant formulas to mimic the natural composition of human breast milk.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this NEUTRAL. This is because taurine is a natural substance already present in the human body and in many common foods we eat every day. When consumed in normal dietary amounts, it is processed easily by the body. No upper limit is established for daily intake, though regulators suggest that the very high amounts found in some energy drinks deserve a mindful approach. It is not considered an additive in Clara's system, so it does not carry an additive risk tier.

What the science says

Scientific bodies have evaluated taurine to ensure its addition to foods and drinks is appropriate. EFSA published a scientific opinion concluding that exposure to taurine through energy drinks at typical consumption levels does not raise safety concerns. JECFA has also reviewed taurine in the context of infant formula and found it acceptable for supporting development. Researchers continue to study how high doses of taurine interact with caffeine in the body, but current evidence shows that typical dietary levels are handled safely by our natural metabolic pathways.

Where you'll find it

You will most commonly find taurine listed on the labels of energy drinks, sports nutrition powders, pre-workout supplements, and some vitamin-enhanced waters. It is also a standard ingredient in infant formulas and some specialised pet foods. On a packaging label, it is almost always listed simply as taurine, as it does not have an assigned E-number. It appears alongside other active ingredients like caffeine, B-vitamins, and glucuronolactone in popular functional beverages.

Sources
