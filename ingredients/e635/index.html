Disodium 5'-ribonucleotides (E635)

E635AdditiveNeutral · limited concern

Disodium 5'-ribonucleotides is a synthetic flavour enhancer used to boost savoury tastes, and regulators consider it a benign additive with no set daily limit.

What it is

Disodium 5'-ribonucleotides is a mixture of two sodium salts that naturally occur in many foods, specifically disodium guanylate and disodium inosinate. These compounds are nucleotides, which are the fundamental building blocks of genetic material in all living cells. When combined, they create a powerful synergy that enhances savoury flavours, mimicking the rich depth of slow-cooked broths and aged cheeses. It is closely related to monosodium glutamate, often working alongside it to multiply the overall perception of savoury taste on our tongues.

How it's made

To produce this ingredient on a commercial scale, manufacturers typically begin with starch or sugar from crops like corn or tapioca. Microorganisms ferment these sugars to produce the necessary nucleotides, which are then purified and combined with sodium to form stable salts. The final product is a white, crystalline powder that dissolves easily in water. While the raw materials are agricultural, the industrial fermentation and purification process makes this a synthetic additive rather than a simple food extract.

Why manufacturers use it

Manufacturers use this additive because it is an incredibly efficient flavour enhancer. It works by binding to the savoury taste receptors on our tongues, making them far more sensitive to other savoury compounds in the food. This means a manufacturer can achieve a deep, satisfying taste while using much less salt or expensive meat extracts. It is highly stable under heat and during long storage periods, making it a reliable and cost-effective choice for keeping packaged foods tasting fresh and savoury.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this NEUTRAL, a limited-concern additive. This rating reflects its low toxicity, and JECFA has not specified an upper limit for its daily intake because it is processed by the body in the same way as natural dietary nucleotides. Because it is broken down into purines, which the body metabolises into uric acid, some individuals who need to manage their uric acid levels choose to moderate their intake, though it is generally considered benign for the wider population.

What the science says

The safety of this ingredient has been thoroughly evaluated by major international food authorities. JECFA established that no daily limit is required, a decision reflecting its safety profile in animal and human studies. The European Food Safety Authority and the US Food and Drug Administration both permit its use in food, concluding that the small amounts consumed through seasoned snacks and processed foods pose no safety concerns for the general public.

Where you'll find it

You will commonly find this ingredient in highly savoury processed foods, such as instant noodles, potato crisps, seasoned crackers, canned soups, and pre-packaged sauces. On a label, it is usually listed by its full name, disodium 5'-ribonucleotides, or by its European code, E635. On US labels, it may sometimes be listed as a mixture of disodium guanylate and disodium inosinate, or simply as a flavour enhancer.
