Gelatine is a natural protein made from animal collagen, used to give foods a smooth, jelly-like texture.

What it is

Gelatine is a natural protein derived from the collagen found in animal bones, skins, and connective tissues. It is essentially a purified, cooked form of collagen, which is the most abundant protein in the animal kingdom. When you simmer meat on the bone and the cooled broth turns into a firm jelly, you are seeing gelatine at work. It is unique because it melts at body temperature, which gives it a distinct, melt-in-the-mouth quality that is difficult to replicate with plant-based alternatives.

How it's made

The production of gelatine begins with raw materials from the meat industry, typically pig skins, cattle hides, and bones. These materials undergo a thorough cleaning process followed by treatment with either an acid or an alkaline solution to break down the tough collagen fibres. Next, the treated material is heated in hot water to extract the soluble gelatine protein. This liquid is filtered, concentrated, sterilised, and dried into thin sheets, noodles, or a fine powder that is ready to be dissolved in warm liquids.

Why manufacturers use it

Food manufacturers rely on gelatine because it is an incredibly versatile texturiser. It acts as a gelling agent, thickener, and stabiliser all at once. In low-fat spreads, it mimics the rich mouthfeel of fat without adding extra calories. In confectionery, it provides a chewy, elastic texture that holds its shape at room temperature. It also stops ice crystals from growing in frozen desserts, keeps aerated mousses from collapsing, and clarifies cloudy fruit juices and vinegars by binding to tiny suspended particles so they can be filtered out.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this NEUTRAL. As a simple processed protein rather than a chemical additive, it is digested by the body just like any other dietary protein, breaking down into harmless amino acids. Because it is a natural food ingredient, no daily intake limit is established by food safety authorities. The main considerations for shoppers are dietary and ethical rather than chemical, as gelatine is always of animal origin and is therefore not suitable for vegetarians, vegans, or those following specific religious dietary laws.

What the science says

Regulatory bodies have thoroughly evaluated gelatine over many decades. Because it is a traditional food ingredient rather than a synthetic additive, it is not subject to the strict daily limits applied to artificial substances. The FDA classifies gelatine as Generally Recognised as Safe, and European regulators permit its use in foods without a specified maximum limit. Scientific assessments focus primarily on the purity of the sourcing and the manufacturing processes, ensuring that the raw materials are processed under strict hygienic conditions to guarantee a clean, high-protein final product.

Where you'll find it

You will find gelatine in a wide variety of household foods, especially gummy sweets, marshmallows, jelly desserts, and chewy candies. It is also common in low-fat yoghurts, sour cream, margarines, and canned meats. On a food label, it does not have an E-number because it is classified as a food ingredient rather than an additive. It is simply listed in the ingredient deck under its common name as gelatine, or occasionally as edible gelatine.
