What it is

Walnuts are the edible seeds of trees belonging to the Juglans genus, most commonly the Persian or English walnut tree. Inside a wrinkled, hard woody shell lies a rich, bumpy kernel that resembles a tiny brain. They have been eaten by humans for thousands of years and are prized for their earthy, slightly bitter flavour and satisfyingly oily texture. Unlike highly processed ingredients, a walnut is a complete, natural package of fats, proteins, and dietary fibre that comes straight from the orchard to the pantry.

How it's made

The journey of a walnut is remarkably simple and involves very little processing. After the green outer husks of the fallen fruit split open on the tree, growers shake the branches to harvest the nuts. The nuts are gathered, washed to remove dirt, and then carefully dried with warm air to reduce their moisture content, which prevents spoilage and keeps them stable. Depending on how they will be sold, mechanical shellers crack the hard outer hulls to release the walnut halves, which are then sorted by size and packaged.

Why manufacturers use it

Food producers choose walnuts because they offer a rich, buttery texture and a distinct nutty flavour that cannot be easily replicated by synthetic flavourings. They provide a natural crunch in baked goods, breakfast cereals, and snack bars, helping to build a satisfying texture. Because they are naturally packed with plant-based fats, they also help to keep products moist and extend their shelf life naturally. Using whole walnuts allows manufacturers to appeal to shoppers looking for simple, recognisable ingredients on the label.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this GOOD. This is a single-ingredient product that represents the whole food itself, meaning there are no industrial additives, artificial colours, or synthetic preservatives to worry about. For the vast majority of people, they are a highly nourishing addition to a balanced diet. However, because they are tree nuts, anyone with a confirmed nut allergy must avoid them entirely, as they can trigger severe allergic reactions. For everyone else, no upper limit of consumption is established by food safety authorities.

What the science says

Major food safety authorities have evaluated walnuts as a standard whole food rather than a food additive. The European Food Safety Authority has reviewed health claims for walnuts and confirmed that eating a moderate handful daily helps improve the elasticity of blood vessels, which supports healthy blood flow throughout the body. Because it is a whole agricultural product, no joint FAO and WHO expert committee has ever needed to set an acceptable daily intake, as it carries no toxicological concern when consumed as part of a normal diet.

Where you'll find it

You will find them in the baking aisle, snack section, and breakfast cereal bays, often listed simply as walnuts, walnut halves, or walnut pieces. In imported European products, they might appear under their continental names such as noix, noci, nueces, or walnüsse. They are also a frequent star ingredient in premium pestos, muesli mixes, artisan breads, and pre-packaged salads, where they are easily identified on the ingredient list without any associated E-numbers.
