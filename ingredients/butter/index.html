Butter

Processed ingredientNeutral

Butter is a traditional dairy fat made by churning cream, used to add rich flavour and texture to foods, and rated neutral by Clara.

What it is

Butter is a familiar kitchen staple made almost entirely of milk fat, water, and milk solids. It is a natural emulsion that has been part of the human diet for thousands of years, long before the advent of modern food processing. Unlike highly refined vegetable oils, butter retains its natural structure and contains small amounts of fat-soluble vitamins. It is prized for its rich, creamy mouthfeel and its distinctively pleasant aroma, which comes from natural compounds produced during the ripening of cream.

How it's made

The journey of butter begins with fresh cow milk, which is spun in a centrifuge to separate the lighter cream from the heavier skimmed milk. This cream is then pasteurised to ensure it is clean and stable. Next, the cream is aged and cooled to allow the fat crystals to form. The crucial step is churning, a mechanical shaking process that breaks the fat globule membranes, causing the tiny droplets of fat to clump together into butter grains. The liquid buttermilk is drained away, and the remaining butter grains are worked and kneaded together into a smooth, cohesive block.

Why manufacturers use it

Food manufacturers choose butter because it delivers an unmatched depth of flavour and a melt-in-the-mouth texture that artificial fats struggle to replicate. In baking, butter plays a vital structural role by shortening gluten strands, which creates a tender crumb in cakes and a delicate, flaky structure in pastries. It also acts as an excellent carrier for other flavours and helps to brown foods beautifully during cooking through natural caramelisation. While vegetable fats are often cheaper, butter remains the premium choice for high-quality biscuits, pastries, and sauces.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this NEUTRAL. Butter is a traditional food ingredient rather than a chemical additive, so it does not carry a risk tier or an artificial additive classification. It is a concentrated source of saturated fats, which means it is best enjoyed in moderation as part of a balanced diet. No regulatory upper limit is established for butter itself, as it is treated as a standard food ingredient rather than a controlled substance. It is a wholesome, minimally processed option for those who prefer simple, traditional ingredients over highly refined alternatives.

What the science says

Because butter is a traditional agricultural product, it is regulated as a standard food rather than an additive. Major food safety authorities, including the FDA and the European Commission, recognise butter as a standard dairy product with a long history of safe consumption. Scientific evaluations focus primarily on its fat profile, noting that while it is high in saturated fatty acids, it also contains short-chain and medium-chain fatty acids which the body metabolises differently. There are no active safety re-evaluations or chemical exposure limits associated with its normal culinary use.

Where you'll find it

You will find butter listed simply as butter on food labels, occasionally specified as salted butter or unsalted butter depending on the recipe. It is a key ingredient in premium baked goods, shortbread, croissants, ready meals, rich sauces, and confectionery. Because it is a whole food ingredient rather than a food additive, it does not have an E-number. It is often highlighted on packaging to signal quality, as manufacturers know shoppers appreciate seeing real butter instead of processed vegetable fats.
