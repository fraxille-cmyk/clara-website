Processed eucheuma seaweed (E407a)

E407aAdditiveAvoid · moderate concern

Diets

✓ Suits every diet Clara checks — as an ingredient

Clara rates this AVOID. It is a seaweed-derived thickener with a temporary safety limit due to outstanding regulatory questions about genetic safety.

What it is

Processed eucheuma seaweed is a plant-derived food additive used to thicken and stabilise foods. It is very closely related to carrageenan, as both are extracted from red seaweeds that grow in coastal waters. While carrageenan is highly refined to isolate the pure gelling fibres, this ingredient is a semi-refined version. It retains a higher amount of natural plant material, including insoluble cellulose from the original seaweed, making it a slightly less processed but functionally similar alternative to pure carrageenan.

How it's made

To make this ingredient, harvesters collect specific species of red seaweed, primarily from the genus Eucheuma. Instead of dissolving and filtering out the gelling agents as they do for refined carrageenan, processors wash the seaweed and treat it with a warm alkaline solution. This treatment alters the chemical structure of the seaweed fibres to improve their gelling properties without dissolving them. The seaweed is then washed again, dried, and ground into a fine, light-coloured powder that disperses easily in water.

Why manufacturers use it

Food manufacturers use this ingredient because it is an exceptionally efficient thickener and gelling agent. It binds water and proteins beautifully, which helps to create a smooth, uniform texture in dairy products and plant-based milks. It prevents ingredients from separating over time, ensuring a long shelf life. Because the manufacturing process is simpler and requires fewer steps than refining pure carrageenan, it is a highly cost-effective option for keeping liquids creamy and preventing water loss in processed meats.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this AVOID, a moderate-concern additive. While it is a natural extract, EFSA has raised questions about its long-term safety. EFSA considers its acceptable daily intake to be only temporary because there are outstanding uncertainties regarding its potential to cause genotoxicity, which refers to damage to cellular genetic material. Until manufacturers provide clearer data to resolve these safety questions, Clara advises choosing products without this additive. No permanent daily limit is established by European regulators at this time.

What the science says

European regulators have scrutinised this seaweed extract closely. In a major re-evaluation, the EFSA Journal scientific opinion of 2018 highlighted that the safety database for this additive has gaps, particularly concerning how it behaves in the gut and whether it can interact with genetic material. Because of these uncertainties, EFSA decided that the safety limit must remain temporary until new, robust studies are submitted. Meanwhile, the FDA permits its use under US regulations, but the European scientific community continues to call for more rigorous testing on its absorption and cellular effects.

Where you'll find it

You will frequently find this ingredient in dairy products like chocolate milk, ice cream, and cream, as well as in plant-based milk alternatives to keep them from separating. It is also common in processed meats, ready meals, and desserts. On ingredient lists, it is typically declared as processed eucheuma seaweed, e 407a, or E407a, and on US labels it is sometimes listed as processed Eucheuma seaweed under federal regulations.
