Canthaxanthin (E161g)

E161gAdditiveNeutral · limited concern

Diets

✓ Suits every diet Clara checks — as an ingredient

Canthaxanthin is a synthetic orange-red colour used in specific foods, with a strict daily intake limit because it can build up in the body.

What it is

Canthaxanthin is a deep orange-red pigment that belongs to the carotenoid family, the same group of natural compounds that gives carrots and autumn leaves their bright hues. While it occurs naturally in some wild mushrooms, green algae, and even the feathers of pink flamingos, the version you see listed on food labels is almost always made in a laboratory. It is highly soluble in fats and oils, which makes it particularly useful for colouring fatty foods that need an appealing, warm orange or reddish tone.

How it's made

To meet commercial demand, manufacturers synthesise canthaxanthin through a multi-step chemical process. It starts with smaller organic chemical building blocks, which are carefully coupled and built up in a laboratory to replicate the exact molecular structure of the natural pigment. This synthetic route ensures a highly pure, stable, and consistent colourant that does not rely on harvesting wild sources. The final product is typically formulated into a fine, free-flowing powder or a suspension in vegetable oil to make it easier to mix into food.

Why manufacturers use it

Food manufacturers use this additive to restore or enhance the warm, reddish-orange colour of foods that might otherwise look pale or unappealing after processing. It is a highly stable pigment, meaning it holds its colour well when exposed to heat, light, and changes in acidity during manufacturing and storage. Because it is fat-soluble, it distributes evenly through fats and oils without separating or bleeding, making it a reliable choice for processed meats and cheeses where water-soluble colours would fail.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this NEUTRAL, a limited-concern additive. This rating reflects its synthetic origin and the fact that it has a strict numeric acceptable daily intake of 0.03 mg per kg of body weight, which was established because high doses can lead to the pigment accumulating in the body, particularly in the retina of the eye. No toxicological concern is raised when consumption remains within this limit. The pigment is not classified by IARC, and regulators consider it safe for the general population when used within the restricted levels permitted in food.

What the science says

Scientific panels have thoroughly evaluated canthaxanthin to ensure it does not pose a risk to human health at normal dietary levels. EFSA re-evaluated the colourant and confirmed the acceptable daily intake of 0.03 mg per kg of body weight, noting that typical exposure from food is well below this threshold. The main scientific focus has been on how the body metabolises and stores the pigment, as very high doses from tanning pills have been shown to form temporary, harmless crystals in the eye, though this does not occur from the tiny amounts allowed in food.

Where you'll find it

You will find this colourant in a very limited range of processed foods. In Europe, it is authorised only for specific products like Strasbourg sausages and certain traditional cheeses. On labels, it is listed by its name, canthaxanthin, or as E161g. In the United States, where its use is restricted to a maximum of 30 mg per pound of solid or semi-solid food, it may appear on US labels simply as canthaxanthin, where it is often used in sauces, dressings, and some confectionery.
