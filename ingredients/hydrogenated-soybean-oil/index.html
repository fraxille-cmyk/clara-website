Hydrogenated soybean oil

Processed ingredientAvoid

Diets

✓ Suits every diet Clara checks — as an ingredient

Hydrogenated soybean oil is a chemically altered vegetable fat that Clara rates AVOID because the hardening process creates harmful trans fats.

What it is

Hydrogenated soybean oil is a vegetable oil that has been chemically altered to turn it from a liquid into a solid or semi-solid fat. It starts as ordinary oil pressed from soybeans, which is rich in unsaturated fats. By forcing hydrogen into the oil, chemists change its molecular structure, making it behave more like butter or lard. This process makes the oil much more stable and resistant to spoiling, but it also fundamentally changes how the fat behaves when you eat it.

How it's made

The journey begins with harvesting and crushing soybeans to extract their natural liquid oil. This oil is refined, bleached, and deodorised to create a neutral base. To harden it, the liquid oil is heated to high temperatures in a pressurised reactor. A metal catalyst, usually nickel, is introduced, and hydrogen gas is pumped through the mixture. This chemical reaction alters the carbon bonds in the fat. If the process is stopped halfway, it produces partially hydrogenated oil, which is the primary source of artificial trans fats.

Why manufacturers use it

Food manufacturers favour this modified oil because it is incredibly cheap, highly stable, and versatile. It has a much longer shelf life than natural liquid oils and does not go rancid easily, which helps packaged foods stay fresh on supermarket shelves for months. It also provides a smooth, creamy texture and a high melting point, which is perfect for creating flaky pastries, spreadable margarines, and crispy fried foods. It is a highly cost-effective alternative to expensive animal fats like butter.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this AVOID. The industrial process used to harden this oil creates trans fatty acids, which are highly detrimental to human health. When we consume these artificial fats, they alter the balance of cholesterol in the blood, raising the fats that clog arteries and lowering the protective ones. No level of intake is considered beneficial for the body, and major food authorities have worked to eliminate these industrial trans fats from the food supply entirely due to their direct impact on blood vessels.

What the science says

Global health authorities have evaluated hydrogenated fats extensively and agree on their risks. The WHO has launched global initiatives to eliminate industrially produced trans fatty acids from the food supply, noting their severe impact on cardiovascular health. EFSA and the FDA have both conducted thorough reviews, concluding that intake of trans fats should be as low as possible within a nutritionally adequate diet. Because even small amounts can negatively affect blood lipids, regulators in many regions have banned or strictly limited these fats in consumer products.

Where you'll find it

You will find this ingredient in a wide variety of highly processed foods, including commercial biscuits, cakes, pie crusts, margarines, vegetable shortenings, and packaged microwave popcorn. It is also common in frozen pizzas, non-dairy coffee creamers, and fast-food frying batters. On a food label, it is typically listed plainly as hydrogenated soybean oil, partially hydrogenated soybean oil, or hydrogenated vegetable fat, and it does not carry an E number.
