Artificial flavourings

FlavouringNeutral · moderate concern

Artificial flavourings are chemically synthesised compounds used to mimic natural tastes, serving as a common marker of ultra-processed foods.

What it is

Artificial flavourings are man-made chemical compounds designed to mimic the taste and smell of natural foods. Unlike natural flavours, which must originate from a plant or animal source, these substances are entirely synthesised in a laboratory. They are designed to target our senses of taste and smell with extreme precision, often recreating complex profiles like ripe strawberry or roasted beef using a combination of simple, highly concentrated molecules. While they are chemically identical to or closely resemble molecules found in nature, their origin is entirely industrial.

How it's made

These flavourings are created through chemical synthesis in industrial laboratories. Chemists identify the specific volatile molecules that give natural foods their distinctive aroma and taste. They then synthesise these target molecules from raw chemical materials, which are often derived from petroleum or coal tar. The process involves precise chemical reactions, purification steps, and blending. The final ingredient is a highly concentrated liquid or powder containing a mixture of these synthetic compounds, often carried in a solvent like propylene glycol or vegetable oil to make it easy to mix into food.

Why manufacturers use it

Food manufacturers choose artificial flavourings because they are incredibly cheap, highly stable, and remarkably consistent. Natural flavours can vary wildly depending on the harvest, weather, and soil, and they often degrade quickly during high-heat factory processing. Synthetic alternatives do not fade over time, ensuring a packet of crisps or a bottle of fizzy drink tastes exactly the same whether it is opened tomorrow or next year. They also allow companies to create intense, appealing taste profiles in highly processed ingredients that would otherwise taste bland or unappealing.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this NEUTRAL, a moderate-concern additive. While these synthetic compounds are permitted for use in food, their presence is a clear marker of an ultra-processed product. Because manufacturers are allowed to declare these ingredients under broad terms like artificial flavouring without listing the individual chemical components, it is impossible for a shopper to know exactly which synthetic molecules they are consuming. No daily intake limit is established for the general category of artificial flavours, as each individual chemical compound is assessed separately by regulators.

What the science says

Major food safety authorities have evaluated the individual chemical compounds that make up these mixtures. The JECFA and the EFSA systematically review synthetic flavouring substances to ensure they do not accumulate in the body or cause cellular damage. While the individual molecules are deemed safe at the tiny concentrations used in food, some consumer advocates point out that these intense flavours can alter our taste preferences, making whole foods seem less appealing. No regulatory body has established a collective upper limit for the blended mixtures themselves.

Where you'll find it

You will find these ingredients in a vast range of ultra-processed foods, including fizzy drinks, sweets, baked goods, cereals, and savoury snacks. On packaging, they are often listed under aliases such as artificial flavouring, artificial flavours, artificial and natural flavourings, or in European products as arôme artificiel, aroma artificiale, and aromas artificiales. Because they do not have a specific E-number, they are always declared by these descriptive names on the ingredient list.
