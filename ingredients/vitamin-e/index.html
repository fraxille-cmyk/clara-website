Vitamin E

FortificantGood

Vitamin E is an essential fat-soluble nutrient added to foods to boost their nutritional value and naturally protect oils from spoiling.

What it is

Vitamin E is a vital nutrient that our bodies cannot make on our own, meaning we must get it from our diet. In the physical world, it is a family of fat-soluble compounds, with alpha-tocopherol being the form our bodies use most effectively. It is naturally abundant in vegetable oils, nuts, seeds, and green leafy vegetables. When you see it on a food label, it is acting either as a nutrient to fortify the food or as a natural shield to protect the product from the damaging effects of oxygen.

How it's made

To meet the demands of food production, vitamin E is obtained in two main ways. The natural form is typically extracted and purified from vegetable oils, such as soybean, rapeseed, or sunflower oil, through a process of distillation. The synthetic form is built in a laboratory by combining chemical compounds to mirror the structure found in nature. Both forms are highly refined to ensure purity, resulting in a clear, viscous, amber-coloured oil that is ready to be blended into various food products.

Why manufacturers use it

Food makers choose vitamin E for two excellent reasons. First, as a fortificant, it restores or enhances the nutritional profile of a food, helping shoppers meet their daily dietary requirements. Second, it is a highly effective natural antioxidant. Fats and oils in packaged foods easily react with oxygen over time, which makes them go rancid and smell unpleasant. Adding vitamin E stops this oxidation process, keeping the food tasting fresh and extending its shelf life without the need for synthetic chemical preservatives.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this GOOD. As an essential nutrient, it is a highly beneficial addition to your diet rather than an additive to watch out for. The body relies on its antioxidant properties to protect cells from everyday oxidative stress. Because it is fat-soluble, the body stores what it does not use immediately, but the levels found in fortified foods are well within normal, healthy limits. There is no reason to limit your intake of this nutrient from food sources, as it supports overall biological function.

What the science says

Regulators worldwide have thoroughly evaluated vitamin E and recognise its safety and nutritional importance. The European Food Safety Authority has established dietary reference values for vitamin E to help guide daily intake for optimal health. Likewise, the US Food and Drug Administration permits its use as both a nutrient supplement and a preservative under established good manufacturing practices. Scientific consensus confirms that it is highly bioavailable, meaning our bodies easily absorb and utilise it to support cellular health and maintain immune function.

Where you'll find it

You will frequently spot vitamin E in fat-based foods that are prone to oxidation, such as margarines, spreads, salad dressings, and vegetable oils. It is also a common addition to breakfast cereals, plant-based milk alternatives, baby foods, and meal replacement shakes, where it serves to boost the overall vitamin content. On packaging, it is most often declared simply as vitamin E, tocopherol, mixed tocopherols, d-alpha-tocopherol, or tocopheryl acetate, making it easy to identify when you are scanning the ingredients list in the supermarket aisle.
