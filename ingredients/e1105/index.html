Lysozyme (E1105)

E1105AdditiveAvoid · moderate concern

Diets

✕ Not vegan
  • Not vegan — lysozyme comes from egg.

Lysozyme is an egg-derived enzyme used to preserve cheese, but Clara rates it AVOID because it can trigger allergic reactions in egg-sensitive shoppers.

What it is

Lysozyme is a naturally occurring enzyme that acts as a gentle, targeted defender against bacteria. In nature, it is found in abundance in chicken egg whites, as well as in human tears, saliva, and milk, where its job is to break down the protective outer walls of certain bacterial cells. When you see it on a food label, it is serving this same protective role, functioning as a natural preservative to keep food fresh and stable.

How it's made

To make the ingredient used in food, manufacturers extract the enzyme directly from hen egg whites. The egg whites are separated, and the liquid is passed through a special filtration process that binds the specific enzyme proteins while letting the rest of the egg white pass through. Once captured, the enzyme is washed, purified, and dried into a fine white powder or dissolved into a liquid concentrate, ready to be added to food without any chemical modification.

Why manufacturers use it

Food producers rely on this enzyme primarily in cheese making to stop a specific type of bacterial fermentation that causes gas bubbles to form inside the cheese. This gas can cause cheeses like Gouda or Edam to split, crack, or develop unpleasant off-flavours during their long ripening process. By using this natural enzyme, cheesemakers can prevent these structural defects and ensure a uniform texture and taste without relying on synthetic chemical preservatives.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this AVOID, a moderate-concern additive. While the enzyme itself is a natural protein, EFSA warns that exposure to lysozyme from its use as a food additive may trigger allergic reactions in egg-allergic individuals. Because it is purified directly from chicken eggs, even tiny residual amounts can cause a reaction in sensitive people. For anyone with an egg allergy or sensitivity, this additive presents a real risk and is best avoided.

What the science says

Regulators have thoroughly evaluated this enzyme and found no general safety concerns for the wider population. EFSA reviewed the substance and determined that no numerical daily limit is established, as it is processed by the body like any other dietary protein. However, scientific panels emphasise that its allergenic potential is identical to that of whole eggs, meaning it remains an active allergen even after food processing. The FDA has also evaluated its safety, accepting its status as generally recognised as safe for specific preservation uses.

Where you'll find it

You will most commonly find this ingredient in ripened cheeses, particularly semi-hard and hard European varieties, as well as in some beers and wines where it helps control bacterial growth during fermentation. On ingredient lists, it is typically declared by its plain name as lysozyme, or by its European code E1105. Because it is derived from eggs, labels in many regions must also carry a clear allergen warning indicating that it contains egg.
