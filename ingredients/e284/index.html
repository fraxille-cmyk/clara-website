Boric acid (E284)

E284AdditiveNeutral · limited concern

Boric acid is a mineral preservative used strictly to protect sturgeon caviar from spoiling, rated neutral by Clara due to its highly limited use.

What it is

Boric acid is a naturally occurring mineral compound containing boron, hydrogen, and oxygen. It is found in volcanic areas, hot springs, and various mineral deposits around the world. While many people associate it with household uses, it has a long history in food preservation, where it behaves as a mild acid. In the kitchen, it is a highly specialised substance that you will not find in everyday pantry items, as its modern food role is restricted to a single luxury delicacy.

How it's made

The production of this ingredient begins with boron-rich minerals mined from the earth, such as borax or colemanite. These raw minerals are dissolved in water and treated with a strong mineral acid, typically sulphuric acid. This chemical reaction causes boric acid to crystallise out of the liquid mixture. The crystals are then separated, thoroughly washed with water to remove impurities, and dried into a fine white powder that is ready for precise measurement in food preparation.

Why manufacturers use it

Manufacturers use this mineral preservative because it is exceptionally good at stopping the growth of yeasts and moulds in delicate seafood. Sturgeon caviar is highly sensitive to temperature and microbial spoilage, and traditional pasteurisation can ruin its delicate texture. This substance preserves the firm pop and fresh flavour of the eggs without requiring high heat. It is a traditional choice in the caviar industry because it extends shelf life while keeping the premium sensory qualities of the product intact.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this NEUTRAL, a limited-concern additive. This rating reflects its highly restricted use, as it is only permitted in sturgeon caviar, meaning the average shopper consumes almost none of it. EFSA has established a strict acceptable daily intake of 0.16 mg per kg of body weight. While some sources claim the substance is banned in the United States, a search of official regulations shows no confirmed FDA ban in the prohibited substances list, though it is not widely used there.

What the science says

Scientific assessments show that boron is an element the body processes and excretes through urine. European regulators have evaluated the substance thoroughly to ensure that the tiny amounts consumed from caviar do not accumulate to levels that could interfere with mineral balance or reproductive health. EFSA concluded in its re-evaluation that the current restricted use does not pose a safety concern for consumers because exposure remains well below the established daily limit. No open safety concerns are currently flagged by European authorities.

Where you'll find it

You will find this ingredient almost exclusively on the labels of high-end sturgeon caviar. It is not permitted in any other food category in the European Union. On a ingredient list, it will be declared plainly as boric acid or by its European code, E284. Because of its highly specific and limited application, you will never see it listed on everyday groceries, canned goods, or standard seafood products in the supermarket aisle.
