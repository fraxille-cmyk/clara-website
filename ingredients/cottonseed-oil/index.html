Cottonseed oil

Processed ingredientAvoid

Diets

✓ Suits every diet Clara checks — as an ingredient

Cottonseed oil is a highly refined vegetable oil that Clara rates AVOID due to its heavy chemical processing and the presence of natural plant toxins.

What it is

Cottonseed oil is a vegetable oil extracted from the seeds of cotton plants. While we mainly associate cotton with clothing and textiles, the seeds left behind during harvesting are rich in oil. This oil has a neutral flavour and a high smoke point, which historically made it a popular choice for frying and baking. However, unlike olives or sunflower seeds, cotton is not a food crop by nature, meaning the raw seeds contain natural defence chemicals that must be aggressively removed before the oil is fit for human consumption.

How it's made

The journey from cotton field to kitchen ingredient is highly industrial. After the cotton fibres are separated for textiles, the remaining seeds are dehulled and crushed. Because the oil does not release easily, factories use chemical solvents, typically hexane, to extract the maximum amount of fat. The crude oil is then subjected to an intense refining process involving alkali washing, bleaching, and deodorisation. This heavy processing is essential to strip away gossypol, a natural toxin found in the cotton plant that protects it from insects.

Why manufacturers use it

Food manufacturers choose cottonseed oil primarily because it is inexpensive and highly stable. It resists oxidation, which helps packaged foods stay fresh on shelves for longer periods without developing off-flavours. Its neutral taste means it does not interfere with the intended flavour of snacks, and its high smoke point makes it ideal for industrial deep-frying. For many years, it was a default choice for creating solid fats through hydrogenation, helping to give margarines and shortenings a smooth, spreadable texture.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this AVOID. While the refining process is designed to clear out the natural toxin gossypol, trace amounts can sometimes remain, and the heavy chemical solvents used during extraction raise concerns about the overall purity of the fat. Cotton crops are also heavily treated with agricultural chemicals compared to traditional food crops, meaning the raw material starts with a higher chemical load. Because of these intensive processing requirements and the potential for chemical residues, Clara advises choosing simpler, less processed oils instead.

What the science says

Regulators have evaluated cottonseed oil to establish limits on gossypol residues in food products. JECFA has assessed the safety of cottonseed flour and oil, focusing on the efficiency of industrial refining in eliminating these natural toxins. While standard refining processes generally succeed in reducing gossypol to levels considered acceptable by authorities, some researchers remain cautious about the cumulative effects of consuming highly processed industrial oils. No daily intake limit is established for the refined oil itself, but the reliance on chemical solvents during its manufacture keeps it under scrutiny.

Where you'll find it

You will find cottonseed oil in a wide variety of processed foods, particularly fried snacks like potato crisps, corn chips, and crackers. It is also a common ingredient in packaged baked goods, margarines, salad dressings, and pre-made baking mixes. On food labels, it is typically declared plainly as cottonseed oil, though it can sometimes be grouped under the generic term vegetable oil or hydrogenated vegetable oil in regions where detailed oil sources do not have to be declared individually.
