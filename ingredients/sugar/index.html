Sugar

Processed ingredientAvoid

Diets

✓ Suits every diet Clara checks — as an ingredient

A highly refined carbohydrate that adds intense sweetness and empty calories, which Clara advises avoiding to keep blood sugar levels stable.

What it is

Sugar is a simple carbohydrate known scientifically as sucrose. It is a natural compound found in the tissues of most plants, where it acts as an energy store. It is most concentrated in sugar cane and sugar beet, which are the sources of almost all the white crystals we buy. To our bodies, it is a rapid source of energy, made of equal parts glucose and fructose bonded together. It has been a central part of the human diet for centuries, though in much smaller quantities than we consume today.

How it's made

The journey begins with harvesting sugar cane or sugar beet, which are then crushed or sliced to extract their sweet juices. This raw juice is mixed with lime to remove impurities and boiled under a vacuum to concentrate it into a thick syrup. As the syrup cools, tiny seed crystals are added to encourage sugar crystals to form. A centrifuge spins the mixture at high speed to separate these crystals from the dark molasses. The resulting raw crystals are washed, dissolved, filtered through carbon, and dried into the familiar white granules. This crystallisation process yields a highly purified substance.

Why manufacturers use it

Food companies use sugar primarily for its clean, sweet flavour, but it also serves vital structural roles. It acts as a preservative by drawing water out of spoilage microbes, keeping food fresh for longer. In baking, it helps yeast rise, tenderises the crumb by slowing down gluten formation, and holds onto moisture so cakes do not dry out. It also caramelises under heat, giving baked goods their golden colour and rich aroma. No single alternative can easily replicate all of these physical properties at such a low cost.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this AVOID. It is a refined carbohydrate that provides empty calories and contributes to high blood sugar levels. While it is not a chemical additive, its presence in large amounts is something to watch closely. The WHO guidelines on sugars recommend limiting free sugars to less than ten percent of total daily energy intake for better health. Because it is absorbed so rapidly by the body, consuming it in large quantities can place a heavy burden on our metabolic systems over time.

What the science says

Major health bodies have evaluated sugar extensively. The WHO guidelines on free sugars recommend a reduced intake throughout the life course based on evidence linking high consumption to excess weight gain. JECFA and the FDA recognise sugar as a standard food ingredient rather than an additive, meaning no specific acceptable daily intake limit is established in the way it is for artificial sweeteners. Instead, scientific consensus focuses on the systemic effects of rapid glucose absorption, which can disrupt how the body manages energy and insulin levels over the long term.

Where you'll find it

You will find sugar in almost every category of processed food, from sweet biscuits, cakes, and fizzy drinks to savoury sauces, ready meals, and bread. On ingredient lists, it is often declared simply as sugar, but it may also appear under its French and Dutch names, sucre or suiker, depending on where the product was packaged. It does not have an E-number because it is classified as a food ingredient rather than a food additive.
