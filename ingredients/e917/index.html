Potassium iodate (E917)

E917FortificantNeutral · benign

Diets

✓ Suits every diet Clara checks — as an ingredient

Potassium iodate is a source of iodine used to fortify table salt and support thyroid function.

What it is

Potassium iodate is an inorganic chemical compound made of potassium, iodine, and oxygen. It is a stable, white crystalline powder that serves as a source of iodine, an essential micronutrient that our bodies cannot make on their own. Iodine is a crucial building block for the hormones produced by the thyroid gland, which regulate our metabolism, body temperature, and overall growth. Because it is highly stable even in hot and humid climates, this compound is widely used to ensure people get enough iodine through their daily diet.

How it's made

To manufacture potassium iodate, producers typically start with elemental iodine, which is mined from natural underground brine deposits or extracted from seaweed. This iodine is reacted with potassium hydroxide, an alkaline compound. The chemical reaction oxidises the iodine, binding it with potassium and oxygen to form potassium iodate crystals. These crystals are then filtered, washed, dried, and ground into a fine powder. This industrial process ensures a highly pure substance that does not degrade easily when stored on kitchen shelves.

Why manufacturers use it

Food manufacturers use potassium iodate primarily to fortify table salt and bakery products. Iodine is highly volatile and can easily evaporate from salt when exposed to air, moisture, or heat during storage and cooking. Potassium iodate is much more stable than other iodine sources, such as potassium iodide, meaning the iodine stays in the salt for a much longer time. This stability makes it the preferred choice for food producers in warm or humid regions, ensuring the food retains its nutritional value from the factory to the dinner table.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this NEUTRAL, a benign additive. It is used as an iodine fortification agent in iodised salt to support thyroid function at trace amounts. Because our bodies require only tiny quantities of iodine to function properly, the amount of potassium iodate added to food is exceptionally small. While consuming massive quantities of pure potassium iodate would be unsafe, the trace amounts encountered in fortified table salt are considered entirely appropriate for daily consumption. No daily intake limit is established for potassium iodate itself, though regulators set guidelines for total daily iodine intake.

What the science says

International food safety authorities have thoroughly evaluated potassium iodate as a source of dietary iodine. JECFA, the joint expert committee of the FAO and WHO, has assessed its use in salt iodisation and concluded that it is an effective and suitable substance for preventing iodine deficiency. The committee notes that once ingested, the body easily converts iodate into iodide, which is then absorbed and used by the thyroid gland. Because it is treated as a nutritional supplement rather than a typical food additive, it is regulated under fortification guidelines rather than standard additive lists in many regions.

Where you'll find it

You will primarily find this ingredient in fortified table salt, iodised salt, and occasionally in commercial bread doughs where it acts as a dough conditioner. On ingredient labels, it may be declared by its full name, potassium iodate, or under its various international names such as kaliumiodat, iodato de potasio, iodato di potassio, or yodato de potasio. In some older regulatory systems, it was associated with the code E917, though it is now primarily managed under nutritional fortification standards rather than standard additive lists.
