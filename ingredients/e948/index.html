Oxygen (E948)

E948AdditiveNeutral · limited concern

Oxygen is a natural gas used in packaging to keep fresh meat looking bright red, and Clara rates it as a neutral, limited-concern additive.

What it is

Oxygen is the very same gas that makes up about a fifth of the air we breathe every day. In the food industry, it is treated as a packaging gas rather than an ingredient you eat. It is introduced into the sealed space around fresh foods to control the atmosphere inside the packet. While we associate oxygen with life and breathing, its presence in food packaging is carefully managed to achieve specific visual and preservation effects before you open the packet at home.

How it's made

Although oxygen is abundant in the atmosphere, the gas used for food packaging is produced synthetically on an industrial scale. Manufacturers cool ordinary air down to extremely low temperatures until it liquefies. Through a process called fractional distillation, the liquid air is gently warmed in stages, allowing different gases to boil off and separate at their specific boiling points. The pure oxygen gas is then captured, compressed, and stored in cylinders, ready to be used by food packagers.

Why manufacturers use it

Food manufacturers use oxygen primarily to maintain the bright red colour of fresh red meat, such as beef. When meat is exposed to high levels of oxygen, a protein called myoglobin binds with it to form oxymyoglobin, which has an appealing cherry-red hue. Without this gas, the meat would quickly turn a dull, unappetising brown, even though it remains perfectly fine to eat. Using oxygen helps products look fresh on supermarket shelves, which encourages shoppers to buy them.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this NEUTRAL, a limited-concern additive. Because we breathe it constantly and our bodies are entirely adapted to it, oxygen in food packaging does not pose a risk to our health. No limit is established for its daily intake because it is not considered to have any adverse effects when used in this way. EFSA re-evaluated the gas and confirmed that its use in packaging is of no safety concern, meaning there is no need to set a numerical acceptable daily limit.

What the science says

Regulators have thoroughly assessed oxygen and found no reason for concern. The European Food Safety Authority re-evaluated it as a food additive and concluded that it is entirely safe for its intended uses, maintaining its status without a specified acceptable daily intake. In the United States, the Food and Drug Administration affirms it as generally recognised as safe. Because it is a natural component of the air we breathe, scientific evaluations focus on its purity as a packaging gas rather than any potential for harm.

Where you'll find it

You will find oxygen used in the modified atmosphere packaging of fresh meats, minced beef, and some poultry products. Because it is a packaging gas that escapes into the air the moment you open the packet, it does not remain in the food itself. On ingredient lists, it is typically declared by its function and name or its European code, appearing as packaging gas oxygen or simply E948, while on US labels it is listed as oxygen.
