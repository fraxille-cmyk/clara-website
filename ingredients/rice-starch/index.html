Rice starch

Processed ingredientNeutral

Diets

✓ Suits every diet Clara checks — as an ingredient

Rice starch is a natural carbohydrate extracted from rice grains, used as a gentle thickener and texturiser in many packaged foods.

What it is

Rice starch is the primary carbohydrate found in rice grains. It is a white, odourless powder made up of tiny starch granules, much smaller than those found in potato or corn starch. This small granule size gives it a unique, creamy texture when mixed with liquids, mimicking the mouthfeel of fats without adding extra oil. It is a familiar, plant-based ingredient that behaves much like the starches you might use in your own kitchen to thicken a sauce or a gravy.

How it's made

To make rice starch, whole or broken rice grains are first cleaned and steeped in a mild alkaline solution to soften them and help separate the starch from the natural proteins. The softened grains are then wet-milled into a fine slurry. Centrifuges spin this slurry to separate the heavy starch granules from the lighter proteins and fibres. Once separated, the starch is thoroughly washed with clean water to remove any remaining alkaline solution, dried into a fine powder, and packaged.

Why manufacturers use it

Food manufacturers choose rice starch because it is an excellent thickener and stabiliser that performs well under heat and shear. Its exceptionally small granule size allows it to create a smooth, creamy texture in low-fat products like yoghurts and desserts. It also has a neutral flavour, meaning it does not mask the delicate tastes of the food itself. Additionally, it remains stable when frozen and thawed, keeping sauces and ready meals from separating or becoming watery during storage.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this NEUTRAL. As a processed carbohydrate, it is a benign ingredient that the body digests just like the starch in a bowl of cooked rice. It is not an additive, so no daily limit is established for its consumption. It is widely tolerated and does not carry the safety concerns associated with synthetic chemical additives. Because it is naturally gluten-free, it is also a popular choice for thickening foods intended for people who need to avoid gluten.

What the science says

Regulators view rice starch as a standard food ingredient rather than a food additive. The FDA categorises it as a generally recognised as safe food substance that can be used without specific limitations other than good manufacturing practice. Because it is a simple carbohydrate, scientific evaluations focus on its nutritional value as a source of energy. It is broken down by enzymes in the digestive tract into glucose, which the body uses for fuel, behaving identically to the starch consumed from whole grains.

Where you'll find it

You will find rice starch in a wide variety of supermarket items, including baby foods, creamy soups, low-fat yoghurts, ready-made sauces, and gluten-free baked goods. On ingredient labels, it is typically declared simply as rice starch. Unlike chemical additives, it does not have an E-number because it is classified as a food ingredient rather than a food additive, making it a common feature in products aiming for a simpler, more recognisable ingredient list.
