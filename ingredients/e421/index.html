Mannitol (E421)

E421SweetenerAvoid · moderate concern

A sugar alcohol used as a low-calorie sweetener and texturiser that requires a mandatory EU warning because excessive consumption can cause laxative effects.

What it is

Mannitol is a sugar alcohol, or polyol, which is a type of carbohydrate that occurs naturally in many plants, including seaweed, mushrooms, and celery. It has a sweet taste but contains fewer calories than regular sugar, and it does not behave the same way as sugar in the body. It looks like a white, crystalline powder and dissolves easily in water, providing a cool sensation on the tongue when it melts.

How it's made

Although it exists in nature, commercial mannitol is typically manufactured on an industrial scale. It is produced by the chemical hydrogenation of fructose, a sugar often sourced from corn starch. During this process, hydrogen gas is added to a fructose solution under high pressure in the presence of a nickel catalyst. The resulting mixture is then filtered, purified, and crystallised to yield a highly pure powder.

Why manufacturers use it

Food manufacturers use it because it provides a clean, sweet flavour with about half the calories of sucrose. It does not absorb moisture easily, which makes it an excellent dusting agent for chewing gum and hard sweets to prevent them from sticking to their wrappers. It also adds bulk and texture to sugar-free products, helping them mimic the mouthfeel of traditional sugary foods while remaining stable during storage.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this AVOID, a moderate-concern additive. While it is not a chemical poison, consuming it in larger quantities can lead to digestive discomfort, bloating, and gas. Because of these digestive issues, the European Union mandates a warning label on foods containing more than ten per cent added polyols, stating that excessive consumption may produce laxative effects. For this reason, Clara advises caution when choosing foods that rely heavily on this sweetener.

What the science says

Regulators have evaluated this substance thoroughly and find no concerns regarding cellular changes or long-term accumulation in the body. EFSA re-evaluated it and confirmed that no specific daily limit is established, meaning it is considered of low risk at typical dietary levels. The FDA also permits its use as a safe food ingredient under specific limits. However, because the human small intestine does not fully absorb sugar alcohols, they pass into the large intestine where they draw in water and are fermented by gut bacteria, which explains the well-documented digestive symptoms.

Where you'll find it

You will find it most commonly in sugar-free chewing gum, breath mints, hard candies, chocolate, and various dietary supplements or chewable vitamins. On ingredient lists, it is typically declared by its plain name as mannitol, or by its European code, E421. On US labels, it is simply listed as mannitol, often accompanied by a voluntary or mandatory warning about its potential laxative effects depending on the concentration in the product.
