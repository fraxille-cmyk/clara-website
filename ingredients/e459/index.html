Beta-cyclodextrin (E459)

E459AdditiveNeutral · limited concern

Beta-cyclodextrin is a starch-derived carrier used to protect delicate flavours and colours, with a daily limit of 5 mg per kg of body weight.

What it is

Beta-cyclodextrin is a specialised carbohydrate made of seven sugar molecules joined together in a ring. This unique ring shape resembles a tiny bucket, which can hold other molecules inside it. It is a natural derivative of starch, sharing a close family connection with the ordinary starches we eat every day, but its structured shape gives it very different properties. In the food world, it acts as a protective carrier, shielding sensitive ingredients from air, light, and heat so they do not degrade before you eat them.

How it's made

To make this ingredient, manufacturers start with ordinary starch, usually sourced from corn or potatoes. They mix this starch with a specific enzyme produced by a bacterium called Bacillus amycolatofaciens. This enzyme acts like a pair of molecular scissors, cutting the long starch chains and curling them into neat, seven-membered rings. The mixture is then purified, crystallised, and dried into a white, odourless powder. Although bacteria are used to shape the molecules, the final product is a pure carbohydrate and contains no living organisms.

Why manufacturers use it

Food makers use beta-cyclodextrin because it is an exceptional protector of delicate ingredients. Volatile flavour oils, natural colours, and vitamins can easily break down or evaporate during food processing and storage. By trapping these sensitive compounds inside its molecular bucket, beta-cyclodextrin keeps them stable and extends their shelf life. It also helps turn liquid flavours into stable powders, masks bitter tastes, and prevents unwanted reactions between different ingredients in the same package, all at a very low cost compared to other encapsulation methods.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this NEUTRAL, a limited-concern additive. It is not absorbed well by the body, and the vast majority passes through to the large intestine where resident microbes break it down safely into simple sugars. EFSA has established a numeric acceptable daily intake of 5 mg per kg of body weight. Because it is used in very small quantities to carry flavours and colours, typical dietary exposure remains well below this limit. There are no reports of adverse effects from the amounts normally consumed in food.

What the science says

Scientific bodies have thoroughly evaluated beta-cyclodextrin and found no safety concerns at normal dietary levels. EFSA re-evaluated the additive in 2016 and confirmed its daily limit of 5 mg per kg of body weight, noting that it does not accumulate in the body. The US FDA has also reviewed its safety, filing it under its GRAS notification system. Research shows that it behaves much like other digestible carbohydrates once it reaches the colon, where it is fermented into short-chain fatty acids by beneficial gut bacteria.

Where you'll find it

You will find this ingredient in a variety of processed foods that rely on stable, long-lasting flavours and colours, such as chewing gum, instant teas, flavoured powders, and certain baked goods. On ingredient lists, it is typically declared by its plain name as beta-cyclodextrin, or by its European code E459. It is also used in some dietary supplements to help the body absorb fat-soluble vitamins more efficiently.
