Sodium carbonates (E500)

E500AdditiveNeutral · benign

A common raising agent and acidity regulator, better known as baking soda, that is entirely benign at the levels used in our food.

What it is

Sodium carbonates are a family of closely related mineral salts that include sodium carbonate, sodium hydrogen carbonate, and sodium sesquicarbonate. The most famous member of this family is sodium hydrogen carbonate, which you probably know as simple baking soda. These white, water-soluble powders are natural alkaline compounds that play a fundamental role in both traditional home baking and modern food preparation, helping to control acidity and create a light texture in baked goods.

How it's made

While these minerals occur naturally in salt lakes and mineral deposits, the sodium carbonates used in food are typically manufactured on a large scale. This is done using the Solvay process, which mixes salt brine, ammonia, and carbon dioxide to yield high-purity sodium hydrogen carbonate. Heating this powder can then convert it into sodium carbonate. The resulting crystals are washed, dried, and milled into a fine, uniform powder that is ready for food production.

Why manufacturers use it

Manufacturers rely on these compounds primarily as raising agents. When heated or mixed with an acid, they release carbon dioxide gas, which creates tiny bubbles that expand and lift doughs and batters. This gives cakes, biscuits, and crackers their light, airy texture. They also serve as acidity regulators, balancing the pH of foods to stabilise flavours, and as anti-caking agents that keep powdered mixes flowing smoothly without clumping during storage.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this NEUTRAL, a benign additive. There is no known mechanism of harm associated with these mineral salts at the levels typically found in packaged foods. Because our bodies naturally use sodium and carbonate ions to maintain a healthy acid-base balance, these substances are processed easily and safely. The only real consideration is that they contribute to the overall sodium content of a product, which some shoppers may wish to monitor for general dietary reasons.

What the science says

Both European and international food safety authorities have thoroughly evaluated these compounds and found no safety concerns. Because they are simple mineral salts that break down into components naturally present in the human body, regulators have not found any reason to set a restrictive daily limit. EFSA re-evaluated sodium carbonates and confirmed that they do not pose a safety risk at standard dietary levels, meaning no numerical acceptable daily intake is required.

Where you'll find it

You will find these ingredients in a wide variety of baked goods, including biscuits, crackers, cakes, and self-raising flours, as well as in instant noodle packets, cocoa products, and powdered soups. On the packaging, they may be listed under their specific names like sodium bicarbonate, sodium hydrogen carbonate, or sodium carbonate, or simply grouped under the collective name sodium carbonates or the code E 500.
