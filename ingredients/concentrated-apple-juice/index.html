Concentrated apple juice

Processed ingredientNeutral

Diets

✕ Not low-FODMAP
  • Not low-FODMAP — concentrated apple juice is high in excess fructose or polyols.

Concentrated apple juice is natural apple juice with the water removed, used to sweeten and flavour foods without using refined white sugar.

What it is

Concentrated apple juice is simply regular apple juice that has had most of its natural water content removed. What remains is a thick, sweet liquid that retains the natural sugars, acids, and subtle fruit flavours of the original apples. It is a familiar, fruit-derived ingredient that sits somewhere between a whole fruit juice and a liquid sweetener, offering a more natural alternative to highly refined syrups while still delivering a concentrated burst of sweetness.

How it's made

The journey begins with whole apples, which are washed, crushed, and pressed to extract their fresh juice. This liquid is filtered to remove the solid pulp and fibres, leaving a clear juice. To concentrate it, the juice is heated under a vacuum, which allows the water to evaporate at a lower temperature to preserve the natural flavours. The steam is drawn off, leaving behind a dense, syrupy concentrate that is cooled and stored until it is ready to be blended into food products.

Why manufacturers use it

Food makers choose this ingredient primarily as a natural sweetener and a source of fruit flavour. It allows them to sweeten products like snacks, baked goods, and yoghurts while keeping the ingredient list appealing to shoppers who want to avoid refined cane sugar. Because it is highly concentrated, it is easy to transport and store, and it blends smoothly into liquid and semi-solid recipes without adding excess water that could spoil the texture or shorten the shelf life.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this NEUTRAL. While it is a fruit derivative rather than a synthetic additive, it is still a concentrated source of free sugars that the body processes in much the same way as other liquid sweeteners. Because it is not classified as a food additive, no daily limit is established by regulatory bodies. It is a wholesome option when you want to avoid highly processed syrups, but it is still best enjoyed in moderation as part of a balanced diet.

What the science says

Major food authorities, including the FDA and EFSA, view concentrated fruit juices as standard food ingredients rather than additives. Because of this, they do not undergo the same additive safety evaluations as artificial sweeteners. Scientific discussions focus instead on how the body metabolises the simple sugars present in the juice. Since the natural plant fibres are removed during processing, the sugars are absorbed quickly, which is why nutritionists recommend mindful consumption of foods sweetened this way.

Where you'll find it

You will find this ingredient in a wide variety of supermarket items, especially in the baby food aisle, in fruit bars, granolas, yoghurts, jellies, and fruit-flavoured drinks. On packaging, it does not have an E-number because it is a food ingredient rather than an additive. It is typically declared plainly on the label as concentrated apple juice, apple juice concentrate, or reconstituted apple juice when water has been added back to it.
