Carboxymethyl cellulose (E466)

E466AdditiveNeutral · benign

Carboxymethyl cellulose is a modified plant fibre used to thicken foods, rated neutral by Clara with no specified daily intake limit set by regulators.

What it is

Carboxymethyl cellulose is a modified plant fibre that acts as a highly effective thickener and stabiliser. It is derived from the structural parts of plants, such as wood pulp or cotton lint, but it undergoes a chemical process to attach carboxymethyl groups to the cellulose chain. This modification makes the fibre highly soluble in water, allowing it to form smooth, stable gels and thick liquids. While it starts as a natural plant material, the final substance is a semi-synthetic compound that behaves differently from raw plant fibre in food mixtures.

How it's made

The production begins with raw plant cellulose, which is harvested from wood pulp or cotton. This cellulose is treated with an alkaline solution, typically sodium hydroxide, to swell the fibres and make them reactive. It is then reacted with monochloroacetic acid, which introduces the carboxymethyl groups onto the cellulose backbone. This chemical reaction is carefully controlled to achieve the desired level of solubility and viscosity. Finally, the mixture is neutralised, purified with alcohol and water to remove byproducts, dried, and milled into a fine white powder.

Why manufacturers use it

Food manufacturers rely on this ingredient because it is an exceptionally versatile thickener that works well in both hot and cold liquids. It binds water efficiently, which prevents ice crystals from forming in frozen desserts and stops liquids from separating in sauces and dressings. It also mimics the creamy mouthfeel of fat without adding calories, making it a popular choice for reduced-fat products. Because it is highly stable under a wide range of temperatures and acidity levels, it outperforms many natural starches and gums at a lower cost.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this NEUTRAL, a benign additive. Because it is a modified fibre, it passes through the human digestive system largely unchanged and is not absorbed into the bloodstream. EFSA re-evaluated the substance in 2017 and determined that there is no safety concern, assigning no specified acceptable daily intake limit. It is generally well tolerated, though consuming extremely large quantities of any modified cellulose might lead to mild digestive changes, such as temporary bloating, similar to eating a very high-fibre meal.

What the science says

The safety of this ingredient has been thoroughly reviewed by major food authorities. The European Food Safety Authority evaluated the additive alongside other modified celluloses and concluded that they do not pose a safety concern at the levels currently used in food. In the United States, the Food and Drug Administration recognises it as generally recognised as safe. Because it is not absorbed by the body, researchers focus primarily on its physical behaviour in the gut, where it acts as a bulk-forming fibre without any toxicological concerns.

Where you'll find it

You will find this ingredient in a wide variety of processed foods, including ice cream, salad dressings, baked goods, gluten-free bread, and low-calorie dairy products. On ingredient lists, it is commonly declared by its full name, carboxymethyl cellulose, or as sodium carboxymethyl cellulose. In European products, it is often identified by its E-number, E466, while on US labels it is frequently listed as sodium carboxymethylcellulose or simply cellulose gum.
