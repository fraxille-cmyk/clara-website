Microcrystalline wax (E905)

E905AdditiveAvoid · moderate concern

A petroleum-derived glazing agent used to coat sweets and chewing gum, which Clara recommends avoiding due to concerns about it building up in the body.

What it is

Microcrystalline wax is a dense, highly refined wax derived from petroleum. Unlike paraffin wax, which has large crystals and can be brittle, this wax consists of tiny, intricate crystals that make it remarkably flexible, sticky, and water-resistant. It is chemically similar to the mineral hydrocarbons found in crude oil, but it undergoes extensive refining to remove impurities before it is deemed suitable for use in food packaging, cosmetics, and certain types of confectionery.

How it's made

The production of microcrystalline wax begins with the heavy lubricating oil fractions obtained during the refining of crude petroleum. These fractions undergo a de-oiling process, where solvents are used to separate the wax from the liquid oil at low temperatures. The resulting crude wax is then heavily refined through chemical hydrogenation and filtration. This intense purification process strips away unwanted odours, flavours, and potentially harmful impurities, leaving behind a highly purified, solid white or pale yellow wax.

Why manufacturers use it

Food manufacturers value microcrystalline wax for its exceptional moisture barrier properties and its flexibility. It is primarily used as a glazing agent to give sweets a shiny, appealing appearance and to prevent them from sticking together in the packet. Because it does not crack under tension, it is also an ideal base ingredient for chewing gum, helping the gum maintain its pliable, chewy texture over a long shelf life without drying out or crumbling.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this AVOID, a moderate-concern additive. While regulators historically permitted its use, EFSA withdrew the temporary daily limit for this substance due to concerns that it can accumulate in the liver and lymph nodes. Because of these unresolved safety questions, Clara advises choosing products that use alternative natural glazing agents like beeswax or carnauba wax instead.

What the science says

European food safety experts re-evaluated this ingredient and raised concerns about its long-term behaviour in the body. Animal studies indicate that these mineral hydrocarbons can be absorbed and subsequently accumulate in the liver and the lymphatic system. EFSA concluded that the available scientific data was insufficient to establish a permanent safe daily intake limit, noting a lack of adequate chronic toxicity and mutagenicity studies to prove that long-term accumulation does not cause adverse effects.

Where you'll find it

You will find this ingredient in various sweets, decorated confectionery, and chewing gum bases. On ingredient lists in the United Kingdom and Europe, it is typically declared as microcrystalline wax or by its E-number, E905. On US labels, it may be listed as petroleum wax or microcrystalline wax, where it is permitted for use in chewing gum bases and as a protective coating on certain fruits.
