Xylitol (E967)

E967SweetenerNeutral · limited concern

Xylitol is a sugar alcohol used as a low-calorie sweetener that is generally safe but can cause digestive discomfort when consumed in large quantities.

What it is

Xylitol is a sugar alcohol, or polyol, which looks and tastes very much like regular table sugar but contains fewer calories. It occurs naturally in small amounts in various fruits, vegetables, and the bark of certain trees like birch. Although it has a natural origin, the version you see on ingredient lists is a chemically modified substance. It has a unique cooling sensation on the tongue, which makes it highly popular in refreshing products. It behaves similarly to sugar in recipes but does not raise blood glucose levels in the same way.

How it's made

To make xylitol on an industrial scale, manufacturers start with agricultural raw materials rich in a complex carbohydrate called xylan. These materials typically include hardwood trees like birch or agricultural leftovers like corn cobs. The xylan is extracted and broken down into a simple sugar called xylose. This sugar then undergoes a chemical modification process called catalytic hydrogenation, where it is treated with hydrogen under high pressure in the presence of a metal catalyst. The resulting liquid is purified, filtered, and crystallised into the white granules used in food.

Why manufacturers use it

Food manufacturers choose xylitol because it provides the exact sweetness of sugar with about forty per cent fewer calories. It is highly stable under heat, meaning it does not break down during baking or pasteurisation. Unlike sugar, it does not feed the bacteria in the mouth that cause plaque, which makes it the premier choice for dental-friendly products. Its distinct cooling effect is highly prized in mints and chewing gums, where it enhances the perception of freshness while helping to bulk out the product and retain moisture.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this NEUTRAL, a limited-concern additive. Xylitol is a chemically modified sugar alcohol used as a low-calorie sweetener, which can cause digestive discomfort when consumed in large quantities. Because the human digestive tract does not fully absorb sugar alcohols, they draw water into the bowel and can be fermented by gut bacteria, leading to gas, bloating, or a laxative effect if you eat too much at once. No limit is established for daily intake because regulators consider it very low in toxicity, but moderation is sensible.

What the science says

The Scientific Committee on Food evaluated xylitol and established that an acceptable daily intake limit is not specified, meaning it is safe for general consumption without a strict scientific ceiling. The European Food Safety Authority and the US Food and Drug Administration both permit its use in food. Research shows that because our bodies metabolise it slowly and incompletely, it has a minimal impact on insulin levels. Scientists agree that the only common side effect is temporary digestive sensitivity, which varies from person to person depending on individual tolerance.

Where you'll find it

You will find this ingredient in a wide variety of sugar-free and reduced-calorie products. It is incredibly common in sugar-free chewing gum, breath mints, throat lozenges, and oral care products like toothpaste and mouthwash. It also appears in sugar-free chocolates, baked goods, jams, and tabletop sweeteners. On a label, it is usually listed plainly by its name as xylitol, but it may also appear under its European code E967 or E 967, and on US labels it is permitted as a direct food additive.
