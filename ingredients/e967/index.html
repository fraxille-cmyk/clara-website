Xylitol (E967)

E967SweetenerAvoid · moderate concern

Xylitol is a low-calorie sugar alcohol used as a sweetener that can cause digestive discomfort and carries a mandatory warning about its laxative effects.

What it is

Xylitol is a sugar alcohol, a type of carbohydrate that looks and tastes much like regular table sugar but contains fewer calories. It is found naturally in small amounts in various fruits and vegetables, as well as in the bark of birch trees. In the food industry, it serves as a bulk sweetener, providing a clean, sweet taste with a distinctive cooling sensation in the mouth. Because it does not raise blood sugar levels the way sucrose does, it is a popular sugar substitute in products designed for sugar-free diets.

How it's made

Although it occurs naturally, the xylitol on food labels is industrially produced through chemical modification. Manufacturers start with agricultural raw materials rich in xylan, a complex carbohydrate found in birch wood, corn cobs, or oat hulls. This material undergoes an industrial extraction process to isolate xylose, a simple wood sugar. This xylose is then chemically hydrogenated using a metal catalyst under high pressure to transform it into xylitol, which is purified, crystallised, and dried into a white crystalline powder.

Why manufacturers use it

Food manufacturers choose xylitol because it matches the sweetness of sugar almost exactly, gram for gram, but with about forty per cent fewer calories. It does not break down under high heat, making it highly stable for baking and processing. Its unique cooling effect makes it especially popular in mints and chewing gum, where it enhances the perception of freshness. Additionally, it does not promote the growth of mouth bacteria, which makes it a preferred choice for dental-friendly confectionery.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this AVOID, a moderate-concern additive. While it is processed by the body similarly to other carbohydrates, it is only partially absorbed in the small intestine. The remaining portion travels to the large intestine, where it draws in water and is fermented by resident bacteria. This process can lead to bloating, gas, and abdominal discomfort. Because of this digestive impact, any food containing more than ten per cent added polyols like xylitol must carry a mandatory warning stating that excessive consumption may produce laxative effects.

What the science says

Regulators have evaluated xylitol extensively to determine its safety in the food supply. The Scientific Committee on Food established that no specific daily limit is necessary, meaning no numerical acceptable daily intake is specified. This indicates a low level of immediate concern, though the physical effects on the digestive tract remain well-documented. The European Union requires clear labelling to warn consumers of potential digestive issues, while the United States Food and Drug Administration permits its direct use as a food additive.

Where you'll find it

You will commonly find this sweetener in sugar-free chewing gums, breath mints, throat lozenges, and sugar-free chocolates or baked goods. It also appears frequently in oral care products like toothpaste and mouthwash. On ingredient lists, it is typically declared by its plain name as xylitol, or by its European additive code, E967. On US labels, it is simply listed as xylitol, often accompanied by a voluntary or mandatory statement about the presence of sugar alcohols in the nutrition facts panel.
