Oxidised starch (E1404)

E1404AdditiveNeutral · benign

Oxidised starch is a modified food starch used to thicken and stabilise foods, which your body digests just like regular starch.

What it is

Oxidised starch is a modified food starch, which means it is a natural plant starch that has been gently altered to improve how it behaves when cooked. It is typically sourced from corn, potatoes, wheat, or tapioca. While it starts its life as a simple carbohydrate, the modification process changes its structure slightly so that it does not break down or lose its thickening power under high heat or when mixed with acidic ingredients. It behaves very much like the cornflour you might use at home, but with extra resilience.

How it's made

To make oxidised starch, manufacturers start with raw starch extracted from crops like maize or potatoes. This starch is suspended in water and treated with a mild oxidising agent, usually sodium hypochlorite, which is similar to household bleach but used here under strictly controlled food-grade conditions. This process introduces carboxyl and carbonyl groups into the starch molecules. Once the desired level of modification is reached, the starch is thoroughly washed to remove any processing chemicals, neutralised, dried, and ground into a fine white powder ready for food production.

Why manufacturers use it

Food manufacturers use oxidised starch because it is an excellent binder, thickener, and emulsifier. When starch is oxidised, it gelatinises at a lower temperature and forms a clearer, more stable paste that resists gelling when it cools down. This makes it ideal for creating smooth textures in sauces, fillings, and batters. It also helps batters adhere better to fried foods like chicken or fish, and it acts as a strong binding agent in confectionery, helping to create the perfect chewy texture in soft sweets without melting.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this NEUTRAL, a benign additive. Once inside your body, oxidised starch is broken down and digested in the exact same way as ordinary, unmodified starch from a potato or a slice of bread. It is converted into simple sugars and used by your cells for energy. Because it behaves so naturally in our digestive system, it does not pose any safety concerns for the general public, and no daily intake limit has been established by food safety authorities.

What the science says

The safety of oxidised starch has been thoroughly evaluated by major international food safety bodies. The European Food Safety Authority re-evaluated modified starches and concluded that they are of no safety concern and require no numerical acceptable daily intake. Similarly, the Joint FAO/WHO Expert Committee on Food Additives has reviewed its safety and established that no limit is necessary. Because it is processed by the body as a normal carbohydrate, scientific assessments focus on its digestibility, which is consistently shown to be excellent.

Where you'll find it

You will find this ingredient in a wide variety of processed foods, including salad dressings, instant soups, gravies, bakery fillings, battered frozen foods, and jelly-like sweets. On food labels, it is usually listed by its name as oxidised starch, or by its European code, E1404. It may also be grouped under the broader term modified starch, depending on local labelling regulations.
