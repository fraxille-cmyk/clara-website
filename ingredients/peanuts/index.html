Peanuts

Whole foodGood

Peanuts are a nutritious whole food rich in protein and healthy fats, though they are a common allergen that must be clearly declared on labels.

What it is

Peanuts are the edible seeds of a legume plant that matures underground, unlike tree nuts. Although botanically related to peas and beans, they share a similar culinary role and nutrient profile with traditional nuts. They are naturally packed with plant-based protein, unsaturated fats, dietary fibre, and essential micronutrients like vitamin E and magnesium. People have enjoyed them for thousands of years, and they remain a staple food across the globe, eaten whole, crushed, or pressed for their rich oil.

How it's made

The journey of a peanut begins in the soil, where the self-pollinating flower of the plant bends down and pushes its seed pod into the earth to mature. Once fully grown, the entire plant is dug up and left to dry in the field. The pods are then mechanically harvested, cleaned, and shelled to reveal the raw kernels. Depending on how they will be used, these kernels are sorted and often roasted using dry heat or oil to develop their characteristic warm, nutty flavour and crisp texture.

Why manufacturers use it

Food makers choose peanuts because they offer an appealing combination of rich flavour, satisfying crunch, and excellent nutritional value at a reasonable cost. They add texture to confectionery, depth to savoury sauces, and structure to snack bars. Because they are naturally high in fats and protein, they help create satisfying products that keep people feeling full. Their natural oils also help bind other dry ingredients together in baked goods, reducing the need for processed fats or artificial binders.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this GOOD. As a wholesome, minimally processed ingredient, it is highly nutritious for most people, though it is one of the most common food allergens. For those with a sensitivity, even tiny amounts trigger a rapid immune response, which is why food safety laws require its presence to be highlighted on packaging. For everyone else, they are a nourishing source of energy. No daily intake limit is established for this whole food, and it is widely encouraged as part of a balanced diet.

What the science says

Major food authorities worldwide have thoroughly evaluated peanuts, focusing primarily on allergen management and nutritional benefits. The FDA and EFSA recognise them as a major allergen, establishing strict labelling rules to protect sensitive individuals. Beyond allergy considerations, public health bodies, including the WHO, highlight them as a valuable source of unsaturated fatty acids and plant proteins. Research shows that incorporating them into a daily diet supports overall metabolic health and helps maintain balanced cholesterol levels in the body.

Where you'll find it

You will spot this ingredient in snack mixes, breakfast cereals, granola bars, baked goods, and satay sauces. On ingredient lists, it is usually declared plainly as peanuts, roasted peanuts, or roasted peanut pieces, and it will always be highlighted in bold or capital letters to alert anyone with an allergy. Because it is a whole food rather than a food additive, it does not have an E-number.
