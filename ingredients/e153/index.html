Vegetable carbon (E153)

E153AdditiveAvoid · moderate concern

Vegetable carbon is a black food colour made from charred plant matter, rated avoid by Clara due to unresolved safety questions about its tiny particles.

What it is

Vegetable carbon is a fine, intense black powder used to colour food. It is essentially a highly purified form of charcoal, consisting almost entirely of elemental carbon. While carbon is one of the most common elements on Earth and forms the basis of all organic life, this particular form is processed to have an extremely high surface area, which allows it to absorb other substances and scatter light to create a deep, solid black appearance in whatever it is added to.

How it's made

To make this ingredient, manufacturers take raw vegetable matter, such as peat, wood, starch, or coconut shells, and heat it to extremely high temperatures in an environment without oxygen. This process, called carbonisation, burns away everything except the carbon skeleton of the plant. The resulting charcoal is then treated with steam or gas to activate it, which creates millions of microscopic pores, before being ground down into an incredibly fine powder that can be easily dispersed into liquids and batters.

Why manufacturers use it

Food producers use vegetable carbon solely as a colour to give products a dramatic, deep black or grey shade. It is highly valued because it is incredibly stable, meaning it does not fade when exposed to heat during baking, light on supermarket shelves, or acids in fruit mixtures. It is also much cheaper and easier to standardise than natural black ingredients like squid ink, allowing manufacturers to achieve a striking, consistent visual effect in trendy black burger buns, cheeses, and confectionery.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this AVOID, a moderate-concern additive. While the carbon itself is not absorbed by the body and passes straight through, the European Food Safety Authority noted that they could not fully assess the safety of the nanoparticle fraction present in the powder. The United States Food and Drug Administration has not approved vegetable carbon as a food colour additive, meaning its use in American food products is banned. No daily intake limit is established by European regulators, but the lack of complete safety data on these tiny particles is why Clara advises caution.

What the science says

Scientific evaluations of vegetable carbon focus on the size of its particles and the presence of impurities. Because the powder is ground so finely, a portion of it consists of nanoparticles, which behave differently in the body than larger particles. The European Food Safety Authority re-evaluated the additive and highlighted that more data is needed to understand if these tiny particles can penetrate the gut lining. Additionally, regulators monitor the charcoal to ensure it does not contain harmful combustion byproducts, which are kept to strict, minimal levels by modern manufacturing standards.

Where you'll find it

You will find this ingredient in eye-catching black foods, including dark liquorice, charcoal-infused cheeses, black macarons, sandwich biscuits, and decorative icings. On ingredient lists, it is typically declared by its name as vegetable carbon, vegetable carbon black, or simply by its European code, E153. It is also sometimes listed as carbon black or activated charcoal when used in bakery products and novelty snacks to give them an intense, dark appearance.
