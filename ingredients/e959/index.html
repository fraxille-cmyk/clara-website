Neohesperidine DC (E959)

E959SweetenerNeutral · limited concern

Neohesperidine DC is an intense sweetener made from bitter oranges that Clara rates as a limited-concern additive.

What it is

Neohesperidine DC, also known as neohesperidine dihydrochalcone, is an intense artificial sweetener and flavour modifier. It is roughly one thousand times sweeter than regular table sugar and is known for leaving a cooling, liquorice-like aftertaste. Although it is chemically modified, its origin lies in the natural compounds found in the peels of bitter Seville oranges. It is used in tiny amounts to sweeten foods or to mask bitter flavours in various products.

How it's made

The production process begins with harvesting bitter oranges to extract a natural substance called neohesperidin from the peel. Manufacturers then treat this extract with a strong alkaline solution, such as potassium hydroxide, to open its chemical ring structure. This intermediate compound undergoes hydrogenation, a chemical reaction with hydrogen gas over a metal catalyst, which converts it into the highly sweet dihydrochalcone form. The final product is filtered, crystallised, and dried into a fine, off-white powder.

Why manufacturers use it

Food manufacturers choose this ingredient because it is incredibly potent, meaning they only need a minuscule amount to achieve the desired sweetness. This helps reduce production costs and lowers the calorie count of the final product. Beyond simple sweetening, it has a unique ability to mask bitter notes and enhance other flavours, particularly creamy and fruity profiles. It also remains highly stable under heat and acidic conditions, making it ideal for pasteurised foods and long shelf-life products.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this NEUTRAL, a limited-concern additive. EFSA considers it safe within the daily limit and has established an acceptable daily intake of 5 mg per kg of body weight. Because it is so intensely sweet, the amount actually added to foods is extremely small, making it very difficult for a shopper to exceed this safety limit in a normal diet. There are no major safety concerns or adverse effects associated with its consumption at typical levels.

What the science says

This sweetener has been thoroughly evaluated by international food safety authorities. JECFA reviewed the substance and found no safety concerns, and EFSA re-evaluated it to confirm its safety limit. Scientific assessments show that the human body does not absorb much of this compound; instead, gut bacteria break it down into harmless, familiar plant metabolites that are easily excreted. No regulatory body has raised concerns regarding its stability or its behaviour in the digestive system.

Where you'll find it

You will find this ingredient in low-calorie drinks, sugar-free chewing gum, dairy products, desserts, and various sauces. On ingredient lists, it typically appears under its full name, neohesperidine DC, or simply as E959. In the United States, where it is primarily used to alter flavours rather than as a standalone sweetener, it may be noted under its FEMA flavour designation or simply grouped under artificial flavourings.
