Acesulfame K (E950)

E950SweetenerAvoid · moderate concern

Acesulfame K is an artificial sweetener about two hundred times sweeter than sugar, used to sweeten foods without adding any calories.

What it is

Acesulfame K, also known as acesulfame potassium, is a calorie-free artificial sweetener. It is a white crystalline powder that is about two hundred times sweeter than standard table sugar. Unlike sugar, the human body cannot break it down for energy, so it passes through the digestive system unchanged. It is often blended with other artificial sweeteners because it has a slightly bitter, metallic aftertaste when used on its own, and combining it with other substances helps to mask this flavour.

How it's made

This sweetener is entirely man-made in a laboratory setting. The production process begins with derivatives of acetoacetic acid, which are reacted with a chemical compound containing sulphur and nitrogen. This reaction creates a ring structure that is then neutralised using potassium hydroxide. The resulting substance is crystallised, purified, and dried to form the final white powder. Because it is a purely synthetic chemical, it does not rely on any agricultural crops or natural plant extracts for its sweet taste.

Why manufacturers use it

Food companies choose this ingredient because it is incredibly sweet and highly stable under heat. Unlike some other sweeteners, it does not lose its sweetness when baked or pasteurised, making it highly versatile for processed foods. It is also incredibly cheap compared to sugar because such tiny amounts are needed to achieve the same level of sweetness. By replacing sugar with this additive, manufacturers can market their products as sugar-free or low-calorie while maintaining a sweet taste profile that appeals to shoppers.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this AVOID, a moderate-concern additive. While it is approved for use, there are concerns that this artificial sweetener may alter the balance of the gut microbiota and affect how our bodies respond to sweet tastes over time. The long-term data on how it affects human biology remains limited. JECFA has established an acceptable daily intake of 15 mg per kg of body weight, and EFSA maintains a similar limit, but many shoppers prefer to steer clear of synthetic sweeteners that do not offer any nutritional benefit.

What the science says

Regulators have reviewed this substance several times to ensure its safety in the food supply. JECFA evaluated the sweetener and established its daily limit, while EFSA reaffirmed its safety for the general population in a re-evaluation. However, modern independent research continues to investigate how synthetic sweeteners interact with the microbes in our digestive tract. Because long-term human studies are difficult to conduct, some scientists argue that we still do not fully understand the subtle, cumulative effects of consuming these artificial compounds daily over several decades.

Where you'll find it

You will find this ingredient in a wide variety of low-calorie and sugar-free products, including diet fizzy drinks, protein shakes, sugar-free chewing gum, yoghurts, and baked goods. On the ingredient list, it can be declared under several different names, including acesulfame K, acesulfame potassium, acesulfame-k, or simply by its European additive number, E 950. It is frequently paired alongside aspartame or sucralose on the label to balance out the sweet profile.
