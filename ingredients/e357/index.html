Potassium adipate (E357)

E357AdditiveNeutral · limited concern

Potassium adipate is a synthetic acidity regulator used to control tartness, rated NEUTRAL by Clara as it is safe within established daily limits.

What it is

Potassium adipate is a synthetic potassium salt of adipic acid, an organic compound that occurs naturally in beetroots and sugar cane. In the food industry, it serves as an acidity regulator, helping to control the tartness and preserve the structure of processed foods. It is a white, odourless crystalline powder that dissolves easily in water, making it highly compatible with liquid and moist food formulations. While it shares characteristics with natural fruit acids, the version used in food is always produced synthetically to ensure purity and consistency.

How it's made

To manufacture potassium adipate, chemical producers start with adipic acid, which is typically synthesised from petrochemical raw materials through a multi-step oxidation process. This acid is then neutralised by reacting it with potassium hydroxide or potassium carbonate in an aqueous solution. The reaction produces potassium adipate, which is subsequently crystallised out of the liquid, filtered, washed, and dried into a fine powder. This synthetic route ensures that the final additive is highly pure and free from organic impurities that might alter the flavour of the food.

Why manufacturers use it

Food manufacturers choose potassium adipate primarily because it is an exceptionally efficient acidity regulator and buffering agent. It helps maintain a stable pH level in foods, which prevents spoilage, maintains texture, and ensures a consistent flavour profile over a long shelf life. It also acts as a flavour enhancer, imparting a mild, pleasant tartness without the sharp bite associated with other acids like citric or malic acid. Because it is highly soluble and stable under heat, it performs reliably in baked goods and processed snacks where other regulators might break down.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this NEUTRAL, a limited-concern additive. This rating reflects its status as a synthetic substance with an established limit on daily intake. EFSA keeps its daily limit of 40 mg per kg of body weight for adipic acid and its salts, including potassium adipate. At typical dietary exposure levels, it is processed by the body in the same manner as natural dietary acids and poses no safety concerns. There are no indications of accumulation in the body, and the substance is considered highly predictable in how it is metabolised.

What the science says

The safety of potassium adipate has been thoroughly evaluated by international food safety authorities. The European Food Safety Authority re-evaluated the additive alongside other adipates and confirmed that it does not pose a safety concern at current levels of exposure. Because it is a simple salt, the body easily splits it into potassium and adipic acid, both of which are processed through normal metabolic pathways. No adverse effects have been observed in dietary studies, and no regulatory body has raised concerns regarding its stability or its behaviour in the human digestive system.

Where you'll find it

You will find potassium adipate in a variety of processed foods, particularly dry dessert mixes, powdered drinks, fillings, and some bakery products. It is also used in low-sodium products as a substitute for sodium-based regulators. On ingredient lists, it is typically declared by its name as potassium adipate or by its European code, E357. On US labels, where its status is less common, it may be listed plainly as potassium adipate to indicate its role as a firming agent or pH control agent.
