Propyl gallate (E310)

E310AdditiveAvoid · moderate concern

Propyl gallate is a synthetic antioxidant used to stop fats from spoiling, which Clara rates AVOID due to a temporary safety limit set by European regulators.

What it is

Propyl gallate is a synthetic antioxidant designed to mimic the natural protective compounds found in plants. Chemically, it is an ester formed by combining gallic acid, which occurs naturally in oak bark and tea leaves, with propanol. While gallic acid itself is a natural substance, the resulting propyl gallate is a manufactured compound that does not occur in nature. It appears as a fine, odourless white crystalline powder that dissolves easily in fats and oils, making it highly effective at preserving fatty foods.

How it's made

The production of propyl gallate is an entirely industrial process. Manufacturers start with gallic acid, which is typically obtained by breaking down tannins extracted from tara pods or gallnuts using acids or enzymes. This gallic acid is then heated and reacted with propanol, a simple alcohol, in the presence of an acid catalyst like sulphuric acid. This chemical reaction, known as esterification, links the molecules together. The resulting mixture is cooled, crystallised, washed with water to remove impurities, and dried into a pure powder.

Why manufacturers use it

Food manufacturers use propyl gallate to prevent fats and oils from reacting with oxygen, a process that leads to rancidity, unpleasant odours, and off-flavours. It is an exceptionally powerful antioxidant that remains stable even during high-temperature processing. By stopping the oxidation of lipids, it significantly extends the shelf life of packaged foods. It is highly valued because it works at very low concentrations, meaning manufacturers can use tiny amounts to keep fat-heavy products tasting fresh for months on supermarket shelves.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this AVOID, a moderate-concern additive. This rating is based on the fact that the European Food Safety Authority assigned it a temporary acceptable daily intake of 0.5 milligrams per kilogram of body weight due to limitations in the available safety database. While regulators have not banned it, the temporary nature of this limit indicates that scientists require more comprehensive data to fully confirm its long-term safety profile. Because of these unresolved questions in the official database, Clara advises choosing products without this synthetic preservative.

What the science says

The European Food Safety Authority re-evaluated propyl gallate and established a temporary acceptable daily intake because the existing scientific database had gaps that prevented a permanent limit from being set. Researchers have analysed how the body metabolises the compound, noting that it is broken down into gallic acid and excreted in urine. While the US Food and Drug Administration permits its use as a generally recognised as safe substance up to specific limits in fats, European experts continue to monitor the additive, waiting for further toxicological studies to resolve the outstanding database questions.

Where you'll find it

You will find this preservative in fat-rich foods that are highly susceptible to spoiling, such as vegetable oils, margarines, shortenings, chewing gum, instant soups, and processed meat products. On ingredient labels, it is typically declared by its plain chemical name as propyl gallate or by its European code, E310. On US labels, it is listed as propyl gallate, often alongside other synthetic antioxidants like BHA or BHT, which are frequently used together to create a stronger preservative effect.
