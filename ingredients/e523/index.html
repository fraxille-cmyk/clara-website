Aluminium ammonium sulphate (E523)

E523AdditiveAvoid · moderate concern

Diets

✓ Suits every diet Clara checks — as an ingredient

Aluminium ammonium sulphate is a synthetic mineral salt used to firm fruits, but Clara rates it AVOID because it can lead to excess aluminium intake.

What it is

Aluminium ammonium sulphate is a synthetic mineral salt that exists as white crystals or a crystalline powder. It is a double salt of aluminium and ammonium, meaning it combines both elements in one structure. While it occurs naturally in rare volcanic minerals, the version used in food is entirely man-made. It is part of the alum family, which people have used for centuries to purify water and prepare food. In modern food production, it acts as a firming agent and an acidity regulator, helping to keep plant tissues crisp and stable.

How it's made

To manufacture this ingredient, producers chemically synthesise it in a controlled industrial setting. They react aluminium sulphate, which is derived from aluminium ore, with ammonium sulphate in an aqueous solution. The mixture is heated and then cooled to crystallise the compound. Once the crystals form, they are separated from the liquid, washed to remove impurities, dried, and ground into a fine powder. This process ensures a highly pure substance that meets strict food-grade standards, with no natural mineral impurities remaining.

Why manufacturers use it

Food manufacturers use this additive primarily to improve the texture of processed fruits and vegetables. When these foods are preserved or candied, their natural cell walls tend to break down and become mushy. This mineral salt interacts with the natural pectins in the plant cells, forming a firm gel structure that keeps the food crisp and plump. It also acts as an acidity regulator, helping to maintain a stable pH level during processing. This ensures the product retains its appealing shape, colour, and bite over a long shelf life.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this AVOID, a moderate-concern additive. While it is authorised for use, EFSA established a tolerable weekly intake of 1 mg of aluminium per kg of body weight in 2008. The regulator noted that some high consumers, especially children, exceed this tolerable weekly intake of aluminium. Because aluminium can accumulate in the body over time, consuming foods with this additive can push your total intake past the recommended limit. For this reason, Clara advises choosing alternatives that do not contribute to your dietary aluminium load.

What the science says

Scientific bodies have closely evaluated the safety of dietary aluminium. EFSA re-evaluated aluminium compounds in 2008 and established a strict weekly limit based on how the human body absorbs and retains the metal. The FDA has affirmed the ingredient as generally recognised as safe under its own regulations. Because aluminium is present in many foods and packaging materials, researchers focus on the cumulative exposure from all sources. The main concern is that children can easily consume more than the recommended weekly limit through a typical diet.

Where you'll find it

You will find this ingredient in a very limited selection of foods because regulations restrict its use. It is primarily used in candied, crystallised, or glaced fruits and vegetables, as well as in some processed egg white products. On ingredient labels, it is listed by its full name, aluminium ammonium sulphate, or by its European code, E523. On US labels, it may be listed as aluminium ammonium sulfate. It is always best to check the ingredients list on festive baking fruits and decorative cherries to see if this additive is present.
