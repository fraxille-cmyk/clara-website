Delta-tocopherol (E309)

E309AdditiveNeutral · limited concern

Delta-tocopherol is a synthetic form of vitamin E used as an antioxidant to keep fats and oils fresh, with a safe daily limit set by regulators.

What it is

Delta-tocopherol is a specific form of vitamin E. While vitamin E occurs naturally in nuts, seeds, and leafy greens, this particular version is a synthetic counterpart designed to protect food. In the world of chemistry, tocopherols exist in several closely related forms, named with Greek letters. The delta form is especially prized in food science because it is highly effective at stopping oxygen from damaging fats, even more so than the alpha-tocopherol form that our bodies preferentially absorb as a nutrient.

How it's made

To make this ingredient on a commercial scale, manufacturers synthesise it in a laboratory. The process involves a chemical reaction between trimethylhydroquinone and phytol or a similar chemical precursor. This synthetic route allows producers to isolate a highly pure, concentrated form of delta-tocopherol. Unlike natural vitamin E extracts which are mixtures of various tocopherols distilled from vegetable oils, this synthetic additive is created through precise chemical synthesis to ensure consistent strength and performance in food preservation.

Why manufacturers use it

Manufacturers use delta-tocopherol as an antioxidant to prevent fats and oils from turning rancid. When food is exposed to air and light, the fats inside begin to oxidise, which leads to unpleasant stale smells and off-flavours. Delta-tocopherol steps in to absorb this oxidative stress, significantly extending the shelf life of packaged foods. It is highly valued because it remains stable at high temperatures, making it much more reliable for baked goods and fried snacks than other delicate preservation methods.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this NEUTRAL, a limited-concern additive. This synthetic form of vitamin E keeps oils and fats from turning rancid, and it has a numeric acceptable daily intake of 2 mg per kg of body weight. EFSA established this limit in its safety evaluations, noting that normal dietary intake from food additives remains well within this range. Because it is a synthetic additive with a specific numeric limit rather than an unlimited safety rating, Clara advises keeping an eye on your overall intake of processed foods.

What the science says

Scientific bodies have thoroughly evaluated delta-tocopherol to ensure it does not accumulate to unwanted levels in the body. EFSA re-evaluated the safety of tocopherols as food additives and confirmed their safety profile, establishing the group acceptable daily intake of 2 mg per kg of body weight. The FDA also permits its use, designating it as generally recognised as safe. Because it is metabolised by the liver much like natural vitamin E, researchers have found no evidence of safety concerns when it is consumed within the established guidelines.

Where you'll find it

You will find this ingredient in a wide variety of fat-based foods, including vegetable oils, margarines, salad dressings, breakfast cereals, and baked goods. On a food label, it is typically listed by its plain name as delta-tocopherol, or by its European code, E 309. On US labels, it is often declared simply as tocopherols or under its specific chemical name to indicate its role as a preservative, helping to keep the fats in the product tasting fresh for longer.
