Glutamic acid (E620)

E620AdditiveAvoid · moderate concern

Glutamic acid is a savoury flavour enhancer that Clara advises avoiding because many people consume more than the recommended daily limit.

What it is

Glutamic acid is an amino acid, one of the fundamental building blocks of protein. It occurs naturally in many foods, especially savoury ones like aged cheeses, tomatoes, and mushrooms. In its free form, it stimulates specific receptors on our tongue, giving us the rich, savoury taste known as umami. While our bodies make this amino acid naturally and use it to build proteins and transmit signals in the nervous system, the version added to packaged food is an isolated additive used to intensify taste.

How it's made

Industrial production of this ingredient relies on a natural fermentation process. Manufacturers feed sugar-rich liquids, such as molasses or starch hydrolysates, to specialised strains of bacteria. These microbes metabolise the sugars and release large quantities of the amino acid into the surrounding liquid. The broth is then filtered, purified, and crystallised into a white powder. This method allows for large-scale production without chemically modifying the structure of the molecule, keeping it identical to the substance found in nature.

Why manufacturers use it

Food companies use this additive to boost the savoury, meaty depth of their products. It acts as a powerful flavour enhancer, making low-cost ingredients taste richer and more satisfying. By intensifying the existing flavours in a recipe, it allows manufacturers to use less salt and fewer expensive whole ingredients, like real meat or cheese extracts, while maintaining a highly appealing taste profile. It dissolves easily and blends seamlessly into liquid and dry formulations alike.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this AVOID, a moderate-concern additive. While it is a natural amino acid, the concentrated form added to processed foods can easily lead to overconsumption. In 2017, EFSA established a group acceptable daily intake of 30 milligrams per kilogram of body weight for these compounds. The regulator noted that high consumers in several population groups exceeded this limit. Because it is so widespread in modern diets, choosing to avoid foods with this added ingredient helps keep your daily intake within a more balanced range.

What the science says

European and American regulators have thoroughly evaluated this substance. The FDA permits its use as a substance generally recognised as safe under federal regulations. However, a comprehensive re-evaluation by EFSA in 2017 led to the introduction of a strict daily limit. Scientists observed that high dietary exposure from multiple food sources could exceed this threshold, particularly in children and individuals who regularly eat processed foods. No toxicological concerns were found at standard levels, but researchers continue to monitor cumulative exposure across different age groups.

Where you'll find it

You will find this additive in highly savoury processed foods, including instant noodles, potato crisps, seasoned snacks, canned soups, gravy granules, and ready meals. On ingredient labels, it is typically listed by its plain name as glutamic acid or under its European code, E620. On US labels it is listed as glutamic acid. It is often grouped with other glutamate additives, so checking the ingredients list for these specific terms helps you identify when a product relies on added enhancers for its savoury taste.
