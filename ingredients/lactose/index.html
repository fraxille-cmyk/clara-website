Lactose

Processed ingredientNeutral

Diets

✕ Not vegan✕ Not low-FODMAP
  • Not vegan — lactose comes from milk.
  • Lactose is not low-FODMAP.

Lactose is the natural sugar found in milk, used by manufacturers to add mild sweetness, improve texture, and help foods brown beautifully.

What it is

Lactose is a natural sugar found exclusively in the milk of mammals. Chemically, it is a disaccharide, which means it is made of two simpler sugars, glucose and galactose, bound together. It is much less sweet than regular table sugar, possessing only about a fifth of its sweetness. Because of this mild profile, it provides body and texture to foods without making them cloying, acting as a familiar dairy component that our bodies are generally accustomed to processing from infancy.

How it's made

To produce lactose on a commercial scale, manufacturers start with liquid whey, which is the natural liquid left over during the cheese-making process. This whey is filtered to concentrate the lactose and remove proteins and minerals. The concentrated liquid is then crystallised by cooling it down under controlled conditions. These crystals are separated from the remaining liquid, washed thoroughly to ensure purity, and dried into a fine, free-flowing white powder that is easy to store and blend into recipes.

Why manufacturers use it

Food producers value lactose because it performs several technical roles that table sugar cannot match. It absorbs moisture poorly, which helps dry mixes and powders stay free-flowing without clumping. When heated, it reacts beautifully with proteins to create a rich brown colour and appealing aroma, making it excellent for baked goods. It also adds a pleasant mouthfeel and carries other flavours and colours evenly throughout a product, all while keeping the overall sweetness level balanced and subtle.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this NEUTRAL. As a natural milk sugar rather than an additive, it does not carry a risk tier. For most people, it is a normal part of a balanced diet. However, many individuals do not produce enough of the digestive enzyme lactase, which is needed to break this sugar down in the small intestine. When undigested lactose reaches the lower digestive tract, it can draw in water and be fermented by resident bacteria, leading to temporary digestive discomfort.

What the science says

Regulators view lactose as a standard food ingredient rather than an additive, so no daily intake limit is established. The European Food Safety Authority has evaluated lactose in the context of dietary reference values and health claims, confirming its role in calcium absorption and energy metabolism. Because it is a natural constituent of milk, scientific assessments focus primarily on helping consumers identify its presence on labels rather than setting safety thresholds, as its effects are well understood and managed through dietary choices.

Where you'll find it

You will find this ingredient listed simply as lactose or lactose powder on ingredient lists. It is a common addition to baked goods like breads and biscuits, powdered soups, sauces, seasonings, processed meats, and infant formulas. It is also frequently used as a carrier for flavourings or as a bulking agent in sweetener tablets. Because it is derived from milk, it is always highlighted in the allergen information on packaging to assist those who need to avoid dairy products.
