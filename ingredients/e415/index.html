Xanthan gum (E415)

E415AdditiveNeutral · limited concern

Xanthan gum is a common plant-based thickener and stabiliser used to give foods a smooth, consistent texture and keep ingredients from separating.

What it is

Xanthan gum is a soluble fibre used to thicken and stabilise foods. Chemically, it is a complex carbohydrate, much like the natural starches found in plants. It behaves like a sponge in water, swelling up to create a thick, gel-like structure. While it has no nutritional value of its own because our bodies cannot digest it, it is highly valued in cooking and food production for its unique ability to hold liquids together and create a smooth, satisfying texture in a wide variety of everyday products.

How it's made

This ingredient is produced through a natural fermentation process, similar to how yoghurt or beer is made. Manufacturers feed a specific bacterium, called Xanthomonas campestris, with simple sugars derived from corn, wheat, or soy. As the bacteria ferment the sugar, they create a sticky, fibrous broth. This mixture is then sterilised to kill the bacteria, precipitated using an alcohol wash, filtered, and dried into a fine, cream-coloured powder that dissolves easily when mixed into liquids.

Why manufacturers use it

Food makers rely on xanthan gum because it is an incredibly efficient stabiliser. It keeps oil and water from separating, which is why salad dressings stay mixed in the bottle. It also gives liquids a thick consistency when resting, yet allows them to flow easily when poured or squeezed, a property that is perfect for tomato ketchup. Additionally, it mimics the elastic texture of gluten, making it an essential ingredient for binding and adding structure to gluten-free baked goods.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this NEUTRAL, a limited-concern additive. Because it is a soluble fibre that passes through the human digestive system without being absorbed, it is generally considered very gentle on the body. EFSA has re-evaluated xanthan gum and confirmed that it is safe for the general population, establishing that no limit is required for its daily intake. In very large quantities, it can sometimes cause mild digestive changes, but the tiny amounts used in everyday food products are not a cause for concern.

What the science says

Major food safety authorities have thoroughly evaluated xanthan gum over several decades. JECFA first assessed its safety in the twentieth century and established that no daily intake limit is necessary. More recently, EFSA reviewed all available dietary and safety data, confirming that the additive does not pose safety concerns at the levels currently found in our food. Because it is not absorbed by the body, scientific interest focuses mainly on how it interacts with our natural gut microbes, which can break it down into short-chain fatty acids.

Where you'll find it

You will find this ingredient in a vast range of supermarket items, including salad dressings, sauces, gluten-free bread, ice cream, and ready meals. On ingredient labels, it is usually listed by its plain name as xanthan gum, or sometimes by its European code, E415. It is also common in vegan products where it replaces the binding properties of eggs, and in low-fat spreads where it helps to recreate the rich mouthfeel of traditional fats.
