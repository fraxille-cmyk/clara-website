Patent blue V (E131)

E131AdditiveAvoid · moderate concern

Diets

✓ Suits every diet Clara checks — as an ingredient

Patent blue V is a synthetic blue food colour that Clara advises you to avoid because it can trigger severe allergic reactions in sensitised people.

What it is

Patent blue V is a synthetic food colour that delivers a deep, vibrant blue shade. Chemically, it is a coal tar dye, which means its molecular structure is derived from hydrocarbons originally sourced from coal processing. It is not something you would find in nature, unlike plant-based blues like spirulina. In the food world, it is used to give products an intense, eye-catching hue that does not fade easily when exposed to light or heat, making it a popular choice for processed items that need to look bright on the shelf.

How it's made

This colour is manufactured entirely in a laboratory through a complex chemical synthesis. The process begins with petroleum-derived or coal-tar-derived hydrocarbons, which undergo a series of chemical reactions, including condensation and oxidation, to build the specific dye molecule. The resulting substance is then purified and isolated as a sodium or calcium salt. Because it is a purely synthetic creation, there are no agricultural raw materials involved in its production, and the final powder is highly concentrated and chemically stable.

Why manufacturers use it

Food manufacturers choose this dye because it is incredibly efficient and stable. Natural blue pigments are notoriously difficult to work with, as they tend to degrade quickly when exposed to acidic environments, high temperatures, or bright supermarket lighting. This synthetic alternative maintains its vivid colour throughout a long shelf life. It is also highly concentrated, meaning manufacturers only need to use a tiny amount to achieve the desired shade, which keeps production costs low and ensures consistent appearance across different batches of food.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this AVOID, a moderate-concern additive. The primary reason for this rating is that the dye can trigger allergic reactions, including severe systemic reactions like anaphylaxis, in sensitised individuals. While many people can consume it without experiencing immediate symptoms, the potential for these sudden and severe allergic responses makes it an ingredient to watch out for. EFSA has established a daily limit of 5 mg per kg of body weight, but the risk of sensitisation remains a notable concern for susceptible shoppers.

What the science says

European regulators have evaluated this colour to ensure it meets safety standards, but they highlight specific sensitivities. EFSA re-evaluated the dye and noted that while it is not considered a concern for genetic toxicity, it is known to cause allergic reactions in some people. The US FDA has never authorised this substance for food use, meaning it is not permitted in American food products. Scientific assessments focus on these allergic responses, and because of these reactions, researchers continue to monitor how sensitised individuals react to even small dietary exposures.

Where you'll find it

You will typically find this colour in highly processed foods that require a bright blue or green shade. It is common in confectionery, lollipops, gelatin desserts, ice cream, sports drinks, and cake decorations. On ingredient labels in the United Kingdom and Europe, it is usually listed by its name, patent blue V, or by its E-number, E131. Because it is not authorised for food use in the United States, you will not see it on American food labels, where manufacturers must use alternative blue colourings instead.
