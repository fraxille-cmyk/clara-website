Patent blue V (E131)

E131AdditiveAvoid · limited concern

Patent blue V is a synthetic coal-tar dye used to give foods a deep blue colour, but Clara advises avoiding it due to potential sensitivity reactions.

What it is

Patent blue V is a synthetic dark blue food dye that belongs to the triphenylmethane family of colourings. Unlike natural pigments derived from plants or minerals, this substance is entirely artificial and does not occur in nature. It is known for producing a very intense, vivid blue shade that remains stable under various processing conditions, making it a popular choice for creating eye-catching hues in processed foods where natural colours would quickly fade or change.

How it's made

This colouring is manufactured through a complex chemical synthesis process starting from petroleum-derived hydrocarbons. Specifically, it is produced by condensing diethylaminobenzaldehyde with disulfonic acids, followed by oxidation to yield the final sodium or calcium salt of the dye. The resulting powder is highly soluble in water and can be easily blended with other synthetic dyes to create various shades of green and violet for industrial food production.

Why manufacturers use it

Food manufacturers choose this additive primarily because it provides a highly stable, uniform, and vibrant blue colour at a very low cost. Natural blue pigments are notoriously difficult to extract and tend to degrade rapidly when exposed to heat, light, or acidic environments. This synthetic alternative resists these processing stresses perfectly, allowing products to maintain their bright appearance on supermarket shelves for months. It is also frequently mixed with yellow dyes to create consistent green shades.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this AVOID, a limited-concern additive. While it is approved for use in the European Union, some individuals may experience sensitivity reactions after consuming it. The European Food Safety Authority has established an acceptable daily intake of five milligrams per kilogram of body weight. Because synthetic dyes are not essential for the nutritional quality or preservation of food, and because of the potential for mild allergic-like responses in sensitive people, choosing products without this colour is a sensible precaution.

What the science says

The safety of this colour has been evaluated by several international bodies. The European Food Safety Authority re-evaluated the substance in a scientific opinion, confirming its current daily limit based on studies of how the body absorbs and excretes the compound. Most of the ingested dye passes through the digestive system without being absorbed, but researchers continue to monitor its potential to trigger histamine release in sensitive individuals. No international regulator classifies this substance as a high-risk compound, but restrictions on its use vary globally.

Where you'll find it

You will find this additive in highly processed items such as confectionery, jellies, ice cream, lollipops, and certain carbonated soft drinks. It is also used to colour the shells of scotch eggs and in some cake decorations. On ingredient labels, it is typically declared by its name, patent blue V, or by its European code, E131, and it is sometimes listed as patent blue violet in older formulations.
