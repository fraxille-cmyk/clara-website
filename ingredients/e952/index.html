Cyclamates (E952)

E952SweetenerAvoid · limited concern

Cyclamates are calorie-free artificial sweeteners that Clara rates AVOID due to potential health concerns and their tendency to trigger gut discomfort.

What it is

Cyclamates are a family of synthetic compounds used to sweeten foods without adding sugar or calories. Discovered by accident in a university laboratory in the nineteen-thirties, they are about thirty times sweeter than regular table sugar. Unlike some other low-calorie sweeteners, they have a clean taste profile that remains stable under heat, making them highly versatile in processed foods. They are often blended with other artificial sweeteners to mask bitter aftertastes and mimic the natural sweetness of sugar more closely.

How it's made

These sweeteners are produced through chemical synthesis in industrial facilities. The process begins with cyclohexylamine, an organic compound derived from petroleum. This starting material is reacted with either sulphamic acid or sulphur trioxide, followed by neutralisation with sodium hydroxide or calcium hydroxide. This reaction yields sodium cyclamate or calcium cyclamate, which are then crystallised, purified, and dried into the white, odourless powder that food manufacturers purchase.

Why manufacturers use it

Food manufacturers choose these compounds because they are highly stable during baking, pasteurisation, and long-term storage. They do not break down under high temperatures or in acidic environments, unlike some delicate natural sweeteners. From a commercial standpoint, they are incredibly cheap to produce and use. By blending them with saccharin, manufacturers can create a intense sweetness at a fraction of the cost of sugar, while also appealing to shoppers looking for low-calorie or sugar-free options.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this AVOID, a limited-concern additive. While some regulators permit its use, there are ongoing discussions about its long-term effects on the body, and it is known to act as a trigger for individuals with sensitive digestive systems, causing bloating and abdominal discomfort. The US FDA banned cyclamates in the late nineteen-sixties, whereas the European Union permits them in specific food categories with strict maximum limits. No universal consensus exists, but Clara advises caution due to these potential gut sensitivities and historical regulatory disagreements.

What the science says

Scientific bodies have evaluated these compounds repeatedly over several decades. JECFA established an acceptable daily intake of eleven milligrams per kilogram of body weight, a limit that EFSA also supports. The primary focus of modern research is how the human gut metabolises the compound, as some individuals convert it into cyclohexylamine, a substance that the body processes differently. Because of these variations in individual metabolism and the potential for digestive irritation, researchers continue to monitor its consumption patterns across different populations.

Where you'll find it

You will find this ingredient in sugar-free carbonated drinks, diet squashes, low-calorie jams, canned fruits, and tabletop sweeteners. On the packaging, it is typically declared in the ingredient list as cyclamates, sodium cyclamate, calcium cyclamate, or simply by its European code, E952. It is frequently paired with saccharin or aspartame in light or zero-sugar versions of popular beverages and gelatin desserts.
