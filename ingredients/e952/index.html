Cyclamates (E952)

E952SweetenerAvoid · high concern

Diets

✓ Suits every diet Clara checks — as an ingredient

Cyclamates are synthetic sweeteners banned in the US but allowed in the EU; Clara rates this AVOID due to the US ban.

What it is

Cyclamates are a family of synthetic intense sweeteners that are about thirty times sweeter than regular table sugar. Discovered by accident in a university laboratory, they include cyclamic acid and its sodium and calcium salts. Unlike sugar, they do not provide energy or raise glucose levels in the body, making them a popular choice for sweetening products without adding calories. They are often combined with other artificial sweeteners to mask bitter aftertastes.

How it's made

These sweeteners are produced entirely in a laboratory through a chemical reaction. The process begins with cyclohexylamine, a synthetic compound derived from petroleum. This compound is reacted with either sulphamic acid or sulphur trioxide to form cyclamic acid. Manufacturers then neutralise this acid with sodium hydroxide or calcium hydroxide to create the highly soluble sodium or calcium cyclamate salts that are used in food production.

Why manufacturers use it

Food manufacturers choose cyclamates because they are highly stable under heat and across a wide range of acidity levels, which makes them ideal for baked goods and pasteurised beverages. They also have a clean, sweet profile that blends exceptionally well with other low-calorie sweeteners. Commercially, they are incredibly cheap to produce and use, allowing companies to manufacture diet products with a long shelf life at a fraction of the cost of using real sugar.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this AVOID, a high-concern additive. This rating is because the FDA banned cyclamate in food in 1970, a decision confirmed in later reviews, while the European Scientific Committee on Food established a daily limit of 7 mg per kg of body weight in 2000. IARC classifies cyclamates as Group 3, not classifiable as to its carcinogenicity to humans. Because of these conflicting regulatory decisions and the long-standing American ban, Clara advises choosing safer alternatives.

What the science says

International authorities have evaluated these compounds extensively over several decades. While some regulators concluded that the evidence did not justify a complete ban, others remained cautious about how the substance is metabolised in the human gut, where some individuals convert it into cyclohexylamine, a compound that raised concerns in animal studies. The European Scientific Committee on Food set a strict acceptable daily intake to ensure a wide margin of safety, but the US FDA has maintained its ban, citing a lack of absolute proof of safety.

Where you'll find it

You will find these sweeteners in low-calorie soft drinks, sugar-free sweets, diet jams, and tabletop sweeteners outside the United States. On ingredient lists in the United Kingdom and Europe, they are typically declared as cyclamates, cyclamic acid, or by their collective additive number, E952. On labels in countries where they are permitted, they may also appear under the specific names sodium cyclamate or calcium cyclamate.
