Sodium malates (E350)

E350AdditiveNeutral · benign

Diets

✓ Suits every diet Clara checks — as an ingredient

Sodium malates are benign acidity regulators made from the natural acid found in apples, used to balance tartness and keep food fresh.

What it is

Sodium malates are the sodium salts of malic acid, which is the natural organic compound that gives green apples and rhubarb their characteristic sharp, tart bite. In the food world, these salts act as acidity regulators and humectants, helping to control the sourness of a product while keeping its moisture levels stable. They are closely related to the natural acids our bodies metabolise every single day during normal energy production, making them highly compatible with human biology.

How it's made

Although malic acid occurs widely in fresh fruit, extracting it from apples for industrial use is not commercially viable. Instead, manufacturers produce sodium malates synthetically. They start by chemically synthesising malic acid from petroleum-derived compounds, then neutralise this acid with a sodium source, such as sodium hydroxide or sodium carbonate. The resulting white, crystalline powder is easily dissolved in water and can be added directly to food formulations.

Why manufacturers use it

Food makers rely on these salts to fine-tune the acidity of their products. Unlike pure malic acid, which can deliver a sharp, sudden sourness, sodium malates offer a more buffered, mellow tartness that lingers pleasantly. They also help maintain a stable pH level, which prevents spoilage and keeps colours from fading. Additionally, their ability to bind water helps prevent dry foods from absorbing moisture from the air and stops moist foods from drying out.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this NEUTRAL, a benign additive. Because sodium malates are simply the sodium salts of a common fruit acid that our cells process naturally, they do not pose a risk to human health. EFSA re-evaluated these compounds and confirmed that no limit is established for their daily intake, reflecting their high level of safety. The only practical consideration is that they contain sodium, which contributes to the overall salt content of a packaged food.

What the science says

Major food safety authorities have thoroughly evaluated sodium malates and found no safety concerns. EFSA published a joint re-evaluation of malic acid and its salts, concluding that they do not present any safety risks and requiring no numerical acceptable daily intake limit. In the United States, the FDA permits their use and designates sodium malate as generally recognised as safe. Because the body processes these salts exactly like the malic acid found in fresh fruit, they are considered entirely non-problematic.

Where you'll find it

You will commonly find this ingredient in processed foods that require a balanced, fruity tartness or a stable moisture level. It frequently appears in confectionery, jams, jellies, soft drinks, canned vegetables, and bakery products. On ingredient lists, it is typically declared by its name as sodium malates, sodium malate, or under its European code, E350. On US labels, it is often listed simply as sodium malate.
