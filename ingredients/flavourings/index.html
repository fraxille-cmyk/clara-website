Flavourings

FlavouringNeutral · moderate concern

A single word on the label that covers a blend of secret flavour compounds, solvents, and carriers, making it impossible to know exactly what you are eating.

What it is

Flavourings are ingredients added to food to give it a specific taste or smell. Unlike whole ingredients like strawberries or vanilla pods, these are concentrated mixtures of chemical compounds. They are designed to mimic natural tastes or create entirely new sensory experiences. When a label simply lists this term, it refers to a proprietary recipe that can contain dozens of individual chemical components, along with hidden substances used to dissolve or stabilise them, none of which have to be declared individually on the packaging.

How it's made

These ingredients are created by blending various aroma compounds in a laboratory or industrial facility. The individual components can be synthesised from petrochemicals or derived from coal tar, wood pulp, or other raw materials through complex chemical reactions. Technicians combine these active flavour chemicals with carrier solvents, such as propylene glycol or vegetable oils, and emulsifiers to ensure the mixture disperses evenly. Because the final blend is considered proprietary commercial information, the exact manufacturing steps for any specific taste remain a closely guarded secret.

Why manufacturers use it

Food manufacturers rely on these mixtures because they are incredibly cheap, highly concentrated, and completely stable. Natural ingredients like real fruit juices or spices vary in taste depending on the season, degrade quickly during factory processing, and cost far more to source. Synthetic mixtures deliver an intense, identical taste profile in every single batch, surviving high heat and long storage times. They allow a factory to make a plain starch base taste like ripe summer berries or smoky barbecue at a fraction of the cost of the real thing.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this NEUTRAL, a moderate-concern additive. The main issue is that the single word on the label acts as a blanket term, hiding the exact chemical compounds, solvents, and carriers used in the blend. While regulators assess individual flavouring substances for safety, shoppers cannot know which specific chemicals are in a product. Manufacturers who use ingredients derived from natural sources almost always state that clearly on the packaging, so a generic listing usually points to synthetic chemicals. Because Clara cannot verify what is in this specific blend, it is best treated as a question mark.

What the science says

Regulators like EFSA and the FDA maintain positive lists of thousands of individual flavouring substances that are permitted in food. EFSA has systematically re-evaluated these compounds to ensure they do not damage genetic material or accumulate in human tissues. However, these assessments look at chemicals in isolation, rather than the complex mixtures consumed in everyday diets. No daily intake limit is established for the generic category itself because the composition changes completely from one product to another, leaving the long-term biological impact of these proprietary mixtures largely unstudied.

Where you'll find it

You will find this ingredient in almost every category of processed food, including soft drinks, crisps, yoghurts, biscuits, ready meals, and confectionery. On the packaging, it can appear under many different names depending on where the product was made, including flavour, flavouring, flavours, flavourings, aroma, aromas, aromes, aromi, or aromen. It is rarely accompanied by an E-number, as these mixtures are classified separately from standard food additives.
