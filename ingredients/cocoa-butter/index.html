Cocoa butter

Processed ingredientNeutral

Cocoa butter is a natural fat pressed from cocoa beans that gives chocolate its smooth texture and rich mouthfeel.

What it is

Cocoa butter is the natural cream-coloured fat extracted directly from the seeds of the cacao tree. These seeds, which we commonly call cocoa beans, are the very same ones used to make chocolate. Chemically, this fat is a blend of saturated and monounsaturated fatty acids, mostly stearic, palmitic, and oleic acids. Unlike animal fats, it has a unique crystalline structure that allows it to remain solid at room temperature but melt smoothly at just below human body temperature. This characteristic gives chocolate its signature snap and velvety texture.

How it's made

The journey starts with harvesting the pods of the cacao tree to extract the beans. These beans undergo fermentation and drying to develop their characteristic flavour before being roasted and cracked open to separate the nibs from the shells. Manufacturers grind these nibs into a rich paste known as cocoa liquor. This liquor is then subjected to immense physical pressure in hydraulic presses, which squeezes out the pure, golden fat. The remaining solid press cake is ground into cocoa powder, while the extracted liquid fat is filtered and cooled to solidify into cocoa butter.

Why manufacturers use it

Food makers rely on cocoa butter because of its remarkable melting properties and stability. It melts precisely at thirty-four degrees Celsius, which is why chocolate stays solid in your hand but melts instantly and smoothly in your mouth. It also acts as a natural structuring agent, helping chocolate hold its shape and preventing the greyish fat bloom that can occur when temperature fluctuates. Commercially, its high resistance to oxidation means it has a long shelf life, helping products stay fresh without the need for artificial preservatives.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this NEUTRAL. As a natural fat extracted from cocoa beans, it is a benign ingredient that is primarily composed of saturated and monounsaturated fats. It is not an additive, so no daily intake limit is established for it. While it is high in saturated fat, which should be consumed in moderation as part of a balanced diet, it does not carry the risks associated with chemically altered or hydrogenated fats. It is digested by the body in the same way as other natural dietary fats.

What the science says

Regulators have evaluated cocoa butter as a standard food ingredient rather than a food additive. The FDA classifies cocoa butter as generally recognised as safe under its GRAS regulations, permitting its use in food without specific limits beyond good manufacturing practice. Because it is a natural component of chocolate, its safety is well-established through centuries of traditional consumption. Scientific assessments focus on its fatty acid profile, noting that its stearic acid content has a neutral effect on cholesterol levels compared to other saturated fatty acids.

Where you'll find it

You will find this ingredient in almost all high-quality chocolates, including milk, dark, and white chocolate, where it may be listed as cocoa butter or beurre de cacao. It also appears in chocolate-coated biscuits, confectionery bars, chocolate spreads, and premium baked goods. Because it does not have an E-number, it is always declared by its plain name on ingredient labels, sometimes alongside other vegetable fats that manufacturers use to supplement it in cheaper products.
