Talc (E553b)

E553bAdditiveAvoid · high concern

Talc is a mineral powder used to stop food from sticking, but IARC classifies it as Group 2A, probably carcinogenic to humans, so Clara says avoid it.

What it is

Talc is a naturally occurring mineral composed of hydrated magnesium silicate. In its pure form, it is the softest known mineral on Earth, famous for its silky texture and its ability to absorb moisture and reduce friction. While most people associate it with cosmetic powders, a highly refined, food-grade version is used in the food industry to keep dry ingredients flowing smoothly and to prevent sticky confectionery from clumping together during packaging.

How it's made

Talc is mined from deep underground mineral deposits where magnesium-rich rocks have undergone geological transformation over millions of years. Once extracted, the raw ore is crushed, washed, and sorted to remove impurities. For food-grade use, the mineral must undergo extremely rigorous purification and milling processes to ensure it is completely free from asbestos fibres, which can naturally co-occur with talc in the earth. The final product is an exceptionally fine, bright white powder.

Why manufacturers use it

Food manufacturers value talc because it is an exceptionally effective anti-caking agent and release agent. It coats the surface of food particles, preventing them from absorbing moisture and sticking to one another or to processing machinery. It is highly stable, does not react with other ingredients, and has no taste or odour. This makes it a very cheap and reliable choice for dusting chewing gum, tableting dietary supplements, and keeping shredded cheese or powdered mixes flowing freely through factory funnels.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this AVOID, a high-concern additive. This rating is because IARC classifies talc as Group 2A, probably carcinogenic to humans. While EFSA re-evaluated talc in 2018 and did not establish a specific numerical daily limit due to low oral absorption, the international classification by IARC raises significant safety concerns for consumers. Because of this evaluation, Clara advises choosing products that use alternative, lower-risk anti-caking agents instead of this mineral powder.

What the science says

The safety of talc has been evaluated by several major food authorities. The FDA permits its use as a generally recognised as safe substance, and EFSA concluded in its 2018 review that talc is not absorbed by the gut to any significant extent. However, the scientific consensus shifted significantly when the IARC published its updated monograph, which evaluated the overall evidence and classified the substance in Group 2A. This classification reflects a high level of concern regarding exposure, prompting a more cautious approach to its presence in everyday consumer products.

Where you'll find it

You will find talc in a variety of dry or sticky foods, including chewing gum, boiled sweets, chocolate decorations, powdered seasonings, and dietary supplements in tablet form. On ingredient labels in the United Kingdom and Europe, it is typically listed as talc or by its E-number, E 553b. On US labels, it is permitted as an anti-caking agent and is simply listed as talc.
