Calcium inosinate (E633)

E633AdditiveNeutral · benign

Calcium inosinate is a savoury flavour enhancer that Clara rates as neutral because it is a benign additive with no established daily intake limit.

What it is

Calcium inosinate is a calcium salt of inosinic acid, a substance found naturally in animal tissues like meat and fish. It belongs to a family of compounds that help create the rich, savoury taste known as umami. While it occurs naturally in many foods, the version used in packaged products is a purified substance. It works in harmony with other savoury compounds, boosting the depth of flavours so that a product tastes more satisfying without needing excessive amounts of salt or other seasonings.

How it's made

To produce this ingredient on a commercial scale, manufacturers typically start with a fermentation process. Microorganisms, such as specific strains of bacteria or yeast, are grown in a nutrient-rich broth where they produce guanylic and inosinic acids. Once the fermentation is complete, the mixture is purified, and calcium ions are introduced to neutralise the acid. This reaction forms a stable, crystalline salt that can be easily dried, stored, and blended into dry food mixtures.

Why manufacturers use it

Food makers use this ingredient as a highly effective flavour enhancer. It is particularly valued because it works synergistically with glutamates, which are other savoury compounds. When combined, they multiply the savoury effect far more than either could do alone. This allows manufacturers to create intense, meaty, or brothy flavours in processed foods while using less sodium. It is a cost-effective way to maintain a robust taste profile in shelf-stable products that might otherwise lose their flavour during processing.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this NEUTRAL, a benign additive. It is processed by the body in the same way as naturally occurring dietary purines, which are normal components of our food. Because of its low toxicity, EFSA established that no numerical acceptable daily intake is specified. This means it is considered safe for the general population at the levels typically found in food. However, because it breaks down into purines, individuals who are advised to limit their purine intake may choose to be mindful of its presence.

What the science says

Scientific bodies have thoroughly evaluated this substance and its related compounds. The European Food Safety Authority completed a full re-evaluation of inosinic acid and its salts, concluding that they raise no safety concerns at current use levels. Because the body metabolises these compounds into uric acid, regulators have looked closely at dietary exposure and confirmed that the small amounts added to food do not pose a risk to the general public. No limit is established for daily intake.

Where you'll find it

You will commonly find this ingredient in highly savoury, processed foods such as instant noodles, potato crisps, canned soups, snack seasonings, and pre-packaged sauces. On a label, it is usually listed by its full name as calcium inosinate, or by its European code E633. On US labels it is listed as calcium 5'-inosinate. It is almost always paired with other flavour enhancers, so you will often see it listed alongside yeast extract or monosodium glutamate.
