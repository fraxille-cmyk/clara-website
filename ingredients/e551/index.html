Silicon dioxide (E551)

E551AdditiveAvoid · moderate concern

Silicon dioxide is a mineral anti-caking agent that Clara rates AVOID because European regulators cannot fully confirm its safety due to nanoparticle concerns.

What it is

Silicon dioxide is a naturally occurring compound made of silicon and oxygen, which are two of the most abundant elements in the earth's crust. In nature, it is most commonly found as quartz or sand. In the food industry, a highly purified, synthetic amorphous form is used. It is a fine, light white powder that does not dissolve in water and is chemically very stable, meaning it does not react easily with other ingredients in a food package.

How it's made

To make the food-grade ingredient, manufacturers do not simply grind up sand. Instead, they produce it synthetically through a chemical process. This usually involves reacting a sodium silicate solution with a mineral acid, such as sulphuric acid, under carefully controlled conditions. This reaction creates a precipitate, which is then filtered, thoroughly washed to remove any salts, dried, and milled into an extremely fine powder with a very high surface area.

Why manufacturers use it

Food manufacturers rely on this ingredient primarily as an anti-caking agent to keep powdered foods flowing smoothly. Because the powder has an incredibly high surface area, it acts like a microscopic sponge, absorbing moisture and oils that would otherwise cause powders to clump together. It also coats the individual particles of other ingredients, reducing friction so they slide past each other easily. This makes it invaluable for high-speed factory packaging and for ensuring a consistent pour at home.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this AVOID, a moderate-concern additive. While it has been used for decades, the European Food Safety Authority concluded in 2018 that its safety could not be fully confirmed because of a lack of data regarding particle size distribution and potential nano-specific toxicity. A portion of this synthetic powder consists of nanoparticles, which are so small they may behave differently in the body than larger particles. IARC classifies silica in its amorphous form as Group 3, not classifiable as to its carcinogenicity to humans.

What the science says

Regulators have evaluated this substance several times to determine how it behaves after it is swallowed. While much of it passes through the body undigested, scientists are increasingly focused on the smallest nanoparticles within the powder. These tiny particles can potentially cross biological barriers, and researchers want to understand if they accumulate in organs over time. Because of these open questions about particle size, EFSA has not established a daily limit, noting that more precise data on how these nanoparticles interact with our tissues is still needed.

Where you'll find it

You will frequently spot this ingredient in dry, powdered products that need to remain free-flowing, such as instant soups, gravy mixes, spices, powdered creamer, and table salt. On ingredient lists, it is typically declared by its plain name as silicon dioxide, but you may also see it listed under its European code, E551. On US labels, it is permitted as a direct food additive under the name silicon dioxide, where its use is limited to a maximum of two percent by weight of the food.
