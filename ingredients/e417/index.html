Tara gum (E417)

E417AdditiveNeutral · benign

Tara gum is a natural plant-based thickener made from the seeds of a South American shrub, rated neutral by Clara as a benign additive.

What it is

Tara gum is a natural soluble fibre that comes from the seeds of the tara bush, a spiny shrub native to the Peruvian Andes. Chemically, it is a galactomannan, which is a type of complex carbohydrate very similar to other familiar plant gums like guar gum and locust bean gum. It sits right between those two in its structure, offering a smooth texture without being too sticky. Because it is a soluble fibre, it passes through the upper digestive system largely unchanged, acting as a source of nourishment for the friendly bacteria in the lower gut.

How it's made

The production of this ingredient is entirely mechanical and thermal, keeping the plant fibres intact without chemical modification. First, the pods of the tara bush are harvested and split open to release the small seeds. The seeds are then heated to loosen their tough outer hulls. Once the hulls and germ are mechanically separated, the remaining nutrient-rich portion, called the endosperm, is milled into a fine, pale-yellow powder. This powder is then sifted and packaged, ready to be dissolved in water where it naturally swells into a thick gel.

Why manufacturers use it

Food makers choose this ingredient because it is an exceptionally efficient thickener and stabiliser that works well in both hot and cold foods. It creates a smooth, creamy texture in low-fat products, mimicking the mouthfeel of real fat without adding calories. It also prevents ice crystals from forming in frozen desserts during storage and keeps moisture from separating out of sauces and dressings. Because it works in harmony with other plant gums, manufacturers can use smaller amounts of it to achieve the exact thickness they want, saving production costs.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this NEUTRAL, a benign additive. It has a long history of safe use as a natural plant gum, and it is not absorbed by the body as a sugar or fat. Because it behaves like any other dietary fibre, it simply passes through your digestive tract. EFSA re-evaluated the ingredient and confirmed there is no safety concern, meaning no daily limit is established. It is well-tolerated by the human body, though like all soluble fibres, consuming it in extremely large quantities could lead to temporary bloating or mild gas.

What the science says

Independent scientific bodies have thoroughly evaluated this plant gum and found no reason for concern. EFSA reviewed the ingredient in its scientific opinion of 2017 and concluded that its use in foods does not raise any safety concerns, maintaining its status of no specified daily limit. JECFA also evaluated the substance and reached a similar conclusion regarding its safety. Because it is a complex carbohydrate, studies show it is fermented by gut microflora in the large intestine into beneficial short-chain fatty acids, behaving exactly like the fibre found in everyday vegetables.

Where you'll find it

You will find this ingredient in a wide variety of processed foods that require a stable, creamy texture. It is common in ice creams, low-fat yoghurts, dairy desserts, salad dressings, sauces, and gluten-free baked goods where it helps mimic the binding properties of gluten. On ingredient lists, it is typically declared by its plain name as tara gum, but you might also see it listed by its European code, E417, or simply as E 417 on some imported labels.
