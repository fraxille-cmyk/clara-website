Sodium ascorbate (E301)

E301AdditiveNeutral · benign

Diets

✓ Suits every diet Clara checks — as an ingredient

Sodium ascorbate is a synthetic form of vitamin C used to keep food looking fresh, and Clara rates it as a benign additive.

What it is

Sodium ascorbate is a mineral salt of ascorbic acid, which is more commonly known as vitamin C. It combines vitamin C with sodium, resulting in a buffered compound that is less acidic than pure ascorbic acid. In the human body, it is absorbed and metabolised just like the vitamin C we get from eating fresh citrus fruits, providing a source of this essential nutrient while performing its technical role in packaged food.

How it's made

To make this ingredient, manufacturers start with ascorbic acid, which is typically produced through a multi-step fermentation and chemical synthesis process starting from natural corn starch. This acid is then dissolved in water and reacted with a sodium source, such as sodium bicarbonate or sodium hydroxide. The mixture is carefully neutralised, crystallised, and dried into a fine white or slightly yellowish powder that dissolves easily in liquids.

Why manufacturers use it

Food makers use this ingredient primarily as an antioxidant to preserve the colour and flavour of foods during storage. When exposed to oxygen, foods can turn brown, lose their appealing taste, or spoil. Sodium ascorbate reacts with oxygen first, shielding the food from oxidation. It is especially popular in cured meats because it speeds up the curing process, helps maintain a stable pink colour, and prevents the formation of unwanted compounds during high-heat cooking.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this NEUTRAL, a benign additive. Because it is a simple salt of vitamin C, the body processes it naturally and efficiently. EFSA re-evaluated the substance and determined that no limit is established for its daily intake, meaning it is considered safe for the general population even in high amounts. It does contribute a small amount of dietary sodium, which is worth noting only for individuals who are carefully monitoring their overall salt intake.

What the science says

Major food safety authorities have thoroughly evaluated this substance and found no reason for concern. EFSA concluded in its assessment that the additive does not pose any safety risks at the levels currently used in food. The FDA also recognises it as generally safe for use as a chemical preservative. Because it behaves identically to natural vitamin C once digested, scientific consensus views it as a highly predictable and well-tolerated ingredient.

Where you'll find it

You will frequently find this ingredient in cured and processed meats like bacon, ham, hot dogs, and deli slices, as well as in canned vegetables, fruit juices, and bakery products. On ingredient labels, it is typically listed by its plain name as sodium ascorbate, or under its European code as E301, and on US labels it is listed as sodium ascorbate.
