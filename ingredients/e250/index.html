Sodium nitrite (E250)

E250AdditiveAvoid · high concern

Sodium nitrite is a preservative used to cure meats, but it can form harmful compounds in the body, leading Clara to recommend avoiding it.

What it is

Sodium nitrite is a mineral salt that acts as a powerful preservative and colour fixer. In its pure form, it is a pale yellowish crystalline powder that dissolves easily in water. While nitrites occur naturally in small amounts in some vegetables, the concentrated version used in food processing is specifically designed to keep cured meats looking fresh and to stop dangerous microbes from multiplying. It is a highly reactive compound that interacts directly with the proteins and iron in meat.

How it's made

To manufacture this ingredient on a commercial scale, chemical producers react a mixture of nitrogen oxides with sodium hydroxide or sodium carbonate. This chemical reaction yields a liquid solution of sodium nitrite, which is then purified, crystallised, and dried into a fine powder. Because pure sodium nitrite is highly potent, manufacturers almost always mix it with regular table salt before selling it to food processors, creating what is commonly known as curing salt.

Why manufacturers use it

Food manufacturers rely on this additive because it performs three distinct roles that are difficult to replicate with other ingredients. First, it reacts with meat proteins to create a stable pink colour, keeping processed meats from turning an unappealing grey. Second, it adds a distinct cured flavour that consumers expect. Finally, it is an incredibly effective preservative that stops the growth of spoilage organisms, extending the shelf life of packaged meats and keeping them stable during transport.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this AVOID, a high-concern additive. When sodium nitrite is exposed to high heat or acidic conditions in the stomach, it can react with protein fragments to form compounds called nitrosamines. The IARC classifies ingested nitrate or nitrite under conditions that result in endogenous nitrosation as Group 2A, probably carcinogenic to humans. Because of this chemical transformation and its impact on the digestive tract, EFSA maintains a strict daily limit of 0.07 mg per kg of body weight.

What the science says

Major food safety authorities have scrutinised this preservative for decades. The main focus of scientific evaluation is not the nitrite itself, but the nitrosamines that form when cured meats are cooked or digested. JECFA and EFSA have both re-evaluated its safety, concluding that while it successfully controls microbes, exposure to nitrosamines should be minimised. European regulators recently lowered the maximum permitted levels of nitrites that can be added to foods to help reduce consumer exposure to these unwanted chemical byproducts.

Where you'll find it

You will find this preservative almost exclusively in processed, cured, and smoked meats. It is a standard ingredient in bacon, ham, hot dogs, salami, sausages, and deli slices. On food packaging, it is typically declared as sodium nitrite, E250, or under its European names like nitrite de sodium, natriumnitrit, nitrito de sodio, or nitrito di sodio, often listed alongside sodium chloride or curing salt.
