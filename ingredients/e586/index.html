4-Hexylresorcinol (E586)

E586AdditiveAvoid · moderate concern

Diets

✓ Suits every diet Clara checks — as an ingredient

A synthetic antioxidant used to stop shellfish from turning black, but EFSA cannot set a safe daily limit due to a lack of safety data.

What it is

This substance is an organic compound that acts as an antioxidant. While it shares some chemical structures with compounds found naturally in whole grains, the version used in food is entirely synthetic. It is specifically designed to target and block the enzymes that cause dark discolouration on the shells and flesh of seafood after harvest, helping to maintain a fresh appearance.

How it's made

The additive is produced through a multi-step chemical synthesis in a laboratory. It starts with resorcinol, a chemical compound often derived from petroleum. This is reacted with hexanoic acid in the presence of an acid catalyst to form an intermediate compound, which is then reduced using zinc and hydrochloric acid to yield the final purified crystals of the antioxidant.

Why manufacturers use it

Seafood processors use this compound to prevent a natural browning process called melanosis, which turns the heads and shells of harvested crustaceans black. While this blackening is natural and does not make the food unsafe, shoppers find it unappealing. This additive is highly effective at low concentrations, allowing processors to keep prawns, lobsters, and crabs looking fresh and pink for much longer during transport and display.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this AVOID, a moderate-concern additive. EFSA evaluated the substance and concluded that no daily limit could be established because there is not enough toxicological data to prove its long-term safety. Furthermore, EFSA noted that estimated dietary exposure for people who eat a lot of shellfish could exceed the threshold where safety concerns arise, making it an additive to watch closely.

What the science says

The scientific consensus is that more research is needed to fully understand how this substance behaves in the human body. EFSA re-evaluated the compound in 2014 and confirmed that the existing database is too sparse to set an acceptable daily intake. The US FDA reviewed a GRAS notice in 2003 and did not object to its use in shrimp, but European authorities maintain strict limits on how much residue can remain on the final seafood product.

Where you'll find it

You will find this additive almost exclusively in the seafood aisle. It is used on fresh, frozen, and deep-frozen crustaceans like prawns, shrimps, lobsters, and crabs. On ingredient lists, it may be declared by its chemical name, 4-hexylresorcinol, or by its European code, E586, often listed alongside a functional class as antioxidant E586.
