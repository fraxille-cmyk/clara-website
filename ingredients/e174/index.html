Silver (E174)

E174AdditiveAvoid · moderate concern

Silver is a metallic colour used for decoration; Clara rates it AVOID because regulators lack the safety data to set a daily limit for its nanoparticles.

What it is

Silver is a precious metal that you likely know from jewellery and coins, but it also serves as a food colour. In the culinary world, it is used in tiny amounts to give a striking, metallic sheen to the surface of festive treats. Unlike many other food colours that dissolve into a mixture, this is a mineral element applied as an ultra-thin layer of actual metal, which remains intact on the food until you eat it.

How it's made

To become a food ingredient, silver must be highly purified to ensure it is free from other heavy metals. It is mined from natural mineral deposits and then refined through chemical and physical processes. The purified metal is beaten or rolled into incredibly thin sheets, known as silver leaf, or ground down into an extremely fine powder that can be dusted onto confectionery or suspended in liquids for spraying.

Why manufacturers use it

Manufacturers use silver purely for its visual appeal, as it has no flavour or preservative qualities. It provides a brilliant, reflective, metallic finish that cannot be replicated by plant-based or synthetic organic dyes. Because it is expensive and highly regulated, it is reserved for premium specialty items where a luxurious, festive, or celebratory appearance justifies the cost of using a precious metal.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this AVOID, a moderate-concern additive. While it has been used in decorations for generations, EFSA could not establish an acceptable daily intake in 2016 due to insufficient toxicity data, particularly regarding the behaviour of silver nanoparticles in the body. Because we do not have enough scientific evidence to prove how these tiny metallic particles behave over time, it is best to minimise your exposure.

What the science says

The scientific community has raised questions about how the body processes silver when it is consumed as a food colour. EFSA evaluated the mineral and highlighted a lack of robust data on how the smallest particles, known as nanoparticles, are absorbed and distributed through our tissues. Because of these gaps in the safety data, no daily limit is established, and regulators have restricted its use to very specific decorative applications.

Where you'll find it

You will find this ingredient almost exclusively on decorative items, such as silver dragées, cake sprinkles, chocolate decorations, and certain luxury liqueurs. On a label, it is typically listed as silver, E174, or E 174. In the United States, it is permitted under strict limits for nonstandardised confectionery, where it may be declared on the packaging simply as silver colour.
