Silver (E174)

E174AdditiveNeutral · limited concern

Diets

✓ Suits every diet Clara checks — as an ingredient

Silver is a metallic mineral used as a shiny food colour; Clara rates it as neutral because it is only used in tiny amounts on decorative treats.

What it is

Silver is a naturally occurring precious metal that you already know from jewellery and coins. In the food world, it is used in microscopic amounts as a mineral colour to give a brilliant, metallic sheen to special treats. Because it is a metal, it does not dissolve in water or oil, meaning it sits on the surface of foods rather than blending into them. It has no taste or smell, so its presence is entirely about creating a striking visual impression.

How it's made

The silver used in food starts as natural silver ore mined from the earth. This raw metal is refined through chemical and physical processes to remove any impurities, ensuring it is extremely pure. Once purified, the metal is beaten or rolled into incredibly thin sheets, known as silver leaf, or ground into a very fine powder. This delicate processing allows a tiny amount of the metal to cover a relatively large surface area on decorative confectionery.

Why manufacturers use it

Food manufacturers use silver to create a luxurious, festive appearance that other food colours cannot replicate. It provides a genuine metallic shimmer and a reflective surface that makes chocolates and sweets look premium. Since it is highly stable, it does not fade when exposed to light, heat, or air during storage. It is the ideal choice for high-end cake decorations, wedding sweets, and specialty liqueurs where a touch of elegance is desired.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this NEUTRAL, a limited-concern additive. This rating is given because the European Food Safety Authority could not set an acceptable daily intake for silver due to insufficient data on its long-term effects. However, because it is restricted to tiny decorative coatings, the actual amount you consume is extremely small. It is not known to cause harm in these minuscule quantities, but the lack of complete safety data means it is best enjoyed as an occasional treat rather than a daily staple.

What the science says

The European Food Safety Authority re-evaluated silver in 2016 and concluded that the available scientific evidence was too limited to establish a specific daily limit. While the metal passes through the body mostly unabsorbed, researchers still want more data on how nano-sized particles of silver might behave in human tissues over time. Regulators in both Europe and the United States keep a close eye on it, restricting its use to very specific decorative applications to ensure that dietary exposure remains extremely low.

Where you'll find it

You will find this ingredient on the labels of festive chocolates, cake decorations, dragées, and certain luxury liqueurs. On ingredient lists, it is typically declared by its name as silver, or by its European code E174. In the United States, where its use is also strictly limited to nonstandardised confectionery, it is listed as silver on the packaging. It is always used on the very outer surface of these products.
