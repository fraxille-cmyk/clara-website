Pork fat is a traditional, minimally processed animal fat used to add rich flavour and a satisfying texture to savoury foods.

What it is

Pork fat is a natural lipid obtained from the fatty tissues of pigs. It is a traditional cooking fat that consists primarily of saturated and unsaturated fatty acids. Unlike highly processed vegetable oils, it remains solid or semi-solid at room temperature and has been used in kitchens for centuries. In culinary terms, it is often referred to as lard when it has been clarified for cooking and baking, offering a distinct richness that is highly valued in traditional recipes.

How it's made

The production of pork fat is a straightforward thermal process known as rendering. Fatty tissues, particularly from the back or around the kidneys of the animal, are chopped and heated slowly. This heat melts the fat, separating it from the connective tissue. The liquid fat is then filtered to remove any remaining solid particles, cooled until it solidifies into a creamy white paste, and packaged without the need for complex chemical solvents or artificial bleaching agents.

Why manufacturers use it

Food producers choose pork fat because it delivers an exceptional depth of flavour and a moist, tender texture that vegetable fats struggle to replicate. It has a high smoke point, which makes it highly stable during high-temperature cooking. In baked goods, it creates a wonderfully flaky pastry because its fat crystals melt at just the right rate. It also stops packaged meats from drying out during storage, keeping them succulent and appealing to shoppers without requiring artificial moisture-retaining additives.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this NEUTRAL. As a whole food ingredient rather than a chemical additive, it does not carry the risks associated with ultra-processed synthetic substances. It is a concentrated source of energy and contains saturated fats, which means it is best enjoyed in moderation as part of a balanced diet. No regulatory body has established a specific toxicological upper limit for pork fat, as it is classified as a standard food ingredient rather than a regulated food additive.

What the science says

The scientific consensus on animal fats focuses on their fatty acid profiles and how they are metabolised by the body. EFSA and the WHO have evaluated dietary fats extensively, consistently recommending that saturated fats should be balanced with unsaturated fats in the daily diet to support cardiovascular health. Because it is a traditional food ingredient rather than an additive, it has not been subjected to the additive evaluation processes of JECFA, but its nutritional role is well documented in global dietary guidelines.

Where you'll find it

You will find pork fat listed on the labels of savoury products like sausages, pates, meat pies, and traditional pastries. It is typically declared plainly as pork fat or lard on the ingredient list. Because it is a natural food ingredient and not a food additive, it does not have an E-number or a functional class, appearing simply under its common name to indicate its presence in the recipe.
