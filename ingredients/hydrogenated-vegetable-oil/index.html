Hydrogenated vegetable oil

Processed ingredientAvoid

Hydrogenated vegetable oil is a liquid plant oil turned into a solid fat, a process that often creates trans fats which are best kept out of your diet.

What it is

Hydrogenated vegetable oil is a plant-based oil that has been chemically altered to turn it from a liquid into a solid or semi-solid fat at room temperature. It is a modified fat that behaves more like butter or lard than a liquid seed oil. You might recognise it as the primary component in traditional margarines and vegetable shortenings, designed to mimic the rich texture of animal fats using cheaper plant sources.

How it's made

To make this ingredient, manufacturers take liquid oils extracted from soy, palm, rapeseed, or sunflower seeds and heat them to high temperatures. They then pump hydrogen gas through the hot oil in the presence of a metal catalyst, usually nickel. This chemical reaction forces hydrogen atoms onto the carbon chains of the oil, straightening the molecules so they can pack tightly together and solidify. If the process is stopped halfway, it is called partially hydrogenated oil, which is the main source of artificial trans fats.

Why manufacturers use it

Food makers use this modified fat because it is incredibly stable and cheap. Liquid vegetable oils spoil quickly when exposed to air, but hydrogenating them makes them highly resistant to rancidity, giving packaged foods a much longer shelf life. It also provides a firm, creamy texture and a high melting point, which is perfect for creating flaky pastries, smooth fillings, and stable frying fats that do not break down under high heat.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this AVOID. The process of partial hydrogenation creates trans fatty acids, which alter cholesterol levels by raising low-density lipoprotein and lowering high-density lipoprotein in the blood. Because of these clear physiological effects, many regulators have moved to ban or strictly limit these fats. The European Commission set a maximum limit of two grams of industrially produced trans fat per one hundred grams of fat in food, while the FDA withdrew its GRAS status for partially hydrogenated oils.

What the science says

Major health authorities have evaluated these modified fats extensively. The World Health Organisation has launched global initiatives to eliminate industrially produced trans fatty acids from the food supply entirely, noting their direct link to adverse cardiovascular changes. EFSA panel opinions confirm that trans fatty acids increase the risk of heart-related issues compared to other fatty acids. While fully hydrogenated oils contain fewer trans fats, they are often grouped together on labels, making it difficult for shoppers to know exactly what they are consuming.

Where you'll find it

You will find this ingredient in a wide variety of highly processed foods, including commercial biscuits, pie crusts, microwave popcorn, margarines, ready-to-use frostings, and packaged cake mixes. On a food label, it is typically listed as hydrogenated vegetable oil, hydrogenated palm oil, hydrogenated soybean oil, or partially hydrogenated vegetable oil. It does not have an E-number because it is classified as a food ingredient rather than a food additive.
