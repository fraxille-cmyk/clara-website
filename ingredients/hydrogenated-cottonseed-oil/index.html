Hydrogenated cottonseed oil

Processed ingredientAvoid

Diets

✓ Suits every diet Clara checks — as an ingredient

A heavily processed vegetable oil that has been chemically hardened to create solid fats, which Clara recommends avoiding due to the presence of trans fats.

What it is

Hydrogenated cottonseed oil is a vegetable fat derived from the seeds of the cotton plant that has undergone a chemical hardening process. Cotton plants are primarily grown for their fibre to make textiles, but the seeds left behind contain a significant amount of oil. In its natural state, this oil is liquid and rich in unsaturated fats, but industrial processing transforms it into a solid or semi-solid fat that behaves more like butter or lard at room temperature.

How it's made

The journey begins with harvesting cotton seeds, which are cleaned, dehulled, and crushed to extract the crude oil. This oil is refined, bleached, and deodorised to remove natural impurities and strong flavours. To harden the oil, manufacturers heat it to high temperatures and pump hydrogen gas through it in the presence of a metal catalyst, usually nickel. This chemical reaction alters the molecular structure of the liquid fats, turning them into solid saturated and trans fats.

Why manufacturers use it

Food manufacturers use this modified oil because it provides a firm, creamy texture and an exceptionally long shelf life at a very low cost. Hardened cottonseed oil is highly stable against oxidation, meaning it does not spoil or go rancid easily when exposed to air or heat. This stability makes it ideal for extending the shelf life of packaged goods and ensuring that baked items maintain their structure and do not become greasy or soft on the supermarket shelf.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this AVOID. The industrial hardening process creates trans fatty acids, which are fats that the human body does not process well. These fats raise the levels of undesirable cholesterol in the blood while lowering the beneficial kind, which can lead to a buildup of fatty deposits in the blood vessels. Because of these clear physiological effects, health authorities recommend keeping the intake of industrially produced trans fats as low as possible.

What the science says

Major regulatory bodies have thoroughly evaluated the effects of hydrogenated oils on human physiology. The FDA has taken steps to remove partially hydrogenated oils from the food supply due to their trans fat content, and WHO guidelines strongly recommend that countries limit or ban industrially produced trans fatty acids in all foods. These assessments focus on how the altered fat molecules interact with blood lipids, showing a direct link to changes in cholesterol markers that affect the circulatory system.

Where you'll find it

You will typically find this ingredient in highly processed foods that require a long shelf life or a specific solid texture. It frequently appears in commercial baked goods like biscuits, cakes, and pie crusts, as well as in margarines, vegetable shortenings, packaged snacks, and pre-made frostings. On food labels, it is usually declared plainly as hydrogenated cottonseed oil or sometimes grouped under the term hydrogenated vegetable oil.
