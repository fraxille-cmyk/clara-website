Tertiary-butyl hydroquinone (TBHQ) (E319)

E319AdditiveNeutral · limited concern

Diets

✓ Suits every diet Clara checks — as an ingredient

A synthetic antioxidant used to keep fats and oils fresh, with a strict daily limit that keeps it in our neutral category.

What it is

Tertiary-butyl hydroquinone, commonly known as TBHQ, is a synthetic antioxidant. Unlike natural antioxidants like vitamin E, this is a highly stable compound created in a laboratory to prevent chemical breakdown. It is a white, crystalline powder with a very mild aroma, designed specifically to dissolve easily in fats and vegetable oils where natural freshness is difficult to maintain over long periods on a supermarket shelf.

How it's made

This additive is entirely synthetic and does not originate from any plant or animal source. It is manufactured through a chemical reaction between hydroquinone and tertiary-butanol, assisted by an acid catalyst. The resulting mixture is then purified, crystallised, and dried into a fine powder. Because it is highly refined through this industrial process, the final ingredient contains no raw agricultural materials.

Why manufacturers use it

Manufacturers choose this ingredient because it is incredibly efficient at stopping oxidation. When fats and oils are exposed to oxygen, they spoil, turn rancid, and develop unpleasant odours and flavours. This additive stabilises these fats, extending the shelf life of fried foods, baked goods, and margarines. It is highly valued in the food industry because it remains stable even under the high temperatures used in commercial baking and frying.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this NEUTRAL, a limited-concern additive. While it is a synthetic chemical, regulators have evaluated its safety thoroughly and established a clear limit for daily intake. EFSA keeps its daily limit of 0.7 mg per kg of body weight, which is a very small amount but easily respected because the additive is used in tiny quantities. There is no evidence of accumulation in the body, as it is metabolised and cleared quickly.

What the science says

Scientific panels have scrutinised this substance to ensure it does not pose a risk when consumed within the established limits. EFSA re-evaluated the compound and confirmed that the current acceptable daily intake is protective of human health. The FDA also permits its use but restricts it to a tiny fraction of the total fat content in a product. No regulatory body has raised active safety concerns regarding its current levels of exposure in processed foods.

Where you'll find it

You will find this ingredient in highly processed foods that contain vegetable oils, animal fats, or shortenings. It is common in instant noodles, crackers, potato crisps, microwave popcorn, and frozen fish products. On a label, it is usually listed as tertiary-butyl hydroquinone, TBHQ, or by its European code, E319. On US labels it is listed as tertiary butylhydroquinone.
