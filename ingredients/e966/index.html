Lactitol (E966)

E966SweetenerAvoid · moderate concern

Lactitol is a low-calorie sweetener made from milk sugar that can cause digestive discomfort and laxative effects when consumed in large amounts.

What it is

Lactitol is a sugar alcohol, or polyol, used as a bulk sweetener to replace sugar. Although its name sounds like lactose, it is a distinct, chemically modified substance that is only about forty per cent as sweet as regular sugar. It has a clean, sugar-like taste with no bitter aftertaste and provides fewer calories than standard carbohydrates because our bodies do not fully absorb or metabolise it. It occurs naturally in very few places, so the version we eat is always produced through a technical process.

How it's made

To make this sweetener, manufacturers start with lactose, which is the natural sugar found in whey, a liquid by-product of cheese making. This milk sugar is purified and then chemically modified through a process called catalytic hydrogenation. Under high pressure and temperature, hydrogen is added to the lactose molecule in the presence of a metal catalyst. This reaction transforms the glucose part of the lactose into sorbitol, creating a stable, crystalline powder that does not absorb moisture from the air.

Why manufacturers use it

Food makers choose this ingredient because it behaves almost exactly like sugar in recipes but with half the calories. It adds bulk, texture, and a pleasant mouthfeel to sugar-free products, which is something intense sweeteners cannot do on their own. It also helps to keep baked goods moist and prevents them from drying out over time. Because it does not feed the bacteria in the mouth that produce harmful acids, it is highly valued for making sweets and chewing gum that protect oral health.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this AVOID, a moderate-concern additive. While it is not a chemical poison, its structure means it passes through the small intestine mostly undigested and enters the large intestine, where it draws in water and is fermented by gut bacteria. This process can lead to gas, bloating, and abdominal discomfort. The European Union is concerned enough about these digestive issues that it mandates a warning label on any food containing more than ten per cent added polyols, stating that excessive consumption may produce laxative effects.

What the science says

Regulators have thoroughly evaluated this sweetener and consider it acceptable for general use, though they recognise its physical effects on the digestive tract. The Scientific Committee on Food established that no specific daily limit is required because the amount needed to cause severe discomfort is generally higher than what people eat in a normal diet. The US Food and Drug Administration has also accepted its status as safe for its intended uses. It is widely tolerated, but individuals with sensitive digestive systems may notice discomfort even with small portions.

Where you'll find it

You will find this ingredient in a wide variety of sugar-free and calorie-reduced foods, including sugar-free chocolates, hard candies, chewing gum, ice creams, and baked goods designed for sugar-conscious diets. On ingredient lists, it is typically declared by its plain name as lactitol, or by its European code, E966. On American packaging, it may be listed as lactitol or simply grouped under the total carbohydrate section as a sugar alcohol.
