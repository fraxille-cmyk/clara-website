Lactitol (E966)

E966SweetenerNeutral · limited concern

Diets

✕ Not vegan✕ Not low-FODMAP
  • Not vegan — lactitol is made from milk sugar.
  • Not low-FODMAP — lactitol is a polyol.

Lactitol is a low-calorie sweetener made from milk sugar that is generally fine in moderation but can cause mild digestive issues in larger amounts.

What it is

Lactitol is a sugar alcohol, also known as a polyol, which is used to replace sugar in foods. Although its name sounds like lactose, it is not actually sugar, but rather a chemically modified carbohydrate that tastes sweet. It has about forty percent of the sweetness of regular table sugar but contains only half the calories. Because our bodies do not fully absorb or metabolise it, it does not cause the rapid rise in blood glucose levels that ordinary sugar does, making it a popular choice for specialised dietary products.

How it's made

Lactitol does not occur naturally in large quantities, so it is manufactured through a synthetic process. Producers start with lactose, which is the natural sugar found in whey, a liquid byproduct of cheese making. This lactose is chemically modified by adding hydrogen under high pressure in the presence of a metal catalyst, usually nickel. This hydrogenation process transforms the sugar molecule into a sugar alcohol. The resulting liquid is then purified, filtered, and crystallised into a white, odourless powder that dissolves easily in water.

Why manufacturers use it

Food manufacturers choose lactitol because it behaves very much like regular sugar in recipes, providing bulk, texture, and a pleasant mouthfeel without the high calorie count. It is highly stable under heat, meaning it does not break down or lose its sweetness during baking or pasteurisation. It also acts as a humectant, which helps foods retain their moisture and prevents them from drying out on the shelf. Additionally, because oral bacteria cannot easily ferment it, it does not contribute to the demineralisation of teeth.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this NEUTRAL, a limited-concern additive. It is generally considered a benign ingredient, but because our bodies cannot fully digest sugar alcohols, they pass largely intact into the large intestine. There, they draw in water and are fermented by resident bacteria, which can lead to bloating, gas, and a laxative effect if you consume too much at once. No strict daily limit is established for lactitol, as regulators consider it safe for the general public, but sensitive individuals may want to monitor how much they eat.

What the science says

The safety of lactitol has been thoroughly evaluated by major food authorities worldwide. The Scientific Committee on Food established that no specified acceptable daily intake limit is necessary, a standing that reflects its low toxicity. The Joint FAO/WHO Expert Committee on Food Additives and the European Food Safety Authority have both reviewed the ingredient and confirmed its safety for use in food. Research shows that while it is well tolerated by most people, the main physiological effect of high doses is temporary digestive discomfort.

Where you'll find it

You will find lactitol in a variety of sugar-free and calorie-reduced products, particularly in hard candies, chewing gum, baked goods, frozen desserts, and chocolate. It is also used in some dietary supplements and sugar-free syrups. On ingredient lists in the United Kingdom and Europe, it is typically declared by its name, lactitol, or by its European code, E966. On US labels, it is simply listed as lactitol, where it is permitted under GRAS status.
