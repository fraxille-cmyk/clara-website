Oxidised polyethylene wax (E914)

E914AdditiveNeutral · limited concern

Oxidised polyethylene wax is a synthetic glazing agent used to coat fresh fruits, rated NEUTRAL by Clara because data are insufficient to set a daily limit.

What it is

Oxidised polyethylene wax is a synthetic, chemically modified wax that acts as a protective glazing agent. Unlike natural waxes harvested from palm leaves or beehives, this substance is a human-made polymer designed to mimic the water-resistant properties of natural coatings. It forms a very thin, invisible barrier over the surface of certain foods, helping them retain their natural moisture and look fresh during transport and display on supermarket shelves.

How it's made

This ingredient is produced through the controlled chemical modification of polyethylene, a common plastic. Manufacturers take low-density polyethylene and react it with oxygen or air under high pressure and temperature. This oxidation process introduces oxygen-containing groups into the polymer chain, which changes the physical properties of the wax. The resulting material is then refined, purified, and prepared into an emulsion that can be easily sprayed onto food surfaces.

Why manufacturers use it

Food producers use this synthetic wax primarily to extend the shelf life of fresh produce. When fruits are harvested, washed, and packed, their natural protective wax layer is often stripped away. Applying this oxidised wax restores that barrier, which prevents moisture loss, slows down natural shrinkage, and keeps the fruit plump. It also provides a glossy, appealing sheen that consumers expect, and it is often preferred over natural alternatives because it is highly stable and cost-effective to produce.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this NEUTRAL, a limited-concern additive. This rating is given because EFSA could not set an acceptable daily intake during its evaluation due to the fact that the available scientific data are insufficient. While there is no evidence of immediate harm, the lack of comprehensive long-term studies means regulators cannot establish a specific safe daily limit. Because of this, its use is strictly restricted to the surface treatment of specific fruits and in food supplements.

What the science says

The safety of this wax has been evaluated by major food authorities, but questions remain due to incomplete evidence. In its re-evaluation, EFSA noted that while the substance is poorly absorbed by the body and mostly passes through unchanged, the overall database is too limited to determine a formal daily limit. The FDA permits its use as a protective coating on fresh fruits under specific conditions, recognising that exposure is low because the wax is applied only to the inedible or washed outer peels of the produce.

Where you'll find it

You will find this ingredient on the outer skins of fresh citrus fruits, melons, papayas, mangoes, avocados, and pineapples, as well as in some food supplements. On ingredient lists or store signs, it is typically declared as oxidised polyethylene wax or by its European code, E914. On US labels, it is listed as oxidised polyethylene, where it is often mentioned in a statement indicating that the fruit has been coated to maintain freshness.
