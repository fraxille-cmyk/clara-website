Oxidised polyethylene wax (E914)

E914AdditiveAvoid · moderate concern

A synthetic wax used to coat fresh fruit, which Clara rates AVOID because regulators lack the safety data to set a clear daily limit.

What it is

Oxidised polyethylene wax is a synthetic substance used as a protective coating on the outside of certain fresh fruits. It is a modified form of polyethylene, which is the very common plastic used in everyday packaging. By chemically reacting this plastic with oxygen, manufacturers create a waxy material that can form a thin, invisible barrier over the skin of a fruit to help keep it fresh during transport and storage.

How it's made

This ingredient starts with ethylene gas, which is polymerised to create polyethylene. This polymer is then heated and reacted with oxygen or air under pressure. This oxidation process breaks down some of the molecular chains and introduces new chemical groups, transforming the tough plastic into a softer, emulsifiable wax that can be dissolved in water and sprayed onto food.

Why manufacturers use it

Food distributors use this wax primarily as a glazing agent to prevent moisture loss and shrinkage in fresh produce. When fruit is harvested, it naturally loses water through its skin, which makes it shrivel and lose its appealing texture. Applying a thin layer of this wax seals in the moisture, extends the shelf life of the fruit, and gives the skin a glossy, fresh appearance that shoppers find attractive.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this AVOID, a moderate-concern additive. This rating is because EFSA could not establish an acceptable daily intake in 2015 due to a lack of adequate toxicological data. Regulators do not have enough clear evidence regarding how the body absorbs, distributes, metabolises, and excretes this substance, nor do they have sufficient data on its long-term effects. While it is only applied to the peel, which is often discarded, the lack of complete safety data means we cannot be sure of its impact if consumed.

What the science says

The European Food Safety Authority re-evaluated this wax in 2015 and highlighted significant gaps in the scientific literature. EFSA noted that the available studies on how the body processes the wax were too limited to draw firm conclusions. Because of these data gaps, no official daily limit is established. The US Food and Drug Administration permits its use on fresh fruits under specific regulations, but the scientific consensus remains limited by the lack of modern, comprehensive feeding studies.

Where you'll find it

You will find this ingredient on the skins of fresh citrus fruits, melons, papayas, mangoes, avocados, and pineapples, as well as in some food supplements. Because it is applied as a surface treatment, it is not always listed on individual fruit labels, but it may appear on bulk boxes or store signs. On ingredient lists, it is typically declared as oxidised polyethylene wax or by its European code, E914.
