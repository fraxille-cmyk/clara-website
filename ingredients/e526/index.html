Calcium hydroxide (E526)

E526AdditiveNeutral · benign

Calcium hydroxide is a mineral-based acidity regulator used to balance pH, and Clara rates it as a benign additive with a long history of safe use.

What it is

Calcium hydroxide is an inorganic mineral compound that has been used in food preparation for thousands of years. Often known historically as slaked lime, it is a fine white powder that dissolves slightly in water to create an alkaline solution. In traditional cooking, it is the key ingredient in nixtamalisation, the ancient Mesoamerican process that softens corn hulls to make masa flour for tortillas. Today, it serves as a reliable tool for balancing acidity in modern food production.

How it's made

The production of this ingredient begins with natural limestone, which is primarily calcium carbonate. Manufacturers heat the limestone to high temperatures in a kiln, a process that drives off carbon dioxide and leaves behind calcium oxide, commonly called quicklime. This quicklime is then carefully mixed with water in a controlled hydration process. The reaction releases heat and transforms the material into calcium hydroxide, which is then dried and milled into a highly purified food-grade powder.

Why manufacturers use it

Food manufacturers rely on this mineral primarily to control acidity and stabilise the pH of their products. It is highly effective at neutralising acids, which helps to preserve flavours and prevent spoilage. Beyond pH control, it acts as a firming agent that strengthens the cell walls of fruits and vegetables during processing, ensuring they stay crisp rather than turning mushy. It also serves as a processing aid in sugar refining, helping to precipitate impurities out of the raw juice.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this NEUTRAL, a benign additive. Because it is a simple mineral compound that the body easily breaks down into calcium and water, it does not pose a risk to human health when consumed in dietary amounts. Regulators have evaluated its safety thoroughly and concluded that it does not accumulate in the body or cause adverse effects. Consequently, no limit is established for its daily intake, meaning it can be used freely in foods according to good manufacturing practices.

What the science says

Scientific bodies have evaluated this mineral over several decades and consistently confirmed its safety. The European Food Safety Authority re-evaluated the substance and found no safety concerns, noting that the calcium it contributes to the diet simply adds to our normal mineral intake. The United States Food and Drug Administration also recognises it as generally recognised as safe. Because the body metabolises it into essential calcium, researchers have found no reason to set a restrictive daily limit on its consumption.

Where you'll find it

You will find this ingredient in a variety of packaged foods, particularly in corn-based products like tortillas, taco shells, and hominy. It is also common in canned vegetables, infant formulas, glacé fruits, and cocoa products. On ingredient lists, it is typically declared by its plain name as calcium hydroxide, or by its European code, E526. On US labels, it is often listed as calcium hydroxide or simply lime, reflecting its traditional culinary roots.
