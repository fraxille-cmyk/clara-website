Lutein (E161b)

E161bAdditiveNeutral · limited concern

Diets

✓ Suits every diet Clara checks — as an ingredient

Lutein is a natural yellow-orange food colour extracted from marigold flowers that is generally considered safe within a set daily limit.

What it is

Lutein is a natural pigment belonging to the carotenoid family, which is the same group of compounds that gives carrots and autumn leaves their bright hues. It is a deep yellow-orange substance that occurs naturally in dark green leafy vegetables, corn, and egg yolks. In the human body, it naturally concentrates in the eyes, where it plays a role in filtering light. When you see it on a food label, it is there as a purified extract used to tint food with a warm, golden colour.

How it's made

To make the commercial food additive, manufacturers harvest the petals of Aztec marigold flowers, which are exceptionally rich in this pigment. The dried petals undergo a process of solvent extraction to separate the crude pigment from the plant fibre. This extract is then purified and typically dissolved in vegetable oil or prepared as a water-dispersible powder so it can be easily mixed into different food recipes. The final ingredient is a highly concentrated, natural colour that has not been chemically modified.

Why manufacturers use it

Food manufacturers use this ingredient to restore the natural yellow and orange tones that can be lost during high-heat processing, pasteurisation, or storage. It provides a highly stable, vibrant colour that helps products look more appealing and consistent on the shelf. Because it is derived from a familiar flower, it also allows brands to avoid synthetic petroleum-based dyes, appealing to shoppers who prefer simple, plant-derived ingredients on the label.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this NEUTRAL, a limited-concern additive. It is a natural plant pigment that is well tolerated by the body, though regulators have established a clear limit on how much should be consumed daily. EFSA keeps its daily limit of 1 mg per kg of body weight for lutein extracted from marigolds. Because it is a concentrated additive rather than a whole food, keeping an eye on this limit ensures we do not consume it in excessive amounts across many different processed foods.

What the science says

Scientific bodies have thoroughly evaluated this pigment to ensure its safety as an additive. EFSA re-evaluated the substance and established the current acceptable daily intake based on how the body absorbs and processes carotenoids. JECFA also reviewed the safety data and concluded that the purified extract does not present safety concerns when used within the established guidelines. Researchers continue to study how the body uses this compound, particularly its natural accumulation in eye tissue, with no safety concerns raised at normal dietary levels.

Where you'll find it

You will find this colour in a wide variety of everyday foods, including yellow cheeses, margarines, spreads, processed desserts, confectionery, and bakery products. It is also frequently added to animal feed to naturally enrich the colour of chicken skin and egg yolks. On ingredient lists in the UK and Europe, it is typically declared by its name as lutein or under its European code, E161b. On US labels, you might see it listed as tagetes extract or marigold extract.
