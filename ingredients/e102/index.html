Tartrazine (E102)

E102AdditiveAvoid · moderate concern

Diets

Tartrazine is a synthetic yellow food colour that requires a mandatory warning in Europe because it may affect activity and attention in children.

What it is

Tartrazine is a synthetic lemon-yellow food dye. It belongs to the azo dye family, which is a class of artificial colourings known for their intense brightness and stability. Unlike natural yellow pigments derived from turmeric or marigolds, this substance is entirely man-made and does not occur in nature. It is highly soluble in water, making it incredibly easy to blend into a wide variety of liquid and solid foods to give them a vibrant, sunny appearance.

How it's made

This colour is manufactured through chemical synthesis, starting with petroleum-derived raw materials. The production process involves a chemical reaction known as diazotisation, where coal-tar derivatives are coupled to form the azo compound. The resulting substance is then purified, crystallised, and converted into a sodium salt. Because it is highly processed and entirely synthetic, the final powder contains no agricultural ingredients and is highly standardised for industrial food production.

Why manufacturers use it

Food manufacturers choose this additive because it is incredibly cheap, highly stable, and delivers a bright yellow hue that natural alternatives struggle to match. Natural colours often fade when exposed to heat, light, or acidic environments during processing and storage, but this synthetic dye remains vibrant for months. It is used to restore the visual appeal of highly processed foods that lose their natural colour during manufacturing, or to create fun, brightly coloured products that appeal directly to younger shoppers.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this AVOID, a moderate-concern additive. While the body absorbs very little of this dye, the European Union requires a mandatory warning label on foods containing it, stating that it may have an adverse effect on activity and attention in children. EFSA has established an acceptable daily intake of 7.5 mg per kg of body weight. Because of the potential behavioural effects in sensitive individuals, especially children, it is an ingredient that many families choose to bypass in the supermarket aisles.

What the science says

Regulators have scrutinised this dye for decades to evaluate its safety. EFSA re-evaluated the substance in 2009 and confirmed the acceptable daily intake, noting that while the evidence on behavioural changes is complex, the precautionary warning label remains necessary to protect young consumers. The FDA also permits its use but enforces strict labelling rules so that sensitive individuals can identify it. No toxicological concern regarding tissue accumulation has been found, as the vast majority of the dye is broken down by bacteria in the gut and excreted.

Where you'll find it

You will find this colour in a vast array of processed foods, including soft drinks, sweets, custard powders, ice creams, packet soups, and decorated baked goods. On ingredient lists, it is typically declared by its name, tartrazine, or by its European code, E102. On products imported from or sold in the United States, it is listed as FD&C Yellow No. 5, so shoppers should look out for both names when checking labels.
