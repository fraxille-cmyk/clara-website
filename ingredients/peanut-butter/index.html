Peanut butter

Processed ingredientGood

Peanut butter is a rich, creamy paste made from roasted peanuts, offering a simple source of plant-based protein, healthy fats, and dietary fibre.

What it is

Peanut butter is a thick, spreadable paste made primarily from ground roasted peanuts. It is a familiar kitchen staple that bridges the gap between whole nuts and a smooth, versatile spread. Naturally rich in plant-based proteins, unsaturated fats, and dietary fibre, it provides a dense source of energy. While some traditional styles contain only peanuts and a pinch of salt, other commercial varieties may include small amounts of vegetable oils to keep the mixture from separating or sugar to adjust the flavour profile.

How it's made

The journey from crop to jar begins with harvesting peanuts, which are actually legumes that grow underground. Once harvested, the shells are removed, and the kernels are dry-roasted in large ovens to develop their characteristic warm, nutty flavour and deep golden colour. After roasting, a rapid cooling process stops the cooking. The peanuts then pass through a blanching machine to remove the thin outer skins. Finally, the clean kernels are ground in two stages, first to a medium paste and then to a fine, smooth consistency, sometimes with salt or oil added during the final grind.

Why manufacturers use it

Food manufacturers value peanut butter for its rich flavour, satisfying texture, and excellent binding properties. In bakery items, snack bars, and confectionery, it acts as a natural emulsifier and structural base, helping to hold dry ingredients together without the need for synthetic binders. It also provides a rich mouthfeel and a slow release of energy, which makes it highly desirable for protein bars and breakfast products. Its natural stability at room temperature makes it an efficient, cost-effective ingredient that enhances both the taste and substance of packaged foods.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this GOOD. As a whole food ingredient rather than a chemical additive, it is a nutritious addition to most diets. No limit is established for its consumption, though its high energy density means it is typically enjoyed in moderation. The primary consideration is for individuals with a specific sensitivity to peanut proteins, as the body can react strongly to even tiny amounts. For everyone else, it is a wholesome source of nutrients that supports daily energy needs without any synthetic chemical exposure.

What the science says

Regulators have evaluated peanut butter primarily from a food safety and standardisation perspective. The FDA maintains standards of identity to ensure that any product labelled as peanut butter contains at least ninety per cent peanuts, limiting the use of optional ingredients like stabilisers and sweeteners. JECFA and other international bodies monitor the agricultural side of peanut production, focusing on storage practices that keep the raw crop free from natural moulds. The scientific consensus supports its role as a nutrient-dense food that delivers beneficial unsaturated fatty acids and essential minerals.

Where you'll find it

You will find this ingredient in a wide variety of supermarket products, from jars of creamy or crunchy spreads to protein bars, breakfast cereals, biscuits, chocolates, and savoury sauces like satay. On a food label, it is typically listed simply as peanut butter, roasted peanuts, or peanut paste. Because it does not carry an E-number, it is easily identified in the ingredient list, sometimes accompanied by a note indicating the percentage of peanuts used in the recipe.
