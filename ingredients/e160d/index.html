Lycopene (E160d)

E160dAdditiveAvoid · moderate concern

Diets

✓ Suits every diet Clara checks — as an ingredient

Lycopene is a red food colour that Clara rates AVOID because young children can easily consume more than the recommended daily limit.

What it is

Lycopene is a bright red pigment that belongs to the carotenoid family, the same group of natural compounds that gives carrots their orange hue. In nature, it is the substance responsible for the deep red colour of ripe tomatoes, watermelons, and pink grapefruits. While it exists abundantly in fresh fruits, the version added to packaged foods is often a highly concentrated extract or a chemically identical synthetic replica designed to survive industrial food processing.

How it's made

Although lycopene can be extracted directly from tomatoes using solvents, manufacturers frequently produce it synthetically to ensure a consistent shade and lower production costs. The synthetic route involves a multi-step chemical synthesis that links smaller organic molecules together to build the complex carotenoid structure. After synthesis, the compound is purified, crystallised, and formulated into powders or oil suspensions so that it can be easily mixed into different food bases.

Why manufacturers use it

Food manufacturers use this ingredient as a colour to restore the appealing red, orange, or pink tones that are often lost during high-heat processing and long storage periods. It provides a highly stable, vibrant hue that makes processed foods look fresh and appetising. It is a popular commercial choice because it performs reliably in acidic environments, such as soft drinks and fruit preparations, where other natural red colours might quickly fade or turn brown.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this AVOID, a moderate-concern additive. While the pigment itself is not inherently dangerous, EFSA established an acceptable daily intake of 0.5 mg per kg of body weight and noted that toddlers and children who eat a lot of coloured foods can easily exceed this limit. Because young children have a low body weight, consuming multiple products coloured with this additive can push their daily intake beyond the level considered fully supportive of long-term health.

What the science says

Scientific panels have evaluated this colour extensively to determine how it behaves in the body. EFSA completed a thorough re-evaluation of the additive, setting the daily limit to protect consumers from excessive exposure. The main concern is not immediate toxicity, as the substance is metabolised through normal pathways, but rather the cumulative intake from multiple dietary sources. Regulators continue to monitor consumption patterns to ensure that cumulative exposure, especially in young populations, remains within acceptable boundaries.

Where you'll find it

You will find this ingredient in a wide range of colourful processed foods, including flavoured milks, yoghurts, soft drinks, sweets, sauces, and bakery decorations. On ingredient labels in the United Kingdom and Europe, it is typically listed by its name as lycopene or by its E-number, E160d. On US labels, it may be declared as synthetic lycopene or lycopene extract from tomato, depending on the specific source used by the manufacturer.
