Tartaric acid esters of mono- and diglycerides (E472d)

E472dAdditiveNeutral · limited concern

An emulsifier made from fats and tartaric acid, used to improve texture. Clara rates this NEUTRAL, a limited-concern additive.

What it is

This ingredient is a food additive that belongs to a family of emulsifiers. It is a compound created by combining plant-based or animal-derived fats with tartaric acid, an acid found naturally in grapes. In food, it acts as a bridge between water and oil, which normally separate. By holding these ingredients together, it helps to create a smooth, uniform texture in processed foods. It is similar to other fat-based emulsifiers but has a slightly different structure that makes it particularly useful in baking.

How it's made

To make this ingredient, manufacturers start with mono- and diglycerides, which are fats typically derived from vegetable oils like palm, rapeseed, or soybean oil, though animal fats can also be used. These fats are chemically reacted with tartaric acid under controlled, high-temperature conditions. This chemical modification changes how the fats behave, allowing them to bind with water much more effectively. The resulting mixture is then purified, neutralised, and dried into a powder or a thick paste ready for food manufacturing.

Why manufacturers use it

Food manufacturers use this substance primarily as an emulsifier and dough conditioner. In baking, it strengthens the gluten network in dough, which helps bread rise higher and keeps the crumb structure soft and uniform. It also prevents staling by slowing down the starch crystallisation process. For manufacturers, it is a highly efficient and cost-effective choice because it performs reliably in large-scale industrial baking, ensuring that every loaf of bread or batch of pastries has the same volume, texture, and shelf life.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this NEUTRAL, a limited-concern additive. This rating is based on its synthetic origin and the fact that it has an established acceptable daily intake. EFSA set a numeric daily limit of 240 mg per kg of body weight for this substance in 2020. Once consumed, the body breaks it down into its familiar components, namely tartaric acid and fatty acids, which are then metabolised normally. There are no major safety concerns or open questions regarding its use, and it is considered a benign addition to the food supply when consumed within normal dietary levels.

What the science says

This additive has been thoroughly evaluated by major food safety authorities. The European Food Safety Authority completed a full re-evaluation of its safety in 2020, confirming that it does not pose a safety concern at current exposure levels and establishing the numeric daily limit. In the United States, the Food and Drug Administration recognises a closely related form as generally recognised as safe. Because it is broken down into simple fats and organic acids that the body processes daily, scientists have found no evidence of accumulation or adverse effects from normal dietary intake.

Where you'll find it

You will most commonly find this ingredient in industrial baked goods, such as packaged sliced bread, rolls, croissants, and sweet pastries. It also appears in some cake mixes, powdered desserts, and processed fats like margarine. On a food label, it is usually listed by its full name, tartaric acid esters of mono- and diglycerides, or by its European code, E472d. On US labels, you may see it or its close relatives listed under similar names, such as diacetyl tartaric acid esters of mono- and diglycerides.
