Thiamin

FortificantNeutral · benign

Thiamin, also known as vitamin B1, is an essential nutrient added to foods to boost their nutritional value, and is completely harmless at label amounts.

What it is

Thiamin is simply vitamin B1, a water-soluble nutrient that your body needs every day to function properly. It is a natural compound that helps your cells turn carbohydrates into energy, supporting your nervous system and muscles. Because your body cannot store this vitamin for very long, you must constantly replenish it through your diet. In the grocery aisles, you will often see it added to processed grains to replace the natural vitamins lost during milling, or to fortify plant-based alternatives so they match the nutritional profile of dairy.

How it's made

While thiamin occurs naturally in whole grains, beans, nuts, and meat, the version added to packaged foods is typically made through chemical synthesis to ensure it remains stable on store shelves. Manufacturers start with simple organic compounds, combining them through a series of controlled chemical reactions to build the precise molecular structure of the vitamin. The final product is purified into a fine, white crystalline powder, usually in the form of thiamin mononitrate or thiamin hydrochloride, which is highly stable and dissolves easily in food mixtures.

Why manufacturers use it

Food producers use thiamin primarily for fortification and enrichment. During the industrial milling of wheat, rice, and other grains, the nutrient-rich outer bran and germ are stripped away, taking most of the natural B vitamins with them. Adding synthetic thiamin back into the flour ensures that everyday foods like bread, pasta, and breakfast cereals still deliver essential nutrition. It is a highly cost-effective way to restore dietary value without altering the flavour, colour, or baking properties of the final product.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this NEUTRAL, a benign additive. Because thiamin is a water-soluble nutrient, your body simply absorbs what it needs and flushes out any excess through your urine. There is no evidence of unwanted effects from consuming thiamin in fortified foods, even at high levels, as the body regulates its absorption very efficiently. No upper limit is established for daily intake by major food authorities, as the amounts used to fortify foods are modest and entirely compatible with a healthy system.

What the science says

Scientific bodies worldwide have thoroughly evaluated thiamin and confirm its safety as a food fortificant. The European Food Safety Authority and the US Food and Drug Administration both recognise it as an essential nutrient that is safe for addition to foods and infant formulas. Because it is highly soluble and easily metabolised, researchers have found no accumulation in tissues or adverse responses in human trials. It is widely accepted as a beneficial dietary addition that supports general metabolic health.

Where you'll find it

You will find this ingredient in fortified breakfast cereals, white flour, enriched breads, pasta, white rice, and baby foods, as well as in plant-based milk alternatives. On the ingredient list, it might appear under its common name as thiamine, thiamin, or vitamin b1. It can also be listed by its specific chemical forms, such as thiamin mononitrate, thiamine hydrochloride, or their French, Spanish, and Italian equivalents like chlorhydrate de thiamine and mononitrato de tiamina.
