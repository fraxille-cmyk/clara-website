Sodium tetraborate (borax) (E285)

E285AdditiveNeutral · limited concern

Sodium tetraborate, also known as borax, is a mineral preservative used almost exclusively to keep sturgeon caviar fresh.

What it is

Sodium tetraborate, commonly known as borax, is a naturally occurring mineral salt. It is a compound of sodium, boron, oxygen, and water. While most of us recognise borax as a household cleaning agent or a craft ingredient for making slime, it has a very long history as a traditional food preservative. In the culinary world, it is prized for its unique ability to firm up textures and prevent spoilage in delicate seafood, particularly sturgeon eggs.

How it's made

This ingredient starts in nature as a mineral deposit. It is mined directly from dried-up lake beds, primarily in arid regions like California and Turkey where ancient waters evaporated and left behind rich salt crusts. Once extracted, the crude mineral ore is dissolved in hot water, filtered to remove insoluble clay and sand, and then cooled. As the liquid cools, pure sodium tetraborate crystals form. These crystals are gathered, dried, and ground into a fine white powder suitable for food preparation.

Why manufacturers use it

Manufacturers use sodium tetraborate because it is exceptionally good at preserving the delicate, firm texture of sturgeon caviar. Caviar is highly perishable and sensitive to temperature and handling. Borax acts as a specialised preservative that controls the growth of unwanted microbes while maintaining the characteristic pop and gloss of the fish eggs. It also helps control acidity, preventing the oils in the caviar from turning rancid, which is a technical job that cheaper salt alternatives cannot do as effectively without altering the flavour.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this NEUTRAL, a limited-concern additive. This rating reflects the fact that the additive has limited safety data and is restricted to a single luxury food. While some sources claim that borax is banned in the United States, this status is actually unconfirmed as it is not listed in the official registry of prohibited substances. Because it is only allowed in caviar, most people consume vanishingly small amounts of it, meaning it is not a day-to-day concern for the average shopper.

What the science says

The European Food Safety Authority re-evaluated this mineral and established a group acceptable daily intake of 0.16 milligrams per kilogram of body weight, expressed as boron. EFSA noted that while typical exposure is very low, adult high consumers of sturgeon caviar in the ninety-fifth percentile could exceed this limit. Because the data on long-term human consumption are limited, European regulators have restricted its use strictly to caviar, ensuring that the general public does not encounter it in everyday staple foods.

Where you'll find it

You will almost never find this ingredient in a standard supermarket aisle. It is authorised solely for the preservation of sturgeon caviar, so it only appears on the labels of high-end, traditional fish roe products. On a label, it is typically declared by its name, sodium tetraborate, or by its European code, E285. On US labels, it may occasionally be referred to simply as borax when imported under specific culinary trade guidelines.
