Sodium tetraborate (borax) (E285)

E285AdditiveAvoid · high concern

A mineral preservative used in caviar that is banned in the US and strictly limited in Europe due to concerns about its effects on reproduction.

What it is

Sodium tetraborate, commonly known as borax, is a naturally occurring mineral salt. It is a compound of boron, sodium, oxygen, and water, often found in dry lake beds where seasonal waters evaporate and leave behind white crystalline deposits. While most people recognise it as a household cleaning agent or a craft ingredient for making slime, it has a long history as a food preservative because of its ability to control acidity and prevent the growth of yeasts and moulds.

How it's made

The production of food-grade sodium tetraborate begins with mining raw borate ores from deep underground deposits. These crude minerals are crushed and dissolved in hot water to separate the soluble borates from insoluble clay and rock. The liquid is filtered and cooled, which causes refined sodium tetraborate crystals to form. These crystals are then washed, dried, and milled into a fine white powder that meets strict purity standards before it can be used in food processing.

Why manufacturers use it

Food manufacturers use this mineral salt primarily as a preservative to extend the shelf life of delicate seafood. It is highly valued in the traditional preparation of sturgeon caviar because it preserves the firm texture and delicate glaze of the eggs without altering their natural flavour. It also acts as a buffer to control acidity, which prevents the product from spoiling during transport and storage, offering a cheaper and more stable preservation method than relying solely on temperature control.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this AVOID, a high-concern additive. The FDA banned sodium tetraborate for use in food in 1910, and it remains prohibited in the United States. In Europe, the European Food Safety Authority established a strict daily limit of 0.16 mg per kg of body weight in 2013. This limit can be easily exceeded by people who regularly eat caviar, raising concerns because boron is known to have undesirable effects on reproduction and development when it accumulates in the body.

What the science says

Regulators have scrutinised this substance for over a century. The FDA confirmed its ban by declaring that borax and its related compounds are not generally recognised as safe. In Europe, a comprehensive re-evaluation by the European Food Safety Authority concluded that while the additive is restricted to a single luxury food item, the margin of safety is narrow. Scientific assessments show that boron compounds can accumulate in tissues and interfere with hormone systems and reproductive health.

Where you'll find it

You will almost exclusively find this ingredient in imported sturgeon caviar, where it is used to preserve the delicate fish eggs. On ingredient lists, it may be declared by its chemical name, sodium tetraborate, or simply as borax. In the European Union, it is identified by its official additive number, E285, or E 285. It is highly unlikely to appear in everyday supermarket groceries, but checking the labels of speciality seafood jars is the best way to spot it.
