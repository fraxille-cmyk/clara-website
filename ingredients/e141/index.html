Copper complexes of chlorophylls and chlorophyllins (E141)

E141AdditiveAvoid · moderate concern

A chemically modified green food colour that Clara advises you to avoid because regulators lack the data to confirm its long-term safety.

What it is

This ingredient is a semi-synthetic green food colour derived from natural chlorophyll, the pigment that gives plants and algae their vibrant green hue. Natural chlorophyll is highly unstable and fades quickly when exposed to heat or light. To make it durable, chemists replace the magnesium atom at the centre of the natural chlorophyll molecule with a copper atom. This chemical modification creates a much sturdier, intense green substance that keeps its colour throughout the shelf life of a packaged food.

How it's made

The production process begins with extracting natural chlorophyll from plant sources, typically alfalfa, grass, or nettles, using solvents. This extract is then chemically modified through a process called saponification, followed by the introduction of copper salts. The copper replaces the natural magnesium in the molecular structure. The resulting compound is then purified to remove impurities and solvent residues, leaving behind a stable, water-soluble or oil-soluble green colouring agent ready for industrial food production.

Why manufacturers use it

Food manufacturers choose this modified colour because natural green pigments degrade rapidly during cooking, pasteurisation, and storage. This copper-based alternative resists heat, acid, and light, ensuring that products maintain an appealing, fresh-looking green colour on store shelves for months. It is a highly efficient and cost-effective way to make processed foods look as though they contain fresh, green plant ingredients, even after intensive factory processing that would otherwise leave them looking grey or brown.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this AVOID, a moderate-concern additive. While it is authorised for use, EFSA concluded in 2015 that the acceptable daily intake could not be confirmed due to a lack of adequate toxicological data. This means that scientists do not have enough information to establish a clear limit for how much can be consumed without potential risk. Because of these unresolved questions regarding how the body absorbs and processes this modified compound, Clara recommends choosing products that get their green colour from simple, natural plant extracts instead.

What the science says

European safety assessors have re-evaluated this colour and raised questions about its behaviour in the human body. EFSA noted a significant lack of data regarding how much copper and chlorophyllin are absorbed by the gut, and whether the compound has any potential to interact with genetic material. Because the available studies do not meet modern scientific standards, no official daily limit is established. The regulator has requested further research from the food industry to clarify these gaps, but until new evidence is provided, its biological impact remains partially uncharacterised.

Where you'll find it

You will find this colour in a wide range of green-hued packaged foods, including pistachios, green vegetables in brine, ice creams, sweets, jellies, liqueurs, and chewing gum. On ingredient lists in the UK and Europe, it is typically declared by its full name as copper complexes of chlorophylls and chlorophyllins, or simply as E141. On US labels, where its use is restricted to specific categories like citrus-based beverages and dry mixes, it may be listed as sodium copper chlorophyllin.
