Calcium disodium EDTA (E385)

E385AdditiveNeutral · limited concern

Diets

✓ Suits every diet Clara checks — as an ingredient

A synthetic preservative that binds metals to keep food looking fresh. Clara rates it neutral, with a daily limit of 2.4 mg per kg of body weight.

What it is

Calcium disodium EDTA is a synthetic compound designed to bind to metal ions in food. Think of it as a microscopic claw that grabs hold of tiny mineral particles, such as iron or copper, which are naturally present in water and ingredients. By locking these metals away, it prevents them from reacting with other components in the food. It is a highly stable, white, odourless powder that dissolves easily in water, making it a reliable tool for preserving the appearance and quality of packaged goods.

How it's made

This ingredient is entirely synthetic and does not exist in nature. It is manufactured in a chemical plant through a multi-step process. The starting materials are ethylenediamine, formaldehyde, and sodium cyanide, which are reacted together to form sodium salts of EDTA. This intermediate substance is then carefully neutralised and reacted with calcium compounds to produce calcium disodium EDTA. The final product is purified, crystallised, and dried into a fine powder, ensuring no raw chemical residues remain before it enters the food supply.

Why manufacturers use it

Manufacturers rely on this additive because metals in food can trigger chemical reactions that spoil the product. When metals react with oxygen, they cause fats to go rancid, flavours to change, and vibrant colours to fade into dull greys or browns. By binding these metals, the additive acts as a powerful preservative. It is incredibly efficient, working at very low concentrations where other antioxidants might fail, which helps manufacturers extend the shelf life of bottled and canned goods without altering their taste or texture.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this NEUTRAL, a limited-concern additive. This rating is based on its synthetic origin and the fact that it has a numeric acceptable daily intake of 2.4 mg per kg of body weight. Because it is highly effective at very low levels, the amount you consume in a normal diet is typically well below this threshold. It passes through your digestive system mostly unabsorbed, carrying the bound metals out of your body with it, meaning it does not accumulate in your tissues over time.

What the science says

European and American regulators have thoroughly evaluated this compound. EFSA re-evaluated the additive in 2018 and established an acceptable daily intake of 2.4 mg per kg of body weight, confirming that current exposure levels do not pose a safety concern for the general population. The FDA also permits its use under strict limits. Because it binds minerals so effectively, scientists have studied whether it could interfere with the absorption of essential nutrients like iron and zinc, but research shows that at approved dietary levels, it does not disrupt mineral balance.

Where you'll find it

You will most often spot this ingredient in canned and bottled foods that are prone to discolouration. It is common in canned beans, chickpeas, mushrooms, and artichokes, where it prevents the vegetables from turning grey. It is also used in mayonnaise, salad dressings, and spreadable fats to stop oils from going rancid. On ingredient lists, it is typically written as calcium disodium EDTA or by its European code, E 385, while on US labels it is often listed as calcium disodium ethylenediaminetetraacetate.
