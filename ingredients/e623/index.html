Calcium diglutamate (E623)

E623AdditiveAvoid · moderate concern

Clara rates this AVOID. It is a savoury flavour enhancer that can easily lead to exceeding the daily intake limit for glutamates in a normal diet.

What it is

Calcium diglutamate is a mineral salt that combines calcium with glutamic acid, an amino acid that occurs naturally in many protein-rich foods like aged cheeses, tomatoes, and cured meats. It belongs to the glutamate family, which is famous for triggering the savoury taste receptors on our tongue. While most people are familiar with the sodium version of this compound, this calcium-bound alternative provides the same rich taste profile while contributing calcium instead of sodium to the food mixture.

How it's made

This ingredient is typically produced through a controlled industrial fermentation process. Manufacturers feed carbohydrate sources, such as sugar beet molasses, starch hydrolysates, or sugar cane, to specific strains of bacteria that naturally produce glutamic acid. Once the fermentation is complete, the resulting glutamic acid is purified and reacted with calcium hydroxide or calcium carbonate. This chemical reaction neutralises the acid and creates the stable salt crystals that are dried and packaged for food production.

Why manufacturers use it

Food manufacturers use this additive primarily as a flavour enhancer to intensify the savoury, umami notes in processed foods. It is a highly effective tool for making low-sodium products taste satisfying, as it delivers the desired mouth-watering depth without adding dietary sodium. It also helps to harmonise and round out other seasoning notes, such as herbs and spices, making the overall flavour profile of a packaged product taste more robust and freshly prepared.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this AVOID, a moderate-concern additive. EFSA established a group acceptable daily intake of 30 mg per kg of body weight for glutamic acid and its salts in 2017. The regulator noted that estimated dietary exposure to these substances exceeded this limit across several population groups, which raises concerns about cumulative daily intake. While the FDA classifies it as generally recognised as safe under US regulations, the high likelihood of consuming more than the recommended daily limit through a standard diet of processed foods makes it an additive to bypass.

What the science says

Scientific bodies have closely evaluated the safety of glutamates in our food supply. In a comprehensive re-evaluation, EFSA analysed the total exposure from all dietary sources and concluded that many consumers exceed the safe threshold. The main focus of scientific consensus is not acute toxicity, but rather the cumulative effect of eating multiple glutamate-heavy foods throughout the day. No limit is established by some older assessments, but modern dietary modelling shows that children and high-exposure adults easily pass the updated safety limits.

Where you'll find it

You will find this ingredient in highly seasoned processed foods, including savoury snacks, potato crisps, instant noodle packets, tinned soups, spice mixes, and processed meat products. On ingredient labels in the United Kingdom and Europe, it is typically declared by its full name as calcium diglutamate or by its European code, E623. On US labels, it is listed as calcium glutamate or calcium diglutamate, often appearing alongside other flavour-enhancing salts.
