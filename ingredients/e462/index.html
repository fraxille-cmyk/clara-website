Ethyl cellulose (E462)

E462AdditiveNeutral · benign

Diets

✓ Suits every diet Clara checks — as an ingredient

Ethyl cellulose is a modified plant fibre used to thicken foods and bind ingredients together. Clara rates it as a neutral, benign additive.

What it is

Ethyl cellulose is a modified form of cellulose, which is the natural structural material found in the cell walls of plants. While natural cellulose is tough and fibrous, this version has been chemically altered to change how it interacts with water and oils. It belongs to a family of modified plant fibres that help give structure to processed foods. In its pure form, it is a white, free-flowing powder that does not dissolve in water but can form stable gels when mixed with certain oils and fats.

How it's made

The production begins with natural wood pulp or cotton fibres, which are rich in cellulose. This raw material is treated with a strong alkaline solution to prepare the plant fibres for modification. Manufacturers then react this treated cellulose with ethyl chloride under controlled heat and pressure. This chemical reaction replaces some of the natural hydrogen atoms in the plant structure with ethyl groups. The resulting material is thoroughly washed, purified to remove any leftover chemicals, dried, and ground into a fine powder suitable for food use.

Why manufacturers use it

Food manufacturers value this ingredient because it is an exceptionally versatile binder and thickener. It can create stable, solid-like structures from liquid vegetable oils, which allows companies to replace saturated animal fats with healthier unsaturated fats without losing the desired texture. It also acts as a protective coating for moisture-sensitive ingredients, prevents clumping in dry mixes, and helps hold tablets or compressed foods together. Because it is highly stable under heat, it performs reliably during baking and high-temperature processing.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this NEUTRAL, a benign additive. Because it is a modified plant fibre, your body does not absorb or digest it, and it passes through your digestive system unchanged. EFSA evaluated the safety of this substance in 2017 and determined that it is of no safety concern, meaning no acceptable daily intake limit is established. It behaves just like natural dietary fibre in the gut, and there are no reports of unwanted digestive effects at the levels normally found in food products.

What the science says

Scientific bodies have thoroughly reviewed this ingredient as part of a wider evaluation of modified celluloses. EFSA concluded in its re-evaluation that the substance does not raise safety concerns and does not require a specific numerical daily limit. The FDA also permits its use as a binder and filler in food products. Because it is not absorbed by the body, researchers agree that it does not interfere with normal metabolic processes, and it has no toxicological concern when consumed as part of a normal diet.

Where you'll find it

You will find this ingredient in a variety of processed foods, including food supplements, encapsulated nutrients, bakery products, and some fat spreads. On ingredient lists in the United Kingdom and Europe, it is typically declared by its name as ethyl cellulose or by its European code, E462. On US labels, it is listed as ethyl cellulose, where it is often used as a protective coating or a binding agent in dry food preparations.
