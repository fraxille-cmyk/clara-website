Aspartame (E951)

E951SweetenerAvoid · moderate concern

Diets

✓ Suits every diet Clara checks — as an ingredient

Aspartame is an artificial sweetener classified by IARC as Group 2B, possibly carcinogenic to humans, which Clara suggests avoiding to limit exposure.

What it is

Aspartame is an intense artificial sweetener that provides a powerful sweet taste without the bulk or energy of ordinary sugar. It is a synthetic compound made by joining two amino acids, aspartic acid and phenylalanine, which are the building blocks of proteins found naturally in many foods. Because it is about two hundred times sweeter than table sugar, only a tiny amount is needed to achieve the same level of sweetness in food and drink.

How it's made

To manufacture aspartame, producers use a fermentation process to grow the individual amino acids, L-aspartic acid and L-phenylalanine. These amino acids are then chemically joined together in a multi-step synthesis. This process involves protecting certain parts of the molecules so they react in the correct sequence, combining them, and then removing the protective groups. The resulting compound is purified, crystallised, and dried into a fine white powder that dissolves easily in liquids.

Why manufacturers use it

Food manufacturers choose aspartame because it delivers intense sweetness at a fraction of the cost of sugar. It does not have the bitter metallic aftertaste associated with some other artificial sweeteners, making it highly effective for mimicking the flavour profile of sugar. Because such small quantities are required, it allows companies to formulate low-energy products that appeal to shoppers looking to reduce their sugar intake, while maintaining a familiar sweet taste in beverages and desserts.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this AVOID, a moderate-concern additive. IARC classifies aspartame as Group 2B, possibly carcinogenic to humans; EFSA keeps its daily limit of 40 mg per kg of body weight. While the substance is approved by EFSA with this daily limit, choosing to skip this additive is a straightforward way to reduce your exposure to synthetic sweeteners. People with a rare genetic inability to metabolise phenylalanine must also avoid it, which is why products containing it carry a warning label.

What the science says

The safety of this sweetener has been evaluated by major international food authorities for several decades. JECFA and EFSA have reviewed numerous studies on how the body breaks down the substance into phenylalanine, aspartic acid, and methanol. While these regulators maintain that current dietary exposure levels do not exceed the established acceptable daily intake, the recent evaluation by the WHO cancer research agency has raised new questions about its long-term effects, keeping it under active scientific scrutiny.

Where you'll find it

You will find this ingredient in a wide variety of sugar-free and diet products, particularly carbonated soft drinks, flavoured waters, sugar-free chewing gum, yoghurts, and low-energy desserts. On ingredient labels, it is typically declared by its plain name as aspartame, or by its European code, E 951, often accompanied by a warning statement indicating that the product contains a source of phenylalanine.
