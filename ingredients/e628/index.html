Dipotassium guanylate (E628)

Dipotassium guanylate is a savoury flavour enhancer made from yeast that is considered safe, with no established daily limit.

What it is

Dipotassium guanylate is a potassium salt of guanylic acid, a natural compound found in ribonucleic acid, which is the genetic material present in all living cells. It is closely related to other savoury compounds like disodium guanylate and works in a very similar way to enhance the rich, meaty taste known as umami. In nature, these compounds are abundant in foods like dried mushrooms, seaweed, and slow-cooked meats. In packaged foods, it serves as a concentrated seasoning helper to make savoury profiles taste deeper and more satisfying.

How it's made

This ingredient is produced through a controlled biological process rather than chemical synthesis. Manufacturers start with yeast cells or specific strains of bacteria, which are grown in a nutrient-rich broth. These microorganisms naturally produce ribonucleic acid during their growth. The genetic material is then harvested and broken down using natural enzymes to isolate the guanylic acid. Finally, this acid is neutralised with potassium hydroxide to create a stable, highly soluble crystalline powder that can be easily blended into dry seasoning mixes.

Why manufacturers use it

Manufacturers use dipotassium guanylate to boost the savoury, salty, and brothy notes in a recipe. It is incredibly potent, especially when paired with glutamates, where the two ingredients work together to multiply the perceived flavour strength. This synergistic effect allows food companies to use much less salt and fewer expensive meat extracts while maintaining a rich, satisfying taste. Choosing the potassium version over the sodium version also helps manufacturers formulate lower-sodium products without sacrificing the intense savoury profile that consumers expect.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this NEUTRAL, a benign additive. Because it is a simple salt of a compound that your body naturally produces and metabolises every day, it is handled easily by your digestive system. During its re-evaluation, EFSA found no safety concerns and concluded that there is no need to specify an acceptable daily intake. It is a highly predictable substance that behaves just like the natural nucleotides in the foods we eat, posing no toxicological concern for the general population.

What the science says

The scientific consensus on this compound is very stable, with major food safety authorities agreeing on its safety. EFSA thoroughly reviewed the additive alongside other guanylates and confirmed that it does not accumulate in the body or interfere with normal metabolic processes. No limit is established because the amounts used in food are self-limiting; adding too much simply ruins the taste of the product. The only minor consideration is for individuals who must manage their purine intake, as guanylates break down into uric acid in the body.

Where you'll find it

You will typically find this ingredient in highly savoury, processed foods such as instant noodles, dehydrated soups, gravy granules, potato crisps, and pre-packaged savoury snacks. On a label, it is usually listed by its full name as dipotassium guanylate or under its European code, E 628. It is often grouped alongside other flavour enhancers or yeast extracts in the ingredients list, and it may appear on imported products under similar names depending on the country of origin.
