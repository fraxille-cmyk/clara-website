Calcium acetate (E263)

E263AdditiveNeutral · benign

Calcium acetate is a benign mineral salt used to control acidity and keep bread fresh; Clara rates it as a neutral, benign additive.

What it is

Calcium acetate is a mineral salt formed by combining calcium with acetic acid, the very same acid that gives vinegar its characteristic sharp taste and aroma. In the food world, it functions as an acidity regulator and a preservative. While it sounds highly chemical, it behaves in food much like other natural mineral salts, helping to balance flavours and keep baked goods from spoiling too quickly. It is a white, crystalline powder that dissolves easily in water, making it highly useful in liquid and dough mixtures.

How it's made

To manufacture this ingredient on an industrial scale, producers neutralise acetic acid using a calcium source, typically calcium carbonate, which is common limestone, or calcium hydroxide. When these substances react, they create calcium acetate, water, and carbon dioxide. The resulting solution is then filtered to remove any impurities, concentrated, and dried into a fine white powder. This straightforward chemical reaction does not structurally modify the components, resulting in a clean mineral salt that is ready for food formulation.

Why manufacturers use it

Food manufacturers rely on this salt primarily to control acidity and to prevent the growth of unwanted micro-organisms in baked goods. It is particularly valued in commercial bread making because it prevents a specific type of spoilage caused by bacteria that makes the crumb sticky and stringy. By regulating the pH of the dough, it also helps other ingredients perform consistently. It is a highly cost-effective choice compared to organic acids, offering excellent stability during high-temperature baking without altering the final texture of the loaf.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this NEUTRAL, a benign additive. Because it is a simple combination of a common mineral and a natural acid, the body processes it easily, breaking it down into calcium and acetate, which are both normal parts of our daily metabolism. No limit is established for its daily intake because regulators have found no reason to restrict how much we consume. It has a long, clean safety record in food production, with no open concerns raised by any major food safety authority regarding its everyday use.

What the science says

This ingredient has been thoroughly evaluated by global food authorities over several decades. JECFA established its safety profile in 1973, designating its acceptable daily intake as not specified, which is a category reserved for substances of very low toxicity. The FDA affirms it as generally recognised as safe under US regulations, and European authorities permit its use across a wide range of foods. Scientific assessments show that the calcium and acetate ions dissociate completely in the digestive tract and are absorbed and utilised by the body without any adverse effects.

Where you'll find it

You will most commonly spot this ingredient in bakery products, particularly pre-sliced breads, rolls, and cake mixes, where it helps extend shelf life. It also appears in some processed cheese products, canned vegetables, and gelatins to maintain firmness and balance acidity. On ingredient lists, it is typically declared by its plain name as calcium acetate, or by its European code, E263. On US labels, it is listed as calcium acetate, where it is recognised as a direct food substance.
