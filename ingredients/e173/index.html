Aluminium (E173)

E173AdditiveNeutral · limited concern

Aluminium is a metallic silver food colour used only on the outside of cake decorations, rated neutral by Clara because its use is highly restricted.

What it is

Aluminium is a shiny, silver-coloured metal that you likely know from kitchen foil and drink cans. In the food world, it is processed into an extremely fine powder to serve as a metallic surface colour. It is not a nutrient or a food ingredient in the traditional sense, but rather a mineral element used purely for its visual effect. Because it does not dissolve in water or fat, it sits on the surface of food to create a bright, reflective metallic sheen that other food colours cannot easily replicate.

How it's made

The journey of this food colour begins with bauxite ore, which is mined and refined into pure aluminium metal. To make the food-grade powder, manufacturers melt the pure metal and spray it through a special nozzle with high-pressure gas, a process called atomisation that creates tiny droplets. These droplets cool into a fine dust, which is then polished and milled in the presence of food-grade lubricants to create flat, microscopic flakes. These flakes reflect light evenly, producing the characteristic silver metallic finish.

Why manufacturers use it

Food manufacturers use this metal powder for one specific reason: to create a true, reflective silver coating on sweet treats. It is used to decorate the outside of sugar pearls, cake decorations, and wedding pastries. No natural plant extract can produce this bright, mirror-like metallic finish. It is highly stable, meaning it does not fade when exposed to light, heat, or moisture during baking and storage, allowing decorated cakes to keep their festive, metallic appearance for a long time.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this NEUTRAL, a limited-concern additive. This rating reflects its highly restricted use, as it is only permitted on the outer coatings of cake decorations which people generally consume in very small amounts. EFSA evaluated the metal in 2008 and concluded that no acceptable daily intake could be set because the available data on this specific food-colour form are insufficient. However, because we eat so little decorative silver confectionery, our actual dietary exposure to the metal from this additive remains extremely low.

What the science says

Scientific panels have looked closely at how our bodies handle this metal. It passes through the digestive tract mostly unabsorbed, and the tiny fraction that does enter the bloodstream is gradually cleared by the kidneys. Because it is a common element in our environment, we naturally absorb small amounts from drinking water and everyday cooking utensils. Regulators keep a close eye on our total weekly intake from all sources combined, rather than just food additives, to ensure we do not accumulate too much of the metal over a lifetime.

Where you'll find it

You will only find this ingredient on the labels of festive cake decorations, silver sugar pearls, dragées, and wedding cake toppings. On a label, it is usually listed as aluminium, E173, or silver colour. On US labels, it is listed as aluminium powder. Because of strict regulations, it is never mixed into the body of a food product, so you will never see it listed as an ingredient in everyday items like biscuits, breads, sweets, or dairy products.
