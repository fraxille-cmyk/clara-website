Aluminium (E173)

E173AdditiveAvoid · moderate concern

Aluminium is a metallic silver food colour used on cake decorations that Clara rates AVOID because it is easy to exceed the weekly safety limit.

What it is

Aluminium is a silver-coloured metal that is familiar to most of us from kitchen foil and drink cans. In the food industry, it is processed into an extremely fine powder to serve as a metallic pigment. It is used in tiny amounts to give a bright, reflective silver finish to the surface of sweets and cake decorations. Unlike many other food colours, it does not dissolve in water or oil, meaning it sits on top of the food as a shiny, solid coating.

How it's made

This ingredient is produced by grinding pure metallic aluminium into a very fine powder. The raw metal is sourced from natural bauxite ore, which is refined and smelted. To turn the solid metal into a food-grade colour, manufacturers use specialised milling machines that beat the metal into microscopic flakes. These flakes are often treated with food-grade lubricants to prevent them from sticking together, resulting in a free-flowing metallic powder that can be applied to confectionery.

Why manufacturers use it

Manufacturers use this metal powder because it provides a brilliant, mirror-like silver finish that cannot be replicated by organic food dyes. It is highly stable, meaning it does not fade when exposed to light, heat, or moisture during storage. This durability makes it ideal for festive cake decorations, sugar pearls, and wedding cake ornaments that need to look pristine for weeks. It is a specialised, low-cost way to add a premium, metallic look to celebratory treats.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this AVOID, a moderate-concern additive. While it is authorised for limited decorative uses, EFSA established a tolerable weekly intake of 1 mg per kg of body weight in 2008, noting that normal dietary exposure can easily exceed this limit. Because the metal can accumulate in the body over time, consuming decorated confectionery regularly can push your intake past the level that regulators consider advisable. No daily limit is established, but the weekly limit suggests a cautious approach is best.

What the science says

Regulators have closely evaluated how the body processes this metal. EFSA published a major scientific opinion on dietary aluminium, concluding that it can accumulate in various tissues and that many consumers already exceed the tolerable weekly intake through their normal diet. The FDA also regulates its use, restricting it to specific decorative applications to limit overall exposure. Because the metal is present in many everyday foods and packaging materials, scientists advise minimising its use as an unnecessary food colour.

Where you'll find it

You will find this colour almost exclusively on festive cake decorations, silver sugar pearls, dragees, and wedding cake toppings. On ingredient labels, it is typically listed as aluminium or by its European code, E173. On US labels, it is listed as aluminium powder. Because of strict regulations, it is not permitted as a general food dye and is restricted only to the external coating of sugar confectionery used for decorating cakes and pastries.
