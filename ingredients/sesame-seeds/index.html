Sesame seeds

Whole foodGood

Sesame seeds are tiny, oil-rich seeds that add a nutty flavour and a pleasant crunch to foods, rated as a good, whole-food ingredient by Clara.

What it is

Sesame seeds are the small, oval seeds of the Sesamum indicum plant, which is native to tropical regions. They are one of the oldest cultivated oilseed crops in human history, prized for their rich, nutty flavour and high oil content. These seeds come in several varieties, including cream-white, black, and red, and they are closely related to other oil-producing seeds. They are a natural source of plant-based fats, proteins, and dietary fibre, making them a nourishing addition to many traditional dishes around the world.

How it's made

The journey of a sesame seed begins in the seedpods of the sesame plant, which burst open naturally when they reach maturity. Farmers harvest the plants and shake them to release the seeds. Once collected, the seeds are cleaned to remove any dirt, chaff, or stems. Depending on the final product, the seeds may undergo hulling, a mechanical process that removes the dark outer skin to reveal a pearly white seed. Finally, they are dried and sometimes gently toasted to bring out their characteristic warm, nutty aroma before packaging.

Why manufacturers use it

Food makers choose sesame seeds because they offer a unique combination of texture, visual appeal, and rich flavour that is difficult to replicate with synthetic alternatives. The natural oils inside the seeds release a deep, toasted aroma when baked, which enhances the overall sensory experience of breads and crackers. Commercially, they are highly stable due to natural antioxidants that keep the oils from spoiling quickly, giving products a reliable shelf life without requiring extra chemical preservatives.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this GOOD. As a whole food rather than a chemical additive, sesame seeds are a nutritious ingredient that provides beneficial fats and minerals. While they are a known allergen for some individuals, they pose no safety concerns for the general population. No daily intake limit is established for this natural food. Regulatory bodies view them as a standard, wholesome ingredient rather than an additive, meaning they do not carry any restrictive classifications or warnings for those without a specific sensitivity.

What the science says

Scientific assessments focus on the nutritional profile of the seeds and their allergen status. The European Food Safety Authority and the US Food and Drug Administration have evaluated sesame thoroughly, primarily to establish clear labelling rules to protect sensitive individuals. Researchers have confirmed that the seeds are rich in lignans and unsaturated fatty acids, which support normal metabolic function and help maintain healthy cholesterol levels in the body. No adverse effects have been identified in non-allergic populations, and no regulatory limits are deemed necessary.

Where you'll find it

You will find these seeds sprinkled on top of hamburger buns, baked into crackers, and mixed into multi-grain breads. They also appear as the main ingredient in tahini paste, halva, and various savoury Asian sauces. On ingredient lists, they are usually declared plainly as sesame seeds, sesame seed, or sesamzaad, and they are often highlighted in bold or accompanied by an allergen warning to alert sensitive shoppers.
