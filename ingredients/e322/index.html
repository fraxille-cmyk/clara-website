Lecithins (E322)

E322AdditiveNeutral · benign

Lecithins are natural fats used to blend oil and water, keeping your food smooth and stable. Clara rates this NEUTRAL, a benign additive.

What it is

Lecithins are a group of fatty substances found naturally in many plants and animals. If you have ever eaten an egg yolk, you have consumed lecithin, as it is the natural compound that allows egg yolks to bind oil and vinegar into a smooth mayonnaise. In the food industry, it is isolated and used as a versatile tool to manage texture. It consists of molecules that love water on one end and oil on the other, making it an excellent bridge between ingredients that usually do not mix.

How it's made

Most commercial lecithin is extracted from vegetable oils, with soy and sunflower seeds being the most common sources. The seeds are crushed to extract the crude oil, which is then mixed with warm water. This water causes the lecithin to separate from the oil as a thick, gummy substance. This gum is separated using a centrifuge, filtered to remove impurities, and then dried. Sometimes it is gently bleached with hydrogen peroxide to ensure a neutral colour that will not affect the final food product.

Why manufacturers use it

Manufacturers rely on this ingredient to keep oil and water from separating in processed foods. It acts as a reliable emulsifier, ensuring that chocolate stays smooth and glossy rather than turning grey and crumbly. In baked goods, it helps distribute fats evenly through the dough, which improves the volume and keeps the crumb soft for longer. It also stops chocolate from sticking to machinery during production, which speeds up the packaging process and lowers manufacturing costs compared to using synthetic alternatives.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this NEUTRAL, a benign additive. This ingredient has a long safety record at typical food-use levels and is processed by the body just like any other dietary fat. Because it is often derived from soy, individuals with severe soy allergies may need to choose sunflower-derived versions instead, though highly refined lecithin contains very little soy protein. No daily intake limit is established by major food safety authorities because it is considered exceptionally low in risk.

What the science says

The scientific consensus is that this substance is safe for general consumption. EFSA re-evaluated it and confirmed that its use in food does not raise safety concerns, noting that the body breaks it down into nutrients it uses every day to build cell membranes. The FDA recognises it as generally recognised as safe. Because it is a natural component of human cells, researchers have found no evidence of adverse effects even when consumed in quantities much larger than those found in a standard diet.

Where you'll find it

You will find this ingredient in a vast range of products, including chocolate bars, margarine, infant formula, baked goods, and instant gravies. On the label, it might be listed as E322, lecithin, lecithine, lécithine, or lecitina. It is also frequently declared with its source plant, appearing as soy lecithin, lécithine de soja, lecitina de girasol, or sunflower lecithin, which helps shoppers identify potential allergens easily.
