Honey

IngredientAvoid

Honey is a natural syrup made by bees, but because it consists almost entirely of free sugars, Clara advises you to limit it in your diet.

What it is

Honey is a sweet, viscous liquid produced by honeybees from the nectar of flowers. It is a complex natural mixture made up mostly of the simple sugars fructose and glucose, along with water, trace minerals, pollen, and enzymes. Humans have gathered it for thousands of years as a natural sweetener. While it is often perceived as a more wholesome alternative to white table sugar, your body metabolises the sugars in honey in a very similar way.

How it's made

Bees collect nectar from flowering plants and digest it using special enzymes in their honey stomachs. Back at the hive, they deposit the partially digested nectar into wax honeycombs and fan their wings to evaporate the excess water. For commercial use, beekeepers harvest the combs, extract the liquid honey using a centrifuge, and strain it to remove wax and debris. Some honey is pasteurised to prevent fermentation and delay crystallisation, while raw honey is simply strained and bottled.

Why manufacturers use it

Food manufacturers use honey to add sweetness, a distinct floral flavour, and a golden colour to their products. It also acts as a natural humectant, which means it holds onto moisture to keep baked goods soft and prevent them from drying out. Because honey has a positive, natural image, brands often use it to replace refined sugar on the ingredient list, appealing to shoppers who are looking for less processed options.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this AVOID. Although it is a natural product, honey is essentially a concentrated source of free sugars, which can lead to rapid rises in blood glucose when consumed in large amounts. Because it is the sole ingredient here, this sugar is by definition dominant, and Clara's established added-sugar dominance rule applies. No upper limit is established for honey itself, but public health guidelines recommend limiting all free sugars to protect your metabolic health.

What the science says

Major food authorities have evaluated honey primarily as a source of free sugars in the diet. The World Health Organisation recommends that free sugars make up less than ten per cent of our daily energy intake to support overall health. While honey contains tiny amounts of antioxidants, minerals, and enzymes, researchers agree that these are present in such small quantities that you would have to consume harmful amounts of sugar to get any nutritional benefit from them.

Where you'll find it

You will find honey sold on its own in jars, or as an ingredient in breakfast cereals, granolas, yoghurts, marinades, and baked goods. On ingredient labels, it may be listed under its English name or European translations such as miel, miele, honig, honing, honning, honung, or mel. It might also be specified by its source or texture, appearing as acacia honey, blossom honey, clear honey, runny honey, set honey, or wildflower honey.
