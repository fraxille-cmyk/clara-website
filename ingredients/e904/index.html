Shellac (E904)

Shellac is a natural resin secreted by the lac insect, used to give food a shiny protective coating. Clara rates it as a benign additive.

What it is

Shellac is a natural resin secreted by the tiny lac insect, Kerria lacca, which is native to the forests of India and Thailand. The insect deposits this sticky substance onto tree branches to create a protective cocoon for its eggs. In its refined form, it is a versatile, edible wax that creates a tough, moisture-resistant barrier. While it is an animal product, it is not a meat product, though shoppers seeking a vegan lifestyle choose to avoid it because it is harvested from insect secretions.

How it's made

The raw material, known as sticklac, is harvested by gathering the resin-coated twigs from host trees. These twigs are crushed and washed to remove insect debris, natural red pigments, and other impurities, leaving behind seedlac. This semi-refined resin is then melted or dissolved in alcohol, filtered to remove any remaining insoluble matter, and dried into thin, brittle sheets. These sheets are broken into flakes, which are dissolved in food-grade alcohol to create a liquid glaze ready for food application.

Why manufacturers use it

Food manufacturers use this resin primarily as a glazing agent to give products a highly polished, appealing shine. Beyond its cosmetic value, it acts as a protective barrier that seals in moisture, which prevents sweets from sticking together or drying out. It also protects fresh fruits from losing water and shrinking during transport, extending their shelf life. Because it is highly stable and resists humidity, it performs better than many plant-based starches or synthetic alternatives in warm climates.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this NEUTRAL, a benign additive. Because it is a natural resin that passes through the human digestive system without being absorbed or broken down into harmful components, it is considered highly stable and non-reactive. EFSA considers it safe and has not specified an acceptable daily intake, meaning there is no restrictive limit on how much can be consumed. It has no known adverse effects on human digestion or metabolism, and it is not associated with any sensitivity or intolerance.

What the science says

Scientific panels have thoroughly evaluated this ingredient and found no safety concerns. EFSA re-evaluated the substance and confirmed that its use as a glazing agent does not pose a risk to consumers, maintaining its status without a numerical daily limit. JECFA also reviewed its safety and concluded that its long history of use in food is supported by its lack of absorption in the body. Because it is insoluble in water and passes through the gut unchanged, researchers find no evidence of accumulation or biological activity.

Where you'll find it

You will find this ingredient on labels listed as shellac or by its European number, E 904. It is commonly applied to confectionery, such as chocolate-coated nuts, jelly beans, and hard-shelled sweets, to give them a glossy finish. It is also sprayed onto the skins of fresh citrus fruits, apples, and melons to replace the natural waxes lost during washing. On some products, particularly in bakery and confectionery items, it may simply be declared as a glazing agent.
