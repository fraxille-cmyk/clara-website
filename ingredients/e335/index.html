Sodium tartrates (E335)

E335AdditiveNeutral · benign

Sodium tartrates are benign salts of natural tartaric acid used to control acidity in food, with a long history of safe use.

What it is

Sodium tartrates are the sodium salts of tartaric acid, an organic acid found naturally in many fruits, most notably grapes. In the food industry, they serve as acidity regulators and emulsifiers, helping to maintain the right pH balance and texture in processed foods. They are closely related to cream of tartar, which is a potassium-based version of the same acid. To a shopper, they represent a familiar family of fruit-derived compounds that help keep food stable and fresh.

How it's made

These salts are produced by neutralising tartaric acid with sodium carbonate or sodium hydroxide. The tartaric acid itself is typically obtained as a natural by-product of the winemaking industry, where it crystallises out of grape juice during fermentation. Once the acid is combined with the sodium source in a controlled aqueous solution, the resulting sodium tartrate crystals are filtered, purified, and dried into a fine white powder ready for food manufacturing.

Why manufacturers use it

Manufacturers rely on sodium tartrates because they are exceptionally good at regulating acidity and acting as buffering agents, which prevents food from becoming too sour or too alkaline. They also act as sequestrants, binding to trace metals to prevent oxidation and colour changes. In bakery products, they assist in leavening by reacting with baking soda to release carbon dioxide, ensuring an even rise and a light texture that is difficult to achieve with liquid acids.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this NEUTRAL, a benign additive. It is a mild substance with a long history of use in everyday foods. EFSA established a daily limit of 240 mg per kg of body weight for tartaric acid and its salts in 2020, which is a very generous allowance that a typical diet is unlikely to exceed. There are no open safety concerns, and the body processes these salts naturally, absorbing a small portion and excreting the rest without any accumulation or disruption.

What the science says

Major food safety authorities have thoroughly evaluated these compounds and found no cause for concern. EFSA re-evaluated the group in 2020 and confirmed their safety, establishing a group acceptable daily intake for tartaric acid and its sodium, potassium, and calcium salts. The FDA also recognises the ingredient as generally recognised as safe. Because it behaves just like the natural acids found in grapes, the body metabolises it easily, and scientific reviews show no adverse effects on human biology.

Where you'll find it

You will frequently find this ingredient in bakery products, such as biscuits, cakes, and self-raising flour mixes, where it helps with leavening. It also appears in jams, jellies, canned fruits, and some processed meat products to control acidity. On ingredient lists, it is typically declared by its name as sodium tartrates, or by its European code, E335, and sometimes you might see it written as monosodium tartrate or disodium tartrate depending on the specific salt form used.
