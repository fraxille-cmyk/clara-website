Coconut oil

Processed ingredientNeutral

Coconut oil is a plant-based fat extracted from coconuts, used to give foods a rich texture and a stable shelf life.

What it is

Coconut oil is a plant-based fat extracted from the kernel or meat of mature coconuts. At room temperature, it is a semi-solid, creamy white fat that melts easily with a little warmth. Chemically, it is composed almost entirely of saturated fatty acids, particularly a type called lauric acid. Unlike many other plant oils that remain liquid, its high saturated fat content gives it a firm structure similar to butter, making it a popular plant-based alternative in modern kitchens and food manufacturing.

How it's made

The journey begins with harvesting mature coconuts from the coconut palm. The outer husk is removed, and the white kernel inside is collected. For virgin oil, this wet meat is pressed quickly to squeeze out the oil and water, which are then separated. For standard refined coconut oil, the coconut meat is dried into copra. This dried meat is pressed to extract the crude oil, which then undergoes refining, bleaching, and deodorising to remove any strong coconut flavours, colours, and free fatty acids, leaving a neutral, stable fat.

Why manufacturers use it

Food makers value coconut oil for its high melting point and exceptional stability. Because it resists oxidation, it does not go rancid easily, which helps packaged foods stay fresh on shelves for much longer. It provides a smooth, melting texture in dairy-free cheeses, spreads, and vegan chocolates, mimicking the mouthfeel of animal fats. Its high smoke point also makes it ideal for frying and baking, as it can withstand high temperatures without breaking down or releasing unpleasant flavours.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this NEUTRAL. Coconut oil is a refined vegetable oil, high in saturated fat. While it is a natural plant extract, its high concentration of saturated fatty acids means it should be consumed in moderation as part of a balanced diet. The body metabolises these fats differently depending on their chain length, but they still contribute significantly to daily energy intake. No regulatory body has established a specific upper limit for coconut oil itself, though general dietary guidelines recommend limiting overall saturated fat intake.

What the science says

Major food authorities have evaluated coconut oil as a dietary fat rather than a food additive. The European Food Safety Authority and the US Food and Drug Administration view it as a standard food ingredient that is safe for general consumption. Scientific discussions focus primarily on how its specific fatty acids, like lauric acid, behave in the human body compared to other vegetable oils. While some studies suggest it raises beneficial lipid markers, regulators agree that it behaves like other saturated fats and should be balanced with unsaturated fats.

Where you'll find it

You will find this ingredient in a wide variety of supermarket products, especially plant-based and vegan alternatives like dairy-free cheeses, margarines, and non-dairy ice creams. It is also a common choice in biscuits, cakes, infant formulas, and confectionery. On packaging, it is simply listed by its plain name as coconut oil, hydrogenated coconut oil, or sometimes grouped under the broader term vegetable oil or vegetable fat.
