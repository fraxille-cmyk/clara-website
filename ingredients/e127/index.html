Erythrosine (E127)

E127AdditiveAvoid · high concern

Erythrosine is a synthetic cherry-red food colour that Clara rates AVOID because the FDA revoked its food authorisation in the US in January 2025.

What it is

Erythrosine is a synthetic food colour that delivers an intense, bright cherry-red hue. It is an organoiodine compound, which means it contains iodine atoms bound to an organic structure. Unlike natural red colours derived from beetroot or cochineal insects, this substance is entirely man-made. It is highly stable under heat and light, making it a reliable way to keep foods looking vibrant even after long periods on a shop shelf.

How it's made

This colour is manufactured in a laboratory setting through a chemical process. It starts with fluorescein, a synthetic organic compound, which is treated with iodine. This chemical reaction attaches four iodine atoms to the fluorescein molecule, creating the specific structure of erythrosine. The resulting powder is then purified and standardised as a sodium salt, ready to be dissolved into liquid mixtures during food manufacturing.

Why manufacturers use it

Food manufacturers choose this colour because it provides a brilliant, stable pinkish-red shade that does not fade when exposed to light or high temperatures during processing. Natural red colours often turn brown or break down when heated, but this synthetic alternative remains bright. It is also highly cost-effective, as a tiny amount can colour a large batch of food, helping manufacturers maintain a consistent and appealing appearance in their products.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this AVOID, a high-concern additive. The main reason for this rating is that the FDA revoked the food authorisation of FD&C Red No. 3, which is the American name for erythrosine, in January 2025, with the ban taking full effect in January 2027. While EFSA maintains a daily limit of 0.1 mg per kg of body weight, the regulatory actions in the United States have prompted Clara to advise shoppers to steer clear of this synthetic dye.

What the science says

Scientists and regulators have evaluated this substance over several decades. IARC classifies erythrosine as Group 3, not classifiable as to its carcinogenicity to humans. However, concerns regarding its effects on thyroid function, due to its high iodine content, have led to strict limits on how much can be consumed. While EFSA re-evaluated the colour in 2011 and kept its low acceptable daily intake, the FDA decision to revoke its use in food reflects a growing regulatory consensus to minimise exposure.

Where you'll find it

In the European Union, this colour is highly restricted and is only permitted in cocktail cherries, candied cherries, and Bigarreaux cherries in syrup. You will find it listed on ingredient labels as erythrosine, E127, or E 127. On US labels, where it has been widely used in decorative cake icings, candies, and fruit snacks, it is listed as FD&C Red No. 3.
