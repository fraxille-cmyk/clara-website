A benign plant- or animal-derived emulsifier used to blend oils and water, which regulators consider safe with no daily limit established.

What it is

This ingredient is a food additive used to bind oil and water together, preventing them from separating in processed foods. Chemically, it is a combination of citric acid, which occurs naturally in citrus fruits, and mono- and diglycerides, which are fats similar to those found in everyday cooking oils. By joining these components, chemists create a substance that has a strong affinity for both water and fat, making it an excellent stabiliser for complex food mixtures.

How it's made

To manufacture this ingredient, producers start with fats, which are typically sourced from plant oils like palm, rapeseed, or sunflower oil, though animal fats can sometimes be used. These fats are broken down into mono- and diglycerides. Next, these glycerides are reacted with citric acid under controlled heating. This chemical modification alters the structure of the fats, creating a specialised compound that is then purified, neutralised, and dried into a powder or a thick, waxy paste ready for food production.

Why manufacturers use it

Food makers rely on this ingredient because it is a highly efficient emulsifier that performs beautifully in challenging conditions. It helps to distribute fat evenly throughout a product, which improves texture, creates a smooth mouthfeel, and extends shelf life by keeping the recipe stable over time. It is particularly valued in baking because it strengthens gluten networks in dough, and in sausages or processed meats because it prevents fat from separating during cooking, beating simpler alternatives in stability.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this NEUTRAL, a benign additive. Because the body breaks this substance down into ordinary dietary fats and citric acid, which are metabolised through normal digestive pathways, it is considered of very low concern. EFSA did not specify an acceptable daily intake in their 2020 evaluation due to its low toxicity, meaning no limit is established for its daily consumption. It is widely recognised as safe for the general population, with no known adverse effects from normal dietary exposure.

What the science says

Major food safety authorities have thoroughly evaluated this substance and found no reason for concern. The European Food Safety Authority re-evaluated the additive and confirmed that it does not pose a safety risk, choosing not to set a numerical limit on its daily intake. In the United States, the Food and Drug Administration permits its use as a safe food substance. Because it behaves just like the fats and acids we eat every day, scientists agree that it is processed naturally by the body.

Where you'll find it

You will frequently spot this ingredient on labels in the bakery aisle, where it keeps bread soft, and in margarine, spreads, and salad dressings to maintain a creamy consistency. It is also common in processed meats, instant gravies, and infant formulas. On packaging, it is usually listed as citric acid esters of mono- and diglycerides, citric acid esters of mono- and diglycerides of fatty acids, or simply by its European code, E472c.
