Potassium nitrite (E249)

E249AdditiveAvoid · high concern

Potassium nitrite is a preservative used in cured meats that can form undesirable compounds called nitrosamines during cooking or digestion.

What it is

Potassium nitrite is an inorganic salt that consists of potassium and nitrogen. It is very similar to its more common sibling, sodium nitrite, which is widely used to cure meats. In its pure form, it is a white or slightly yellowish crystalline powder that absorbs moisture from the air very easily. While it occurs naturally in very small amounts in some soils and plants as part of the nitrogen cycle, the substance added to our food is always manufactured in an industrial setting to ensure its purity and strength.

How it's made

To manufacture this additive on a commercial scale, chemical producers start with potassium hydroxide or potassium carbonate. They react these alkaline substances with nitrogen oxides, which are gases containing nitrogen and oxygen. This chemical reaction yields a liquid solution of potassium nitrite. The solution is then carefully filtered to remove any impurities, concentrated by evaporating the water, and cooled to crystallise the salt. Finally, the crystals are separated, dried, and packaged as a fine powder, ready to be shipped to food processing facilities where it is blended into curing salts.

Why manufacturers use it

Food manufacturers rely on this additive because it performs several critical roles in cured meats. It stops the growth of dangerous bacteria that can multiply in oxygen-free environments, keeping packaged meats edible for much longer. It also reacts with the natural iron-containing proteins in meat to lock in a bright pink colour, which shoppers expect to see in ham and bacon. Without it, cured meats would turn an unappealing greyish-brown colour. Additionally, it slows down the oxidation of fats, which helps to maintain a fresh flavour and stops the product from going rancid.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this AVOID, a high-concern additive. The primary reason for this rating is that nitrites can react with natural protein fragments in meat, especially under high heat or in the acidic environment of the stomach, to form compounds called nitrosamines. The EFSA re-evaluated this additive and established an acceptable daily intake of 0.07 mg per kg of body weight, noting that exposure to nitrosamines should be kept as low as possible. Because of how these compounds behave in the body, we recommend choosing nitrite-free alternatives whenever you can.

What the science says

Major food safety authorities have scrutinised this substance for decades. JECFA and EFSA have both set strict limits on its use in food to minimise the formation of nitrosamines in the human body. The IARC classifies ingested nitrates or nitrites under conditions that result in endogenous nitrosation as Group 2A, probably carcinogenic to humans. Scientific discussions continue to focus on how much nitrite is actually converted into these compounds during digestion, and regulators regularly review the maximum levels permitted in cured meat products to balance food safety with these long-term physiological considerations.

Where you'll find it

You will primarily find this ingredient in processed and cured meat products. It is commonly used in traditional hams, bacon, sausages, salami, corned beef, and various deli meats. On a food label, it is typically listed by its full name as potassium nitrite, or by its European code, E249. Sometimes it is grouped under a generic term like curing salt. Because it is highly effective at preserving meat, it is rarely found in fresh, unprocessed meats, which means you can easily bypass it by choosing fresh cuts or products explicitly labelled as nitrite-free.
