Vitamin D3 is a crucial nutrient added to foods to boost their nutritional value, helping your body absorb calcium and keep your bones strong.

What it is

Vitamin D3, also known as cholecalciferol, is a fat-soluble vitamin that our bodies naturally produce when sunlight hits our skin. It is one of the two main forms of vitamin D used to fortify food, the other being vitamin D2, or ergocalciferol. In the body, this nutrient acts more like a hormone, playing a vital role in helping us absorb calcium from our food and maintaining healthy bones, teeth, and muscles. Since many people do not get enough sunlight, adding it to everyday foods helps maintain healthy levels.

How it's made

To make the vitamin D3 used in food fortification, manufacturers typically start with lanolin, which is a natural waxy substance extracted from sheep wool. The lanolin is purified to obtain cholesterol, which is then chemically converted into a compound called 7-dehydrocholesterol. This compound is exposed to ultraviolet light in a laboratory, mimicking the natural process that occurs in human skin when exposed to the sun. The resulting vitamin D3 is then refined, crystallised, and dissolved in oil or dispersed as a fine powder so it can be easily added to food products.

Why manufacturers use it

Manufacturers add vitamin D3 to foods primarily as a fortificant to improve the nutritional profile of their products. It is an effective way to help consumers meet their daily nutritional requirements, especially in regions with limited sunlight. From a technical standpoint, vitamin D3 is highly stable during food processing and has a long shelf life. It does not alter the flavour, colour, or texture of the food, making it an ideal choice for enriching products without changing the sensory experience that shoppers expect.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this NEUTRAL, a benign additive. Vitamin D3 is a vital nutrient that is entirely harmless at the amounts typically added to food labels. While extremely high doses from concentrated supplements can lead to an excess of calcium in the blood, the levels found in fortified foods are carefully controlled and present no risk of overexposure. No upper limit is established for fortified foods themselves, as the quantities used are designed to safely support daily nutrition rather than replace medical supplementation.

What the science says

Major food safety authorities have thoroughly evaluated vitamin D3 and support its use as a food fortificant. The European Food Safety Authority has established safe upper intake levels for daily consumption from all sources, which are far higher than the amounts found in fortified foods. The US Food and Drug Administration permits its addition to specific food categories to help public nutrition. Scientific consensus confirms that vitamin D3 is highly bioavailable, meaning the human body can easily absorb and metabolise this form to support skeletal health and immune function.

Where you'll find it

You will commonly find this ingredient in fortified plant-based milks, breakfast cereals, margarines, spreads, orange juices, and infant formulas. On the ingredient list, it may be declared simply as vitamin D, vitamin D3, or by its technical names such as cholecalciferol, colecalciferol, or ergocalciferol. It is also frequently listed on nutritional panels to show how much of your daily recommended intake a single serving provides.
