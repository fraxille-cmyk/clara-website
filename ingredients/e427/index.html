Cassia gum (E427)

E427AdditiveAvoid · moderate concern

A natural plant gum used to thicken foods, but Clara advises avoiding it because European regulators found the safety data insufficient to set a daily limit.

What it is

Cassia gum is a natural thickening agent that comes from the seeds of the Cassia tora and Cassia obtusifolia plants, which belong to the legume family. Chemically, it is a galactomannan, a type of soluble dietary fibre similar to guar gum and locust bean gum. When mixed with water, it forms a thick, stable gel. It is used in small amounts to give foods a smooth, satisfying texture and to keep ingredients from separating during storage.

How it's made

The journey of this ingredient begins with the harvesting of wild cassia plants. The seeds are collected, cleaned, and dehulled to isolate the endosperm, which is the nutrient-rich inner part of the seed. This endosperm is then split and milled into a fine, pale yellow powder. To ensure it meets food-grade standards, the powder undergoes a purification process using isopropyl alcohol to wash away unwanted compounds, leaving behind a clean, soluble fibre that dissolves easily in warm water.

Why manufacturers use it

Food manufacturers value this ingredient because it is an exceptionally efficient thickener, especially when combined with other plant gums like carrageenan or xanthan gum. This synergy allows makers to use much less total additive while achieving a firm, appealing texture. It is highly stable under heat and in acidic conditions, making it an ideal choice for products that undergo pasteurisation or sterilisation. It is also a cost-effective alternative to other gelling agents.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this AVOID, a moderate-concern additive. While it is a natural plant fibre, EFSA concluded in 2006 that the available data were insufficient to establish an acceptable daily intake and requested additional safety data. Because regulators have not been able to set a clear daily limit due to these gaps in the scientific literature, Clara recommends choosing products with more thoroughly researched alternatives until further safety evaluations are completed.

What the science says

The scientific consensus on this ingredient is incomplete. EFSA evaluated the substance in 2006 and determined that the existing animal studies and dietary assessments did not provide enough robust evidence to define a specific, safe daily limit. No toxicological concern was proven, but the lack of comprehensive long-term data prompted regulators to ask for further research. In the United States, the FDA reviewed a GRAS notice in 2009, allowing its use in specific food categories under strict limits.

Where you'll find it

You will most commonly find this ingredient in dairy-based desserts, processed cheeses, ice creams, sauces, and gravies, where it helps maintain a creamy consistency. On ingredient lists, it is typically declared by its plain name as cassia gum, or by its European code, E427. Because it is often used in combination with other stabilisers, you will frequently see it listed alongside carrageenan, locust bean gum, or xanthan gum on the back of the packet.
