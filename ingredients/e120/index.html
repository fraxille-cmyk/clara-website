Cochineal (carmine) (E120)

A natural red colour made from crushed insects that can trigger allergic reactions in some people due to leftover protein impurities.

What it is

This is a traditional red food colour that has been used for centuries. It is a natural pigment derived from the dried bodies of female cochineal insects, which are tiny scale bugs that live on cacti. The active colouring agent is carminic acid, which produces a brilliant, long-lasting crimson hue. Because it comes from an animal source, it is not suitable for vegetarians or vegans, and it is often a surprise to shoppers to find insect-derived ingredients in their everyday sweet treats.

How it's made

The production process begins by harvesting female cochineal insects from cactus plantations, primarily in South America. These insects are dried in the sun, crushed, and then heated to extract the carminic acid. To make the commercial colour known as carmine, this extract is treated with aluminium salts to precipitate a bright red lake pigment. This pigment is then filtered, purified, and dried into a powder, though tiny traces of insect proteins can remain in the final ingredient.

Why manufacturers use it

Food manufacturers choose this colour because it is incredibly stable and vibrant. It resists fading under intense heat, light, and acidic conditions, outperforming many plant-based red colours like beetroot juice. This stability makes it highly cost-effective for processed foods that need to sit on supermarket shelves for months without losing their appealing pink or red appearance. It is the go-to choice when a manufacturer wants a bold, reliable red shade.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this AVOID, a moderate-concern additive. While it is a natural extract, the main issue lies with the tiny insect proteins that remain after processing. EFSA raised concerns in 2015 about the allergenic potential of these protein impurities, which can trigger severe allergic reactions, including breathing difficulties and skin swelling, in sensitive individuals. Because of these reactions, regulators now require it to be clearly labelled so that people who are sensitive can steer clear of it.

What the science says

Scientific bodies have evaluated this colour thoroughly to ensure its safety. EFSA re-evaluated the substance in 2015 and established a daily limit of 2.5 mg per kg of body weight, noting that exposure for most people remains well within this limit. However, the panel emphasised that the protein impurities are known allergens. The FDA also updated its rules to require clear labelling by name rather than hiding it under generic terms like artificial colour, specifically to protect consumers who are prone to allergic reactions.

Where you'll find it

You will find this colour in a wide variety of bright pink and red products, including strawberry yoghurts, sweets, ice creams, soft drinks, and glacé cherries. On ingredient lists in the United Kingdom and Europe, it is typically declared as cochineal, carmine, carminic acid, or by its E-number, E120. On US labels, you will see it listed as cochineal extract or carmine, as the FDA requires its specific name to be declared to warn sensitive shoppers.
