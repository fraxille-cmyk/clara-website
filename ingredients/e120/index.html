Cochineal (carmine) (E120)

E120AdditiveAvoid · limited concern

Cochineal is a vivid red food colour derived from crushed insects that can trigger allergic reactions in sensitive individuals.

What it is

Cochineal is a natural red dye that has been used for centuries to colour textiles and food. It is also known as carmine or carminic acid, which is the actual pigment molecule that gives the substance its deep crimson hue. Unlike synthetic red dyes made from petroleum, this colour comes from a biological source, specifically the dried bodies of female scale insects that live on cacti. It is highly valued in the food industry because it produces a beautiful, stable red shade that does not fade easily under light or heat.

How it's made

The production of this colour begins with harvesting female cochineal insects from prickly pear cacti, primarily in South America. Once collected, the insects are dried in the sun or in ovens. The dried insects are then crushed and immersed in an acidic water solution to extract the carminic acid. To turn this extract into the commercial pigment known as carmine, the carminic acid is precipitated with aluminium ions. This chemical process creates a bright red lake pigment, which is then filtered, purified, dried, and standardised into a powder or liquid ready for food manufacturing.

Why manufacturers use it

Food manufacturers choose this colour because it is incredibly stable and vibrant. It resists the fading that usually happens when foods are exposed to bright supermarket lights, high processing temperatures, or acidic environments. This makes it far more reliable than other natural red colours derived from beetroot or berries, which tend to turn brown or degrade quickly. By using this insect-derived dye, companies can ensure their products maintain an appealing, fresh-looking red or pink appearance throughout their entire shelf life at a relatively low cost.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this AVOID, a limited-concern additive. While it is approved for use, the main issue is that the protein residues left over from the insect extraction process can trigger severe allergic reactions, including swelling and breathing difficulties, in sensitive individuals. Because of these allergen concerns, EFSA maintains a strict daily limit of 2.5 mg per kg of body weight for carminic acid. It is not considered a general hazard for the wider population, but those with known sensitivities or those who follow vegan, vegetarian, or kosher diets must steer clear of it entirely.

What the science says

Scientific bodies have thoroughly evaluated this colour to understand its impact on human health. JECFA and EFSA have both reviewed the safety data and concluded that the pigment itself does not pose a risk of genetic damage or reproductive issues. However, both organisations have highlighted that the manufacturing process leaves behind tiny amounts of insect proteins, which are highly allergenic. Because of this, EFSA recommends that the presence of these protein impurities be kept as low as possible to protect consumers, and both regulators continue to monitor reports of hypersensitivity reactions.

Where you'll find it

You will find this ingredient in a wide range of pink and red foods, including strawberry yoghurts, ice creams, fruit fillings, sweets, glacé cherries, and soft drinks. On ingredient lists, it can be declared under several different names depending on the region. You might see it listed as cochineal, carmine, carminic acid, natural red four, or simply by its European additive code, E120. Because it is an animal product, its presence is a key detail for anyone looking to avoid non-vegetarian ingredients in packaged foods.
