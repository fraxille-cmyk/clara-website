Blackberries

Blackberries are a whole, nutrient-dense fruit packed with natural fibre, vitamins, and minerals that make them a wonderful addition to any diet.

What it is

Blackberries are the edible, dark purple fruit of the bramble, a woody shrub belonging to the rose family. They are aggregate fruits, meaning each berry is composed of dozens of tiny, juice-filled drupelets clustered together around a central core. Historically gathered from wild hedgerows across Europe and Asia, they are now cultivated globally. They have a deep, complex flavour that balances natural sweetness with a sharp tartness, and their rich colour comes from natural plant pigments that are highly valued for their nutritional properties.

How it's made

These berries are grown on farms where they are carefully cultivated, harvested by hand or machine when fully ripe, and then washed and sorted. Unlike processed additives, they do not undergo complex chemical extraction or synthesis. For packaged foods, they are either used fresh, frozen immediately after picking to preserve their structure and nutrients, or gently dried. Sometimes they are pressed to create a rich juice or pureed to be incorporated into various recipes, keeping the natural fruit structure intact without any chemical modification.

Why manufacturers use it

Food manufacturers choose to include these berries in their products to provide a genuine, natural fruit flavour and a vibrant, appealing purple colour without relying on synthetic additives. They add a pleasant, chewy texture to baked goods and breakfast cereals, and their natural pectin helps to thicken jams and fruit spreads naturally. Commercially, using real fruit on an ingredient list appeals strongly to shoppers looking for wholesome, minimally processed options, making it a premium choice that beats cheap artificial flavourings and synthetic colourings in high-quality products.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this GOOD. As a whole, natural fruit, there are no safety concerns or regulatory limits associated with eating them. They are packed with dietary fibre, which supports a healthy digestive system, alongside essential vitamins and minerals that help the body function at its best. No regulator has established an upper intake limit for this wholesome food, and it is widely celebrated by nutritionists as a highly beneficial part of a balanced diet that supports overall physical well-being and vitality.

What the science says

Scientific bodies and food safety authorities worldwide view this fruit as a highly nutritious whole food. EFSA and the FDA recognise it as a rich source of vitamin C, manganese, and dietary fibre, all of which play vital roles in supporting the body's natural defences and metabolic processes. Researchers have studied the natural dark pigments in the fruit, known as anthocyanins, which act as antioxidants in the body. Because it is a traditional agricultural product rather than a food additive, it has never required a formal safety re-evaluation or an acceptable daily intake limit.

Where you'll find it

You will find this ingredient listed simply as blackberries, blackberry puree, or blackberry juice concentrate on a wide variety of labels. It is a common feature in the freezer aisle as frozen whole fruit, and it frequently appears in breakfast cereals, granolas, fruit bars, yoghurts, and organic fruit spreads. It is also a popular addition to smoothies, juices, herbal teas, and baked goods like pies and muffins, where it is always listed by its plain, recognisable name rather than any complex chemical code or E-number.
