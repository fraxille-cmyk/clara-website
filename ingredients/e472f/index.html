A synthetic emulsifier used to improve the texture of bread and baked goods, rated neutral by Clara with a safe daily limit established by regulators.

What it is

This ingredient is a synthetic food additive used to blend ingredients that would normally separate, such as oil and water. It is a complex compound made by combining plant-based fats with two organic acids: acetic acid, which gives vinegar its sour taste, and tartaric acid, which occurs naturally in grapes. By linking these components together, chemists create a versatile emulsifier that helps maintain a consistent structure in processed foods, particularly in baked goods where gas bubbles need to be trapped in the dough.

How it's made

The production process starts with fats, usually derived from vegetable oils like palm, rapeseed, or soybean oil. These fats are broken down into mono- and diglycerides. Manufacturers then chemically react these glycerides with acetic anhydride and tartaric acid under controlled, high-temperature conditions. This chemical modification alters the molecular structure, creating a hybrid emulsifier. The final mixture is refined, neutralised, and purified into a powder or a sticky liquid ready for industrial food production.

Why manufacturers use it

Food manufacturers rely on this additive primarily in commercial baking because it strengthens dough. It interacts with the gluten network in wheat flour, making the dough more elastic and better at holding gas. This results in a larger loaf volume, a finer crumb structure, and a softer texture that stays fresh on supermarket shelves for longer. It is a highly efficient and cost-effective choice compared to natural emulsifiers like egg yolk, as it performs consistently under intense mechanical mixing.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this NEUTRAL, a limited-concern additive. This rating reflects its synthetic origin and the fact that regulators have set a specific limit on how much you should consume. EFSA established an acceptable daily intake of 240 milligrams per kilogram of body weight in 2020. Because it is broken down in your digestive system into normal dietary components like fatty acids and organic acids, it is processed easily by the body and does not accumulate in your tissues.

What the science says

Scientific panels have thoroughly evaluated this substance and found no safety concerns when it is used within established limits. EFSA re-evaluated the additive in 2020 and confirmed its safety profile, noting that the body metabolises it into compounds that are already part of a normal diet. The FDA has also affirmed its status as generally recognised as safe. There are no open concerns regarding its use, and it does not require any special warning labels on food packaging.

Where you'll find it

You will most commonly find this ingredient in pre-packaged breads, rolls, croissants, and other baked goods that require a long shelf life and a soft texture. It also appears in some cake mixes, biscuits, and powdered beverage creamers. On ingredient lists, it is typically declared by its full name, mixed acetic and tartaric acid esters of mono- and diglycerides, or by its European code, E472f, while on US labels it is often listed under related glyceride ester categories.
