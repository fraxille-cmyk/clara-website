L-cysteine is a natural amino acid used to make dough easier to handle and stretch, rated neutral by Clara with no safety concerns.

What it is

L-cysteine is a naturally occurring amino acid, one of the fundamental building blocks of protein that your body uses every day. In the human body, it plays a key role in building proteins and supporting metabolic functions. In the food industry, it is used as a flour treatment agent to modify the physical properties of dough. While it is found naturally in many protein-rich foods like poultry, eggs, and grains, the version added to your bread is a concentrated form that acts specifically on the gluten structure.

How it's made

Historically, this amino acid was extracted from natural protein sources such as feathers or animal hair. Today, the L-cysteine used in food is primarily produced through modern fermentation processes using bacteria, or through chemical synthesis from simpler organic compounds. This synthetic or microbial route ensures a highly pure, consistent, and reliable ingredient that meets strict food-grade standards. The resulting white crystalline powder dissolves easily in water, making it simple for bakeries to distribute evenly throughout their flour mixtures.

Why manufacturers use it

Manufacturers use this ingredient as a dough conditioner to improve the texture and elasticity of baked goods. When added to flour, it breaks down some of the strong protein bonds in gluten, which relaxes the dough and makes it much easier to stretch, shape, and mould. This prevents the dough from shrinking back during commercial baking processes, leading to an even size and a softer, more uniform crumb structure in the finished bread. It is a highly efficient tool that saves time and energy in high-speed bakery production lines.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this NEUTRAL, a benign additive. Because it is a normal amino acid that the human body regularly metabolises from daily food, it does not pose a risk to human health when consumed in normal dietary amounts. JECFA evaluated the substance in 1987 and determined that no limit is established for its daily intake, meaning it is considered safe for its intended technological uses without a specific restrictive limit. There are no known adverse effects or open safety concerns associated with its use in bakery products.

What the science says

Scientific bodies have thoroughly evaluated this ingredient and confirmed its safety. The European Food Safety Authority analysed its use and found no safety concerns when it is added to flour. In the United States, the Food and Drug Administration recognises it as generally recognised as safe for use as a dough conditioner. Because it behaves exactly like the amino acids already present in your diet, the body processes it in the same natural way, and researchers have found no evidence of accumulation or unwanted biological reactions.

Where you'll find it

You will primarily find this ingredient in commercially baked goods, including sliced bread, burger buns, wraps, and pastry doughs. On food labels, it is typically listed by its name as L-cysteine, L-cysteine hydrochloride, or by its European code E920. On US labels, it is commonly declared as L-cysteine or simply as a dough conditioner within the ingredient list. Because it is used in very small quantities to treat the flour, it is often near the end of the ingredient declaration.
