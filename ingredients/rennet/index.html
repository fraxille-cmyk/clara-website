Rennet

IngredientNeutral

Rennet is a natural set of enzymes used to curdle milk and turn it into cheese, making it a traditional and neutral part of cheesemaking.

What it is

Rennet is a complex of enzymes that curdles milk, which is the essential first step in making almost all traditional cheeses. The key active component is an enzyme called chymosin, which specifically targets milk proteins to make them clump together. Historically, it is associated with dairy production, acting as the natural catalyst that separates liquid whey from the solid curds that eventually become cheese. While traditionally sourced from animals, modern versions can also come from plants or microbes.

How it's made

Traditional animal rennet is extracted from the inner lining of the fourth stomach of young calves, kids, or lambs, where the enzyme naturally exists to help them digest milk. The stomach lining is washed, dried, and sliced, then soaked in an acid solution to activate the enzymes before filtration. Today, manufacturers also produce microbial rennet by fermenting specific moulds or yeasts that naturally yield similar milk-clotting enzymes. There is also vegetable rennet, which is extracted directly from plants like cardoon thistles, nettles, or fig tree sap.

Why manufacturers use it

Manufacturers use rennet because it is the most efficient and reliable way to coagulate milk. Without a clotting agent, milk remains liquid, or only forms weak, acidic curds that cannot be aged into firm cheese. Rennet works quickly and precisely, cutting the milk proteins at a specific point without breaking down other proteins too much, which would otherwise create a bitter flavour. It allows cheesemakers to control the texture, moisture, and firmness of the final cheese, making it a vital tool for producing everything from soft brie to hard cheddar.

Is it harmful

Clara rates this NEUTRAL. Because it is a processing aid rather than an additive that remains active in the final food, it is not classified under standard additive numbering systems. The enzymes are mostly lost in the whey or become inactive as the cheese ages, posing no safety concerns for the general population. For those who follow a vegetarian diet, traditional animal-derived rennet is often avoided, but microbial and vegetable alternatives are widely available and function in the exact same way. No limit is established for its consumption.

What the science says

Food safety authorities have thoroughly evaluated the enzymes in rennet. The Joint FAO/WHO Expert Committee on Food Additives and the European Food Safety Authority have assessed the various sources of chymosin, including those produced by genetically modified microbes, and concluded they present no safety concerns. Because these enzymes are proteins, the human digestive system simply breaks them down into ordinary amino acids during digestion, just like any other dietary protein. No regulatory body has found any reason to restrict its use in traditional cheesemaking.

Where you'll find it

You will find rennet listed on the ingredient labels of most cheeses, including cheddar, parmesan, mozzarella, and gouda. On a label, it is usually declared simply as rennet, microbial rennet, vegetarian rennet, or sometimes just enzymes. Because it does not have an E-number, it is always listed by these plain names. It is rarely found in fresh dairy products like yoghurt or cottage cheese, which are typically curdled using lactic acid cultures rather than these specific coagulating enzymes.
